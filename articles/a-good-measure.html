A Good Measure

The letter as a whole: Titus. The earlier article on sheep churches and goat churches: Our God-Sponsored Projects. And a book on Matthew 25: What We Did Without Knowing.

In an earlier article I wrote about sheep churches and goat churches. A goat church can tell you everything it does. It’s all on the website. A sheep church does the work and doesn’t keep a list. In Matthew 25 the people who fed the hungry and visited the prisoner were surprised to hear they had done it for Jesus.

I still believe that. But I can see how it might be heard. It could sound as though the safest church is the one that does very little, so there’s nothing to boast about. That would be wrong, and Titus says so.

Paul left Titus on Crete to set churches in order. It’s a short letter, forty-six verses, and “good works” comes up six times. Each time it’s held up against someone.

The false teachers are “unfit for any good work” (1:16).
Titus is to be “a model of good works” (2:7).
The people Christ redeemed are “zealous for good works” (2:14), “ready for every good work” (3:1), “careful to devote themselves to good works” (3:8), and are to “learn to devote themselves to good works” (3:14).

It’s the same measure for the people who were leading the church astray, for the man sent to put it right, and for the church itself.

Crete was not short of religious activity. Paul describes people who were “insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers”. They were busy. They were teaching, and “upsetting whole families” while they did it, “teaching for shameful gain what they ought not to teach” (1:10–11). Even one of the Cretans’ own prophets said of them that they were “always liars” (1:12).

Paul sums them up at the end of the chapter: “They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works. They are detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work” (1:16).

The word translated “unfit” means failing the test. Paul uses it of himself in 1 Corinthians 9:27, when he says he disciplines his body “lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified”. It describes someone who has been tested, not someone who has been idle. These people had plenty to show. What they did had been weighed, and it was found to be about gain.

So what did Paul tell Titus to do about it? He didn’t give him a programme. He told him to find people. “Appoint elders in every town” (1:5). Then he described them, and almost everything on the list is about character. An elder must not be “arrogant or quick-tempered or a drunkard or violent or greedy for gain, but hospitable, a lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, and disciplined” (1:7–8). Only one line is about what he teaches (1:9).

“Greedy for gain” is set against “teaching for shameful gain” just a few verses later. The false teachers and the true elders are measured by the same thing. It isn’t how much they do. It’s what they are doing it for.

Before Paul gets to the church, he turns to Titus himself. “Show yourself in all respects to be a model of good works, and in your teaching show integrity, dignity, and sound speech that cannot be condemned, so that an opponent may be put to shame, having nothing evil to say about us” (2:7–8).

The leader is measured first. And Paul doesn’t ask Titus to run good works or organise them. He asks him to be a model of them. It’s the same word Paul uses when he tells Timothy to “set the believers an example” (1 Timothy 4:12). A model is a pattern other people copy.

“In all respects” leaves no part of Titus’s life outside the measure. His teaching is included, and so is the way he teaches. Integrity and dignity aren’t about what he says. They’re about the kind of man who is saying it.

That puts Titus on the opposite side from the teachers in chapter 1. They taught for gain. He is to teach in a way nobody can fault.

Churches copy their leaders, and they copy what the leader does more than what he says. A leader who does good quietly teaches a church to do good quietly. A leader who needs his good works noticed teaches that as well.

How does a people “unfit for any good work” become “zealous for good works”? Paul’s answer comes in the middle of the letter, and it doesn’t start with us.

“For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age” (2:11–12). Grace comes first, and then grace trains. It carries on until it reaches Christ, “who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works” (2:14).

“A people for his own possession” goes back to Sinai, where God called Israel his “treasured possession” (Exodus 19:5). The zeal comes after the belonging. People who know they are God’s own, and treasured, are the ones who become keen to do good.

Paul knew this word from the inside. He said that in his old life he was “so extremely zealous… for the traditions of my fathers” (Galatians 1:14). That zeal sent him after the church. Grace didn’t take his zeal away. It gave him something else to be zealous for.

He says it again in chapter 3, in the same order. “He saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy” (3:5). Only after that does he come back to good works (3:8).

Mercy first. Works after.

Paul wants these works seen. Three times in chapter 2 he gives a reason, and each reason is about the people watching. Young wives are to live well “that the word of God may not be reviled” (2:5). Titus is to teach so “that an opponent may be put to shame, having nothing evil to say about us” (2:8). Bondservants are to be faithful “so that in everything they may adorn the doctrine of God our Saviour” (2:10).

The people given the job of adorning the doctrine were slaves. They had no platform and no name. Their good works were done in someone else’s house, for someone else’s benefit, and the only thing put on display was the gospel.

Jesus held both sides of this in one sermon. “Let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven” (Matthew 5:16). A few verses later he says, “Beware of practising your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them”, and “do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing” (Matthew 6:1, 3). The works are the same. What changes is who gets the glory.

The works are the same. What changes is who gets the glory.

That is the line between giving account and promoting. When Paul and Barnabas came home from their first journey, they “gathered the church together” and “declared all that God had done with them” (Acts 14:27). They reported to the people who had sent them, and God is the one acting in every line. Promotion turns that round. We run, we provide, we reach, and it’s said to people outside.

The church has often been best known for good works it never advertised. In the fourth century the emperor Julian, who wanted the old gods back, complained in a letter that the Christians fed not only their own poor but the pagans’ poor as well.1 The Christians didn’t write that. Their opponent did.

The drift doesn’t usually begin with a bad decision. It begins with a good work.

A local church sees a need and meets it. Other churches hear about it and want to help. It grows and becomes a denominational venture, then an organisation in its own right. To keep going it needs funding, and the funding comes from government. To keep the funding it needs reports and outcomes and promotion, and the promotion has to look professional. In time the staff are chosen for their skills, and whether they know Christ stops being part of the question.

Nobody along the way decided to stop being a church. Each step made sense. The work may still be good. People are still fed and housed and helped, and that is real.

But the measure has changed. Titus measured people: their character, and what they were doing their work for. The new measure is output: how many, how much, how well it looks.

I think the first sign is often small. A church starts a website or a Facebook page, and the reason it wants one is so people will notice what it does. Wanting to be noticed is where it starts. From there it’s a short step to the end justifying the means, and to choosing the right person for the job instead of the right kind of person.

The last three times Paul uses the phrase, he is talking to the whole church.

“Remind them… to be ready for every good work” (3:1). Ready doesn’t mean always busy. A fire crew isn’t measured by how often it’s seen driving round town. It’s measured by whether it turns out when the bell rings. A ready church doesn’t have to invent good works to put on a website. It answers the need in front of it, often without stopping to notice it has done anything at all.

Then: “those who have believed in God may be careful to devote themselves to good works. These things are excellent and profitable for people” (3:8). The word translated “devote themselves” is the one Paul uses elsewhere of a man who manages his own household well (1 Timothy 3:4). It means putting something first and taking charge of it. Good works aren’t one department of the church. The whole church is responsible for them.

Paul’s last word on good works comes in the closing lines of the letter. “And let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful” (3:14).

“Learn” means it doesn’t come naturally, even to people saved by grace. It’s practised, like any skill. “Urgent need” says what the works are for: the person in front of you who can’t wait. And “not unfruitful” names the other danger. A church can drift into promotion, but it can also drift into doing nothing, so wary of boasting that it does no good at all.

The sheep in Matthew 25 were ready for every good work. When the hungry came, they fed them. When someone was in prison, they went. They did good works and kept no list, and they were surprised to learn who they had done them for.
