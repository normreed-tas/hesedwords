Ephesians 4:7–16
24 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Body

One of a series on Ephesians. It carries on from One. More of Ruth is in Sitting with a Sinner.

The church I pastor sits on a hill above a prison.

When I first visited it in 2008, it was known for what had gone wrong. People could tell you about the ministers who had fallen morally. They couldn’t have told you much about what the church did, because it did very little beyond its own doors. It looked inward, and it was dying.

The church owns eight houses on the property. One was set aside for the families of prisoners, people who had come a long way to visit someone inside. Of the other seven, only one had someone living in it who came to church.

I moved from Adelaide in 2010.

Everything a church might need was on that hill. There was a building, there were houses, and below it was a prison full of men and women who needed someone to visit them. What it didn’t have was life.

A church can have every part in place and still not be alive. A body laid out on a table has every part in place.

When Paul starts listing what the church holds in common, the first thing he names is a body.

There is one body and one Spirit, even as you also were called in one hope of your calling. (Ephesians 4:4)1

The list goes on: one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father. But the first two items are joined. Body and Spirit come as a pair.

That’s not a new pairing. In Genesis, God forms the man from the dust of the ground, and he lies there complete and still. Then God breathes into him, and “man became a living soul” (Genesis 2:7). James puts it from the other side: “the body apart from the spirit is dead” (James 2:26). A body without breath is a corpse, however well it’s made.

So when Paul calls the church a body, he isn’t reaching for a picture of good organisation. Plenty of things are well organised: a company, a club, a committee. You can draw a chart for any of them, with a box for each person and lines showing who answers to whom. You can improve the chart, reorganise the boxes, add a new line. None of that makes anything alive.

A body is different. It isn’t arranged from outside. It grows from inside, it feeds itself from what it’s given, it heals when it’s hurt, and every part depends on every other part. Paul has already said whose body it is. Christ is the head, and the church is “his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all” (Ephesians 1:23).

The church on the hill had a chart. It had a building, a roster and a property full of houses. It didn’t have breath.

Then Paul turns from what we hold together to what each of us is given.

But to each one of us, the grace was given according to the measure of the gift of Christ. (Ephesians 4:7)

Nobody is left out of “each one of us”, and nobody sets their own measure. The measure is “the gift of Christ”. What each person receives depends on what he chooses to give, not on how gifted they think they are or how much they think they deserve.

Paul backs this up from a psalm.

Therefore he says, “When he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, and gave gifts to people.” (Ephesians 4:8)

Psalm 68 is a victory song. God goes up to his holy hill with a long line of captives behind him, the way a king came home from war. In the psalm, the king receives gifts. That was the usual way of it: the conqueror collected the spoils. When Paul quotes it, the gifts go the other way. The one who won gives them away.

Paul then explains who has ascended. Before going up, he “first descended into the lower parts of the earth” (4:9). The one who came all the way down is the one who has gone “far above all the heavens, that he might fill all things” (4:10). Christ went down as far as it’s possible to go, and he came back up as the victor. What he won, he hands out.

That’s where a church’s life starts. It doesn’t start with what a congregation has gathered, raised or built. It starts with what Christ has given, and he has given something to each one.

A church can have every part in place and still not be alive.

Then Paul says what the gifts are. They aren’t abilities. They’re people.

He gave some to be apostles; and some, prophets; and some, evangelists; and some, shepherds and teachers; for the perfecting of the saints, to the work of serving, to the building up of the body of Christ. (Ephesians 4:11–12)

The risen Christ gives the church people, and he tells us what they’re for. They don’t do the serving on behalf of everyone else. They perfect the saints for the work of serving.

The word translated “perfecting” has a working life outside the church. The same family of words is used of fishermen mending their nets (Mark 1:19). It means getting something ready to do its job. A net that’s been mended isn’t admired on the shore; it goes back into the water. The shepherds and teachers are there to mend and ready the saints, and the saints do the serving.

That’s what happened on the hill, slowly. One by one, the houses filled with people who came to church. They were given room to do what they were there for, and they were supported in it. We didn’t pay them to minister. They paid rent to live there, and they ministered. They became the means by which the church did its work.

My sister Ruth and her husband were travelling around Australia in a caravan. They called in to visit, and they never left. Before long they had moved out of the caravan and into one of the houses. Ruth worked with me in the prison, especially on the programmes for prisoners’ children. About eight years ago a group of women began meeting at the church to make birthday cards for people in the congregation. It became a place of community. Then people started staying on after the cards, having lunch in the church café and reading the Bible together from our regular devotional readings. That study is still running.

Nobody designed that, and nobody was paid for it. A woman passing through in a caravan stayed, was given room, and became a working part.

Paul says where all this is heading.

…until we all attain to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to a full grown man, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ, that we may no longer be children, tossed back and forth and carried about with every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men, in craftiness, after the wiles of error. (Ephesians 4:13–14)

The goal is a grown body. Paul uses the word “measure” again. In 4:7 it was the measure of the gift each one receives. Here it’s the measure of the stature of Christ himself, and that’s the height the body grows towards.

The opposite is a child in a small boat. Every wind moves it, and anyone clever enough can steer it. A body that never grows up is easy to push around, and easy for others to lead off course.

A growing body has parts at different stages. That’s what a body looks like while it’s growing, and it isn’t a fault.

We didn’t pay them to minister. They paid rent to live there, and they ministered.

Once the church on the hill had a reason to exist, people began to come because of it. Some were mature Christians, steady in the faith, who wanted to reach into the prison and had found a church that would let them. They were released to do it and supported while they did. Others came with needs of their own, some of them from hard places. They found the steady ones already there, people who could sit with them, answer their questions and stay put when things went wrong.

So on any Sunday the room held both: those ministering and those being ministered to. Often it was hard to tell which was which, and over time some moved from one to the other. That’s what growing up looks like in a body. Not everyone arrives mature. There are enough grown parts that the young ones aren’t tossed about while they grow.

Paul finishes the paragraph with the body at work.

…but speaking truth in love, we may grow up in all things into him who is the head, Christ, from whom all the body, being fitted and knit together through that which every joint supplies, according to the working in measure of each individual part, makes the body increase to the building up of itself in love. (Ephesians 4:15–16)

The growth starts with the head: “from whom all the body…” The life comes from Christ. But it doesn’t reach the body by skipping the parts. It travels through them, “through that which every joint supplies”. A joint is the place where one part meets another, and something passes across it. Each part receives, and each part passes on.

“Measure” appears a third time. There was the measure of the gift in 4:7 and the measure of Christ’s stature in 4:13. Here it’s the measure of “each individual part” doing its work. No part is asked to do the whole job, and no part is excused from its share. When each part works in its measure, the body builds itself up in love. Nobody brings in a builder. The body grows itself.

Over the years, that’s what happened on the hill. Criticism became care. Languishing became loving. The fractured became whole.

The church had been known for what had gone wrong. It came to be known for the good it did in the community, and for reaching into the prison. Prisoners’ families stayed in one of the houses while they visited. Children came up from visiting their parents inside. Women made birthday cards, then stayed for lunch and the Bible.

Jesus said, “A city located on a hill can’t be hidden” (Matthew 5:14). For years the church on that hill had been easy to see for the wrong reasons.

It became a light on the hill to the prison it overlooks.
