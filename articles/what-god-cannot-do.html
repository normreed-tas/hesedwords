What God Cannot Do

Near the end of his life, chained in Rome and largely alone, Paul wrote one more letter to Timothy. In the middle of it he quoted something the churches already knew. He introduces it with the words “The saying is trustworthy”. It may have been sung or recited. It was certainly remembered. It runs to four lines:

If we have died with him, we will also live with him;
if we endure, we will also reign with him;
if we deny him, he also will deny us;
if we are faithless, he remains faithful—
for he cannot deny himself.

2 Timothy 2:11–13

Read it once quickly, and it sounds like a tidy piece of instruction. Read it again slowly, and something in it doesn’t fit.

Look at how the first three lines are made. Each one has two halves, and the second half answers the first in kind.

We died with him, so we will live with him. We endure, so we will reign with him. We deny him, so he will deny us.

What we do, he answers. Shared death leads to shared life, and shared hardship to a shared throne. Even the third line follows the rule, though it turns the other way. If we turn our back on him, he will turn his back on us.

It’s the kind of pattern a child could learn and finish. Say the first half of the next line, and anyone listening knows how it should end. So when we reach “if we are faithless”, the ear is already waiting for the answer: he will be faithless to us.

That isn’t what the line says.

Before we follow that broken line, we need to stay with the one before it. If we deny him, he also will deny us.

It’s tempting to hurry past this line. It’s uncomfortable, and the next line is so much kinder. But it isn’t Paul’s invention, and it isn’t a harsh note slipped in by an early church with a taste for warnings. It’s Jesus. “Whoever denies me before men, I also will deny before my Father who is in heaven” (Matthew 10:33). The saying keeps the Lord’s own words, and we have no right to blunt them.

So what does it mean to deny him? The word is arneomai. It means to disown, to say “I do not know him” and mean it. It isn’t a doubt or a stumble. It’s a verdict, a settled choice about who we belong to.

For Timothy and the churches who first said these lines, denial wasn’t an idea. It was a choice put to them by people who could kill them. After the great fire of Rome in AD 64, Nero blamed the Christians. The historian Tacitus records that some were dressed in animal skins and torn apart by dogs. Others were crucified, and some were burned as torches to light the emperor’s gardens.1 Paul wrote this letter from a Roman prison in those years, expecting to die, and he did.

Fifty years later the procedure was routine. Pliny, a Roman governor, wrote to the emperor Trajan explaining how he tested people accused of being Christians. He had them call on the gods, offer incense to the emperor’s image, and curse Christ. Those who did were released.2 To deny him was a sentence you could say aloud to save your life.

We read these lines from the lounge room. The worst most of us face is being called a God-botherer or a do-gooder. That isn’t nothing. But we should be honest that when the saying talks about denying him, it means something its first hearers could actually do, at a price we have never been asked to pay.

God takes that choice seriously. He made us able to love him, which means he made us able to refuse him, and he won’t overrule the refusal. C. S. Lewis wrote that the doors of hell are “locked on the inside”.3 When God finally denies the one who has denied him, he isn’t taking revenge. He’s telling the truth about where that person has chosen to stand.

The third line stands. It has to, or the fourth line means nothing.

Now the fourth line. If we are faithless, he remains faithful.

The pattern breaks exactly here. We have spent three lines learning that what we do, he answers in kind, and now he doesn’t. Our half of the line goes one way and his half goes the other. The saying will not finish the sentence the way the pattern demands.

Notice that the word has changed. It isn’t “if we deny him” again. It’s “if we are faithless”, apistoumen. The word means to fail in trust, to lose our grip, to be unable to hold on. Denial is a door shut from the inside. Faithlessness is a hand that has gone weak. One says, “I don’t belong to him.” The other says, “I can’t hold on to him.”

To the weak hand, the answer is: he remains faithful.

The Greek plays on its own words. Paul introduces the saying as pistos ho logos, “the saying is faithful”. Then ei apistoumen, “if we are un-faithful”. Then ekeinos pistos menei, “that one remains faithful”. The faithfulness sits on either side of our failure and holds it like two hands.

And the verb is menei: he remains, he stays, he abides. It’s the word John loves, the word of the vine and the branches. We fall away from him, and he doesn’t move.

If you want to see the difference between the third line and the fourth in one life, look at Peter.

On the night Jesus was arrested, Peter followed at a distance into the high priest’s courtyard. He sat by a charcoal fire to keep warm. A servant girl looked at him and said, “This man also was with him.” Peter said, “Woman, I do not know him” (Luke 22:57). He said it twice more to two more people, the third time with curses. The word the Gospels use is the same one as our saying: he denied him.

Hours earlier Jesus had told him this would happen. But he said something else as well, and it’s easy to miss. “Simon, Simon, behold, Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, but I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:31–32).

Denial is a door shut from the inside. Faithlessness is a hand that has gone weak.

Look at what Jesus prayed for. He didn’t pray that Peter would never deny him. He knew Peter would. He prayed that Peter’s faith would not fail, that underneath the collapse something would still be holding. And he spoke of afterwards, “when you have turned again”, as if the way back already existed.

When the rooster crowed, Luke says, “the Lord turned and looked at Peter” (Luke 22:61). Peter went out and wept bitterly. That isn’t the weeping of a man who has settled who he belongs to. It’s the weeping of a man who has found out how weak his grip is.

Then comes the beach. After the resurrection, Jesus is cooking breakfast on the shore of Galilee, over a charcoal fire. John uses the same word for it as for the fire in the courtyard, and only those two times in the whole New Testament. Three times Jesus asks, “Simon, son of John, do you love me?” Three times Peter answers, and three times Jesus gives him work to do: “Feed my sheep.” Three denials by one fire are met by three restorations by another. (The shepherd’s side of that morning is told in A Shepherd Is What a Shepherd Does.)

Jesus then tells Peter how he will die: “you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you and carry you where you do not want to go” (John 21:18). John adds that this showed “by what kind of death he was to glorify God.” Early tradition says Peter was crucified in Rome under Nero, in the same persecution that took Paul.4 The man who could not say “I know him” to a servant girl by a fire said it in the end to Rome itself.

Peter was faithless that night, and Jesus remained faithful. That is the fourth line, lived.

The saying doesn’t end with line four. It adds a reason: for he cannot deny himself.

That’s a strange thing to say about God, who can do anything. But this “cannot” isn’t a weakness. It’s the one thing God will never do: stop being who he is. And who he is was told to Moses on the mountain: “The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness” (Exodus 34:6). In Hebrew the last two words are hesed and emet.

Hesed is the love that keeps covenant. It holds to a promise when the other party has failed to hold to theirs. Emet is truth and firmness, the quality of something you can put your full weight on. It comes from the same root as amen and as ’emunah, faithfulness. Moses said it again to Israel: “Know therefore that the LORD your God is God, the faithful God who keeps covenant and steadfast love with those who love him … to a thousand generations” (Deuteronomy 7:9). The Greek Old Testament calls him there theos pistos, the faithful God, the same word as our saying.

So when Paul writes “he remains faithful”, he isn’t making a new claim about God. He’s repeating the oldest one.

And notice that the “cannot” holds lines three and four together. Because he is emet, he won’t pretend that someone who has walked away is still with him. That would be a lie, and he can’t lie about himself. Because he is hesed, he won’t let go of someone whose grip has failed. That would be breaking covenant, and he can’t break his own word. Both lines rest on the same ground: God being true to himself.

That’s why the fourth line isn’t wishful thinking. Optimism is a forecast about how things will turn out. This is a character reference. It doesn’t tell us what will happen to us. It tells us who he is, whatever happens.

Paul didn’t learn this saying from a book. Near the end of the same letter he describes his own trial.

At my first defence no one came to stand by me, but all deserted me. May it not be charged against them! But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me. (2 Timothy 4:16–17)

Everyone deserted him. Demas had already gone, “in love with this present world” (4:10). All in Asia had turned away (1:15). When Paul stood before a Roman court, the bench where his friends should have sat was empty. That was the faithlessness of others, the weak grip, the people who couldn’t bear to be seen with him.

Look at what Paul does with it. May it not be charged against them. He doesn’t deny them. Their grip failed, and he holds on to them anyway. The man who taught the saying is living its fourth line toward the people who let him down.

And then: But the Lord stood by me. When every human loyalty had failed, one stayed. The Lord remained.

Optimism is a forecast about how things will turn out. This is a character reference.

This isn’t the confidence of a man whose life has gone well. Paul doesn’t expect to walk free. A few verses earlier he writes, “I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come” (4:6). When he says, “The Lord will rescue me from every evil deed and bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom” (4:18), he means rescue through death, not from it. Nothing in his circumstances has turned out well. His certainty doesn’t rest on circumstances. It rests on who was standing beside him in the courtroom.

He had said it at the start of the letter: “I know whom I have believed, and I am convinced that he is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me” (1:12). He doesn’t say what he believed but whom.

There are things God cannot do. He cannot pretend that a door locked from the inside is open. And he cannot let go of a hand that has gone weak. Our grip will fail us. His won’t. He remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself.
