Ephesians 2:11–22
17 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Peace

Ephesians 2:1–10, the passage before this: Rich. Ephesians 1: Church.

The people who first read this letter lived in a world run by soldiers.

Ephesus was a great city in the Roman province of Asia. Rome had brought it peace, and Rome was proud of that peace. It had a name, the Pax Romana, and in Rome the Senate built an Altar of Peace to honour the emperor Augustus. But everyone knew how that peace had been made. Rome had fought until its enemies could fight no more. Peace was what you had when the other side had lost.

That kind of peace had to be guarded. There were soldiers on the roads, soldiers in the ports and soldiers at the city gates. War was never far away, and if it wasn’t close, the threat of it was.

Paul knew soldiers well. When he wrote this letter he was a prisoner, and a soldier was guarding him (Acts 28:16). He calls himself “an ambassador in chains” (Ephesians 6:20). He could look up from his writing and see a Roman uniform.

So when Paul writes about peace, his readers already know one kind of peace. It is peace won by a sword and kept by a wall. He is about to describe another kind.

There was a wall in the temple at Jerusalem.

The temple stood in a series of courts. Anyone could come into the outer court, Gentiles included. But a low stone barrier marked how far a Gentile could go, and along it were notices in Greek and Latin. Two of those notices have been found. They warn that no foreigner may go beyond the barrier, and that anyone caught doing so will have only himself to blame for his death.

It was a wall with a sign on it, and the sign told you which side you belonged on.

Paul was arrested because of that wall. Some Jews from Asia saw him in Jerusalem with a man named Trophimus, “the Ephesian” (Acts 21:29). They supposed Paul had taken him into the temple, past the barrier. The crowd seized Paul and dragged him out, and they were trying to kill him when Roman soldiers arrived (Acts 21:30–32). Those chains led all the way to Rome, and to the prison where he wrote this letter.

So when the Ephesians read that Christ “broke down the middle wall of separation” (Ephesians 2:14),1 they didn’t have to imagine the wall. One of their own had been accused of crossing it, and Paul was still in chains because of it.

“Therefore remember that once you, the Gentiles in the flesh, who are called ‘uncircumcision’ by that which is called ‘circumcision’…” (Ephesians 2:11).

Paul starts with a name. The Gentiles were called something. The other side had a word for them, and it was not a kind one. Before Paul says anything about what they lacked, he reminds them they had been labelled.

Then he lists what they lacked. They were “separate from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of the promise, having no hope and without God in the world” (2:12).

It is a picture of standing outside. They were outside the people and outside the promises. They had no hope, because hope belonged to the people inside. And they were without God, so they were outside his house as well.

Then come the words that turn everything: “But now.”

“But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off are made near in the blood of Christ” (2:13).

The Gentiles didn’t get near by learning the rules and keeping them. They didn’t get near by being accepted by the people on the inside. They were brought near, and what brought them near was the blood of Christ. Someone else paid the cost of their coming in.

“For he is our peace, who made both one, and broke down the middle wall of separation” (2:14).

Paul doesn’t say Christ brought peace, or taught peace, or arranged it. He says Christ is our peace. Peace here is a person.

Then Paul says what Christ did with the wall. He abolished “the hostility, the law of commandments contained in ordinances” (2:15). The word for ordinances is dogmata. It means decrees, the rulings of an authority, the kind that were often posted up for the public to read. Paul isn’t saying God’s law was bad. He is saying it had become a wall, a way of marking who was in and who was out, and the wall had to go.

What Christ built in its place is surprising. He didn’t make the Gentiles into Jews, and he didn’t make the Jews into Gentiles. He created “in himself one new man of the two, making peace” (2:15). Neither side became the standard for the other. Both became something new.

And he did it “through the cross, having killed the hostility through it” (2:16).

Rome made peace by killing its enemies. Christ made peace by letting himself be killed, and on the cross it was the hostility that died. The enemies lived, and they were made into one family.

When I came to the church in Hobart, it was small, and it was already divided.

People talked about “us” and “them”. You could see where the lines were, because they were stuck up on the walls. There were signs all through the building, and each one told you what to do. You couldn’t smoke. You couldn’t leave your cup in the sink. You couldn’t talk here. You couldn’t sit in this seat. This door had to stay closed, and that door had to be locked.

None of these were bad things. But if you did one of them, you became one of “them”.

The signs made it easy not to talk to each other. If someone left a cup in the sink, nobody had to go and speak to them. You could just point to the sign. Nobody washed the cup, picked up the cigarette butt or closed the door. Instead, people in the congregation became police, enforcing the rules.

He didn’t put up a notice. He came in person, and he spoke.

When rules are what hold a community together, the community soon splits into parties. I don’t mean the happy kind. I mean factions. Depending on what bothered you most, you gathered with the people who agreed with you. One group complained about the smoke. Another complained about the mess in the kitchen. Another complained about where the chairs were.

Those signs were our dogmata. Each one was a small wall, and each one told you which side you were on.

Over time the atmosphere in the church changed.

It didn’t change because people stopped making mistakes. Cups were still left in the sink. Now and then a door was still left unlocked. What changed was how we saw each other.

There was forgiveness. People stopped carrying grudges. People started washing the cups.

I began taking the signs down. I did it on purpose, but slowly, one at a time. And I never replaced them.

The person who left the cup in the sink was no longer public enemy number one. They were someone just like us, fallible but loved.

That is what Paul means when he says Christ killed the hostility. The people who left their cups were still there. The hostility was what died. Our peace didn’t come from a better set of rules. It came because we started treating each other as people Christ had already made one.

“He came and preached peace to you who were far off and to those who were near” (2:17).

The signs in our church took the place of talking. Christ did the opposite. He didn’t put up a notice. He came in person, and he spoke.

He spoke to both sides. The ones who were far off needed peace, and so did the ones who were near. The people inside the wall weren’t fine just because they were inside it. They needed peace as much as anyone.

“For through him we both have our access in one Spirit to the Father” (2:18). The door is open to both, and it is the same door.

Then Paul tells the Gentiles who they are now. “So then you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and of the household of God” (2:19). They don’t have to earn a place in the family or wait for the people inside to accept them. They already belong.

Paul finishes with a building. The household is built on the apostles and prophets, “Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone,” and the whole building “grows into a holy temple in the Lord” (2:20–21).

Paul had been arrested over a wall in the temple. Now he says that God is building a new temple, and it is made of people. The people that wall kept out are part of it.

Paul comes back to soldiers at the end of the letter. He tells the church to put on the armour of God. He says our struggle “is not against flesh and blood” (6:12), and he names each piece. One of them is shoes: “having fitted your feet with the preparation of the Good News of peace” (6:15).

The soldier guarding Paul wore shoes made for marching to war. The Christian’s shoes are made for carrying peace.

That leaves a question for us. What signs are up in our church? Some may be on the walls. Others are in us: the unwritten rules we use to decide who is “us” and who is “them”, and the things we point to so we don’t have to speak to someone.

The way forward is the way it went in Hobart. We take them down one at a time, and we don’t put new ones up. We wash the cup. We speak to the person. We forgive.

Paul ends the letter the way he began it:

“Peace be to the brothers, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ” (Ephesians 6:23).
