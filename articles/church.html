Ephesians 1
15 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Church

The prison next door also closes What We Did Without Knowing, on Matthew 25.

There is a side door in the church at Risdon Vale. I used to say I could open it and spit over the fence into the prison. I suspect it is one of the closest churches to a prison anywhere.

Before I arrived as pastor fifteen years ago, the church had no involvement in the prison. Over the years since, the two have shaped each other. The Onesimus Foundation started here. I have retired from the church, and I still work in the prison.

But this is about a Sunday morning.

When we gather — park, find a seat, sing, hear the reading, stay for a cup of tea afterwards — what do we think we are doing? Most of us would answer from our side of it. We come to worship. We come to be fed. We come because these are our people. All of that is true. But each answer starts with us and ends with us, and the prison stays on the other side of the fence.

Paul wrote to a church, a letter meant to be read aloud to people gathered much as we gather. He does not begin with us.

After a short greeting, the letter opens like this:

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ” (Ephesians 1:3).

The word comes three times in one sentence. God has blessed us, with every blessing, and so we bless him. Anyone raised in the synagogue would have known the shape. It is how the psalms bless: “Blessed be Yahweh, the God of Israel” (Psalm 41:13). You did not begin prayer by asking. You began by saying who God is and what he has done.

What he has done takes Paul twelve verses, and in Greek they are one sentence. He does not stop for breath. The Father chose us “before the foundation of the world,” and adopted us as his children (1:4–5). The Son brought us back through his blood, our trespasses forgiven, “according to the riches of his grace” (1:7). The Spirit sealed us, the first instalment of everything still to come (1:13–14).

Three times Paul comes to rest on the same words: to the praise of his glory (1:6, 12, 14). The Greek word for glory, doxa, is the one the Greek Old Testament used for the Hebrew kavod — the weight of God, the glory that filled the tabernacle so that Moses could not go in. Each part of the blessing ends there, on his weight and his worth.

So the first thing Paul says to a gathered church is not what they should do. It is what has been done for them. They are blessed before they have lifted a finger.

We do not come on a Sunday morning to get God’s attention. We come because we already have it, and to give back the blessing that came first.

Then Paul prays for them. He has heard of their faith and their love for all the saints, and he cannot stop thanking God for them (1:15–16). Then he asks.

He does not ask for more blessing. He has just told them they have every blessing there is. He asks that they would see it:

“having the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know what is the hope of his calling, and what are the riches of the glory of his inheritance in the saints, and what is the exceeding greatness of his power toward us who believe” (1:18–19).

In Hebrew thinking the heart is not mainly where you feel. It is where you understand and decide. Paul is praying that they would see with the part of themselves that makes decisions.

What he wants them to see comes in three parts, and every part belongs to God. His calling. His inheritance. His power.

The middle one is easy to read past. It is his inheritance in the saints — not only what we inherit from God, but what God inherits in us. Moses sang it long before: “For Yahweh’s portion is his people. Jacob is the lot of his inheritance” (Deuteronomy 32:9). A small congregation on a Sunday morning, a few dozen chairs and an urn in the corner, is what God counts as his own.

A church can have every blessing in this chapter and see none of it. Paul’s prayer for such a church is not a rebuke. It is a request for eyes.

Paul does not leave the power abstract. He says where it has already been used.

It is the power God “worked in Christ when he raised him from the dead and made him to sit at his right hand in the heavenly places” (1:20).

The heavenly places. Paul used the phrase in his first sentence: God has blessed us “in the heavenly places in Christ” (1:3). Now he shows us what is there — a throne, and Christ seated on it. The blessing came from the room where he sits.

Paul is drawing on two psalms. “Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool for your feet” (Psalm 110:1). “You have put all things under his feet” (Psalm 8:6). Christ is seated “far above all rule, authority, power, dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this age, but also in that which is to come” (1:21).

Eugene Peterson’s paraphrase puts it as everything “from galaxies to governments.”1 A careful translation is like a map: you need it to know exactly where you stand. Peterson is closer to a painting of the same country. He loosens the wording in places, but he shows why anyone would want to go there, and he keeps Paul’s breathlessness, which the tidier translations break into separate sentences.

Every rule and authority. From the side door at Risdon Vale, that includes the one over the fence. A prison is about the plainest form of human authority there is: locks, counts, sentences, officers. Paul says it sits under Christ’s feet.

The power Paul prays the church would see is the power that raised a dead man and seated him above all of it. And it is “toward us who believe” (1:19). It is not kept in heaven. It is aimed at a congregation.

Then Paul says where the church fits. God “put all things in subjection under his feet, and gave him to be head over all things for the assembly, which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all” (1:22–23).

Where we might expect church, the World English Bible says assembly. The Greek is ekklēsia. In a Greek city it was the word for the citizens gathered to do the city’s business. The Greek Old Testament used it for the assembly of Israel gathered before God. Neither meaning is a building. Both are people gathered, with something to do.

Look at who Christ is given to. He is head over all things, and he is given to the assembly. The one who rules everything is given to a gathered people as their head.

Earlier Paul named God’s plan: “an administration of the fullness of the times, to sum up all things in Christ, the things in the heavens and the things on the earth” (1:10). The word for administration is oikonomia, the running of a household. God is running the household of everything towards one end — all things gathered up in Christ. The church is where that gathering has already begun. It is “the fullness of him who fills all in all.”

A congregation, then, is not the audience for what God is doing in the world. It is the body through which the head acts.

That gives a church authority, and it is easy to say that badly. A body has no authority of its own. A hand does what the head intends, or something is wrong with it. The authority is Christ’s. We are the means.

When a church forgets this, it tends to go one of two ways. It grasps at an authority that was never its own, or it shrinks into a group that meets on Sundays and keeps to itself.

So what changes on a Sunday morning, if a congregation sees itself the way Paul prays it would?

We stop coming only as people to be served, and come as an assembly with business to do.

The prayers change. When a church prays for the prison over the fence — for the men and women inside, for the officers, for the families who visit — it is not sending wishes into the air. It is the body speaking to the head about something already under his feet.

The way we see the streets around us changes. They are not the background to what the church does. They are part of the “all things” Christ is filling, and the church is where the filling has begun.

None of this begins with effort. Paul’s order holds all the way through: first what God has done, then eyes to see it, then a life that follows. In chapter 1 he prayed that they would know “the hope of his calling.” In chapter 4 he turns the whole letter on the same word: “I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you to walk worthily of the calling with which you were called” (4:1).

Prisoner in the Lord. Paul wrote this letter in chains (3:1; 6:20). The man who told a church it sits with Christ above every rule and authority was himself being held by one.

When I open the side door at Risdon Vale, the fence is still there, and so is the prison. Ephesians takes neither away. It tells the church who is seated above both, and then it tells the church to walk.
