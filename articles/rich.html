Ephesians 2:1–9; 3:14–19
16 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Rich

Ephesians 1, the chapter before this: Church. Paul’s prayer for the dimensions of that love: Measured, and Immeasurable.

Paul tells the church what they were before he tells them who God is.

“…you were dead in transgressions and sins, in which you once walked according to the course of this world” (Ephesians 2:1–2). They had lived the way everyone lives, “doing the desires of the flesh and of the mind,” and were “by nature children of wrath, even as the rest” (2:3). Paul writes “we” in the middle of it. He puts himself in the same column.

If this were a ledger, that is our side of it. There is nothing in it to set against the debt. A dead man has no assets.

Then two words turn the page: “But God.”

“But God, being rich in mercy, for his great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ” (2:4–5).

Paul names three riches in as many verses. Mercy — eleos, the word the Greek Old Testament most often used for hesed. Grace — charis. And kindness — chrēstotēs: “that in the ages to come he might show the exceeding riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus” (2:7).

Then he closes the account. “For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast” (2:8–9).

Nothing was paid in from our side. Everything came from his.

Every country is known for what it is rich in. Australia is rich in iron ore, oil and gas, and it trades on them. They are what it sends out into the world, and they are what the world knows it for.

Chapter 1 of Ephesians said who governs: Christ, seated above every rule and authority. Chapter 2 says how. God is rich in mercy, in grace, in kindness. That is how his government runs, and it is what he trades on.

The psalms knew it. “Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne. Loving kindness and truth go before your face” (Psalm 89:14). Justice is what the throne stands on. Hesed goes out ahead of it; it is what you meet first.

So the church’s stock in trade is not its own. It is what God is rich in.

Paul shows the currency moving. In 2:7 the kindness is God’s: “the exceeding riches of his grace in kindness toward us.” Two chapters later it has passed into the church’s hands: “Be kind to one another, tender hearted, forgiving each other, just as God also in Christ forgave you” (4:32). The words are related — chrēstotēs in 2:7, chrēstoi in 4:32. What God spends on us, the church spends on each other. That is what currency does. It circulates.

God wants to be known for his grace in kindness.

Jesus told two stories about stewards — men put in charge of a master’s wealth. In both, everything turns on what the steward believes about his master.

In Matthew 25 a servant is given one talent, and he buries it. When his master returns he explains himself: “Lord, I knew you that you are a hard man… I was afraid, and went away and hid your talent in the earth” (25:24–25). He thought his master was hard, so he was careful with his master’s money. Nothing was lost. Nothing was done with it either, and that is the whole charge against him.

Luke 16 begins, “There was a certain rich man who had a manager” (16:1). The manager is accused of wasting the rich man’s goods and told to “give an accounting of your management” (16:2). He is about to lose his job. So he calls in his master’s debtors one by one and cuts their bills. A hundred measures of oil become fifty. A hundred of wheat become eighty. And “his lord commended the dishonest manager because he had done wisely” (16:8).

Kenneth Bailey reads the story this way.1 The master was known as a generous man. When the debtors went home with their bills reduced, they went home praising him. To take the cancellations back now would make him look mean. The manager had staked everything on his master’s generosity, and it held.

One steward read his master as hard and buried the riches. The other read his master as generous and gave them away. What each believed about the master decided what he did with the money.

Paul calls himself a steward too. He writes of “the administration of that grace of God which was given me toward you” (Ephesians 3:2). The word is oikonomia, the same word Luke uses for the manager’s “management.” Paul does not own the grace. He manages it, and his job is to give it away.

In the parable the riches are given away without the master’s permission. In Ephesians, giving them away is the master’s plan.

It would be easy to say that a church goes wrong by trading in the wrong currency — respectability, programmes, being right. That happens. But I think the more common failure is quieter. We do not swap God’s riches for something else. We hold on to them. We are careful with grace, as if it might run out, or as if we might spend it on the wrong person and be made fools of.

Working in the prison has shown me where most people’s grace runs out. It has a limit, and the limit is usually the prison fence. There are some prisoners, whole categories of them, of whom people will say God could never forgive them.

That says a great deal about the measure of our grace. It says nothing about the riches of his mercy.

The servant in Matthew 25 was not a thief. He handed back exactly what he had been given. His failure was that he thought his master was short of something, and so he treated the money as if it were a scarce commodity.

Paul met the opposite fear in his own day. If grace is that rich, won’t people take advantage of it? “Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? May it never be!” (Romans 6:1–2). But his answer was never to ration grace.

Grace does not leave the person who receives it as it found him.

Romans also names the root. “Knowing God, they didn’t glorify him as God” (Romans 1:21), and so they “traded the glory of the incorruptible God” for images (1:23). Glory there is the kavod of the Old Testament — weight. They held the heaviest thing there is and did not value it. A church can do the same without ever bowing to an idol. It can hold the riches of God in its hands and treat them as small.

The failure is not counterfeit currency. It is a church that cannot see what it is holding.

Most of us know God’s mercy the way we know water from a tap. It arrives when we need it, and we are grateful. We do not think much about where it comes from.

That is the gap Paul prays about in chapter 1. He asks that the church would have “the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know… what are the riches of the glory of his inheritance” (1:18). They had received the mercy. They had not seen the depth of the well it was drawn from.

In chapter 3 he gives the riches a name. He was given grace “to preach to the Gentiles the unsearchable riches of Christ” (3:8). The word is anexichniastos — untraceable, a track you cannot follow to its end. Paul uses it again in Romans, when he runs out of words for God: “Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out!” (Romans 11:33).

English has a sailor’s word for it. A fathom is six feet, about the span of a man’s outstretched arms. Sailors measured the depth of the sea by letting down a weighted line and counting the fathoms as it went. Where the line ran out before it touched anything, the water was unfathomable. You cannot find the bottom. The New American Standard Bible uses that word in 3:8: “the unfathomable riches of Christ.”2

This is where the picture of a nation’s resources gives way. Iron ore is dug until the seam is exhausted. Gas fields run dry. A country rich in them has to plan for the day they end. God’s riches have no such day. You cannot follow the seam to where it stops, because it does not stop.

A woman at a well once said to Jesus, “you have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep” (John 4:11). She was right about the well.

The church that hoards grace has measured the well by the cup in its hand.

Why would God be so lavish? Paul gives a reason, and it is not only for our sake.

“That in the ages to come he might show the exceeding riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus” (2:7).

God wants to be known. Not chiefly for his power, though he has it, or for his justice, though his throne stands on it. He wants to be known, for ever, for his grace in kindness. And the place he has chosen to show it is us.

That brings us back to the manager in Luke 16. His master’s reputation was in his hands. Every debtor who went home with a smaller bill went home talking about how generous the master was. The manager did not make his master generous. He made it known.

A church does the same, or fails to. When it spends God’s mercy freely — on the people over the fence, on the ones others have written off, on each other — the people who receive it go home with the master’s name on their lips. When it hoards, they go home with nothing to say about him at all.

The well has no bottom, and the master is rich in mercy. He has put it in our hands to spend.

Paul prays for the church twice in this letter. In chapter 1 he asked that they would see the riches. In chapter 3 he asks that they would take hold of them, and the prayer begins where this whole piece has been:

“For this cause, I bow my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ… that he would grant you, according to the riches of his glory, that you may be strengthened with power through his Spirit in the inner person, that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith, to the end that you, being rooted and grounded in love, may be strengthened to comprehend with all the saints what is the width and length and height and depth, and to know Christ’s love which surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled with all the fullness of God” (Ephesians 3:14–19).
