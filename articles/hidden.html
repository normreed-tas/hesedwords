Ephesians 3:1–13
18 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Hidden

Ephesians 2:11–22, the passage before this: Peace. Ephesians 2:1–10: Rich. Ephesians 1: Church.

When I came to the church in Hobart, one of the things I was asked to do was change its name. It was called Real Life Church at the time. The name didn’t seem to mean much to the community around us, and I wasn’t attached to it. The new name was to be Christian Family Centre.

I found it harder than I expected. When I looked back over the church’s history, almost every pastor who had come had renamed it. Here was another new pastor about to do the same thing. I knew the church would need to be brought along on the journey, and I wasn’t sure how.

Early one morning I was going through a cupboard full of things earlier pastors had left behind. I pulled out a brochure, an A4 sheet folded in half. I opened it at the back and started reading. The writer said that every morning, as he walked around the property at Lindisfarne, he thanked God for the Christian Family Centre.

I stopped. I turned the brochure over. On the front was a logo very like the one our church in Adelaide had used. It was a vision statement for the church, written in the late 1970s. The pages set out what the church was going to be, where it was going and what it planned to do. Its name was the Christian Family Centre.

The brochure had been written by the pastor who founded the church. Not long afterwards his ministry came to an unexpected end, and everything he had planned fell apart. The vision was never carried out. The brochure went into the cupboard and stayed there.

The hairs went up on the back of my neck. I sensed God saying to me, “You’re not naming the church. It has always had a name. This was my intention all along. You are stepping into what I already planned.”

When I went to the church formally about the name, I stood up with the brochure in my hand. I told them what I had found and what had happened to me that morning. Once people could see that this church had carried the name from its beginning, almost every argument fell away. Instead of becoming a point of contention, the new name became a confirmation.

Paul has a word for a plan like that. He uses it three times in Ephesians 3. The word is mystērion, and our English word “mystery” comes from it.

To us a mystery is a puzzle, like a detective story where we try to work out who did it. In Paul’s world the word meant a secret.

Some religions of his day were built around secrets, and they were called the mysteries. People joined through ceremonies only members could see, and they swore never to tell outsiders what they had seen. The secret belonged to the insiders and stayed with them.

The word was also used for a king’s plans. In the book of Judith, a Jewish book written before Christ, King Nebuchadnezzar calls his officers together and tells them “the mystery of his counsel”, his secret plan for war. Paul uses almost the same phrase in Ephesians 1:9, where God has made “known to us the mystery of his will.”1

So when Paul talks about a mystery, he means that God has a plan, made long ago and kept out of sight. The difference is what Paul does with it. He has been told the secret, and his job is to tell it to everyone.

“By revelation the mystery was made known to me… and to make all men see what is the administration of the mystery which for ages has been hidden in God, who created all things through Jesus Christ” (Ephesians 3:3, 9).

Paul did not work the mystery out. He says so plainly: “by revelation the mystery was made known to me” (Ephesians 3:3). He was not a man who sat down with the Scriptures and came up with a clever new idea about the Gentiles. Before he met Christ on the road to Damascus, he was trying to destroy the church. What he knew about God’s plan, he had been shown.

He is also careful about his own size in the story. “To me, the very least of all saints, was this grace given, to preach to the Gentiles the unsearchable riches of Christ” (3:8). The Greek word Paul uses is stranger than the English. He takes the word for “least” and makes it bigger still, something like “leaster”. The man telling the secret is very small, and the secret is very large.

His job was to pass on what he had been shown. He wrote it down so that others could see it too: “when you read, you can perceive my understanding in the mystery of Christ” (3:4). To the Corinthians he described himself and the other apostles as “Christ’s servants and stewards of God’s mysteries,” and added, “it is required of stewards that they be found faithful” (1 Corinthians 4:1–2). Nobody asks a steward to be original, only to be faithful with what he has been given.

That is how it felt in the church that morning. I had not thought of a better name. I had found one that had been there all along, and my part was to stand up and show people what I had found.

Why would God keep his plan hidden at all?

Part of the answer is strategy. Paul says that none of the rulers of this age understood God’s wisdom, “for had they known it, they wouldn’t have crucified the Lord of glory” (1 Corinthians 2:8). The people who put Jesus on the cross thought they were ending him. They were carrying out the very plan they knew nothing about.

C. S. Lewis tells the same story in The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe. The White Witch knows the old law that a traitor’s life belongs to her. When Aslan gives himself up in Edmund’s place, she kills him and believes she has won. She does not know that there is a “Deeper Magic from Before the Dawn of Time”.2 An innocent victim who dies willingly in a traitor’s place breaks death itself. The Stone Table cracks, and Aslan comes back. The Witch knew the law. She did not know the plan behind it.

You’re not naming the church. It has always had a name.

Part of the answer is timing. Paul speaks of God’s plan for “the fullness of the times” (Ephesians 1:10), and in Galatians he writes, “when the fullness of the time came, God sent out his Son” (Galatians 4:4). A seed lies hidden in the ground for a season. Nothing is wrong with it. It is simply not harvest yet.

And part of the answer is that the plan was never really out of sight. Paul says the mystery was “kept secret through long ages” but is now made known “by the Scriptures of the prophets” (Romans 16:25–26). It had been written down all along. God told Abraham, “All the families of the earth will be blessed through you” (Genesis 12:3). After the resurrection, Jesus walked with two disciples to Emmaus and, beginning with Moses, showed them what the Scriptures had said about him all along (Luke 24:27). He didn’t give them new Scriptures. He opened the ones they already had.

Our brochure had been in that cupboard for years. It was not hidden from us. We had simply never looked.

There is a difference between hiding something from someone and hiding it for them. A parent hides a birthday present in the wardrobe. It is out of sight, but it was always meant to be found, and by the person it was hidden from.

Paul says God’s plan was hidden like that. “We speak God’s wisdom in a mystery, the wisdom that has been hidden, which God foreordained before the worlds for our glory” (1 Corinthians 2:7). It was planned before the world began, and it was planned for us. In Colossians he says that in Christ “all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden” (Colossians 2:3). Jesus told of treasure hidden in a field. A man found it, and in his joy he sold everything he had to buy that field (Matthew 13:44).

So what was the present? Paul tells us in one sentence:

“that the Gentiles are fellow heirs and fellow members of the body, and fellow partakers of his promise in Christ Jesus through the Good News” (Ephesians 3:6).

In Greek each of those three phrases begins with the same small word, syn, meaning “together”. Paul says it three times. Heirs together, one body together, sharing the promise together.

The secret was not a piece of hidden knowledge for a spiritual few. It was people. Those who had been far off and those who had been near, now in one family under one roof. That is the wall coming down that Paul described in chapter 2. In chapter 3 he tells us it was the plan all along.

Ephesians keeps taking us behind the curtain. Five times it uses a phrase found nowhere else in Paul: “in the heavenly places”. We are blessed there (1:3). Christ is seated there, far above every ruler and authority (1:20–21). We are seated there with him (2:6). Our battle is there (6:12). And in chapter 3 there is an audience there.

Paul says the plan was hidden in God “to the intent that now through the assembly the manifold wisdom of God might be made known to the principalities and the powers in the heavenly places” (3:10). The word for “manifold” is polypoikilos. It describes something with many colours, like embroidered cloth or a field of wildflowers. Kenneth Wuest, who taught Greek for many years, translated it “the much-variegated wisdom of God”.3 People of every background are woven into one cloth, and the powers are made to look at it.

Peter says something similar. The good news is something “angels desire to look into” (1 Peter 1:12). Wuest called the church a “university” for angels. They are students, leaning over to see what God has done.

But not all the powers in Ephesians are friendly. The same words, “principalities” and “powers”, turn up again in chapter 6, where they are the ones we wrestle against (6:12). Some of the watchers are not students at all. They are the ones who thought the wall between people would stand forever. For them the church is not a classroom. It is the evidence that they lost.

The Bible has shown us a scene like this before. The book of Job opens in heaven. “Now on the day when God’s sons came to present themselves before Yahweh, Satan also came among them” (Job 1:6). God points to a man on earth and asks, “Have you considered my servant, Job?” (1:8). Job knows nothing of it. He loses his children, his wealth and his health without ever being told what was said in that room. As far as we are told, he never finds out. We, the readers, are shown it.

Ephesians does for the church what Job’s first two chapters do for the reader. It lets us see the room. God is still pointing to his people before the powers, and saying, in effect, “Have you considered my church?”

We usually think the important things happen somewhere else, and that our Sunday morning is ordinary and small. Paul turns that around. The church is the stage, and heaven is watching. Paul told the Corinthians the apostles had been “made a spectacle to the world, both to angels and men” (1 Corinthians 4:9). The word is the one we get “theatre” from.

The plan of God is not small just because the church looks small.

When the Syrian army surrounded the city, Elisha’s servant saw only the enemy. Elisha prayed, “Yahweh, please open his eyes, that he may see.” Then the young man saw that the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire around them (2 Kings 6:17). Paul prays the same prayer for the church: “having the eyes of your hearts enlightened” (Ephesians 1:18). Nothing changed on the mountain. What changed was what the servant could see.

At the end of the letter Paul tells the church to “put on the whole armor of God, that you may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil” (Ephesians 6:11). The word for “wiles” is methodeia. We get “method” from it. It means schemes, plotting, tricks worked out in advance.

So there are two plans in Ephesians, but they are not equal. The devil has schemes. God has “the eternal purpose which he accomplished in Christ Jesus our Lord” (3:11). God’s purpose was made before the world began. The devil’s schemes are made up as he goes, against a plan he did not see coming. And the outcome is already settled. Christ is seated “far above all rule, authority, power, dominion” (1:21).

That is why Paul does not tell us to win. He tells us to stand. In four verses he says it again and again: stand, withstand, stand, stand (6:11–14). The battle is real. The victory has already been won.

I think of that brochure in the cupboard. Something went badly wrong in that church’s early years. The man with the vision did not see it through, and for a long time it must have looked as if the plan had died with his ministry. It hadn’t. It lay in the dark, waiting for the morning someone opened the cupboard.

“It is the glory of God to conceal a thing” (Proverbs 25:2). God hid his plan from the powers, kept it through the long ages, and showed it at the right time. He hid it for us, not from us. And Paul ends the first half of his letter with this:

“to him be the glory in the assembly and in Christ Jesus to all generations, forever and ever. Amen” (Ephesians 3:21).

On Sunday morning most of us will see nothing unusual. A few people, a few songs, a coffee afterwards. But the plan of God is not small just because the church looks small. Heaven is watching. This is where God has chosen to show his wisdom.
