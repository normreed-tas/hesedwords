Ephesians 4:1–4
21 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Called

One of a series on Ephesians. It picks up the word Church ended on, and carries on from We.

When I was a boy, people would ask me what I wanted to be when I grew up. The answer shifted a little depending on who was asking and where, but most of the time it came out the same: a teacher or a missionary.

I have done a lot of teaching in the church over many years. But looking back, the thing underneath the teaching is clearer than the teaching itself: a call to ministry, and to missionary service. At times the path was hard to see. Looking back, it is easy to see the intention in the journey.

Paul puts it like this: “For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared before that we would walk in them” (2:10).1 The works were prepared first. The walking came after.

That is how most of us hear the word calling. One person, one life, and a thread through the years that only shows up when you look back.

But when Paul uses the word, he is not writing to one person. He is writing to a church.

I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you to walk worthily of the calling with which you were called. (Ephesians 4:1)

In English, you can mean one person or many. In Greek it can’t be ambiguous, and both of the yous in that sentence are plural. Paul is not asking each reader to go away and find their calling. He is telling a church that it already has one.

He says it twice in one breath, “the calling with which you were called”. Three verses later he says it twice again: “There is one body and one Spirit, even as you also were called in one hope of your calling” (4:4). One body, one hope, one calling.

He has been heading here since the first chapter, where he prayed that they would know “the hope of his calling” (1:18). The calling was God’s before it was theirs. He called a people before any of them answered.

We have made the word private: my calling, my ministry, my gift. Those are real. But they sit inside a calling that belongs to the whole church, and that was there before any of us.

Years ago I heard Craig Groeschel preach a message about something he called “it”. Some churches have it, he said. Most churches want it, but few have it. He went through a list of things it might be. Each time he said, “But that’s not it.” You know when a church has it, and you know when it doesn’t.

He later wrote a book with the same title. In the preface he still won’t name it: “I don’t know. Really, I don’t.”2 What he is sure of is that it comes from God, and from nowhere else.

I think it has a name. I think it is calling.

I don’t mean calling seen from the outside looking in, the kind written into a vision statement and framed in the foyer. I mean a calling on the inside, one the church has recognised and that is trying to get out.

You can’t fake it, in the same way you can’t put on an accent convincingly. You don’t have to announce it either. People hear where you’re from before you’ve finished your first sentence. When a church has its calling, you know it before you walk through the door.

A church’s calling doesn’t get out through the pulpit first. It gets out in how its people treat each other.

I have seen it in churches. Not all churches, and if I am honest, not many. But where I have seen it, there was a sense of purpose that went beyond anything printed. Some of those churches had a vision statement, but the statement wasn’t it. There was a sense of community that went beyond the structure holding it together.

So if every church has been called, why do so few have it?

I think it is because most have not yet discovered their vocation: the reason they exist.

Vocation comes from the Latin word for a calling, and a call needs someone to make it. A vision statement is something a church writes about itself. A vocation is spoken over a church from outside, before anyone thought to ask.

That is why a church can’t manufacture it. It can only find what it has already been given. Paul has spent three chapters telling the Ephesians what that is. They are blessed with every spiritual blessing. They are rich in a mercy they didn’t earn. They have been made one where a wall used to stand. They are part of a plan that was hidden for ages and is now on show. “I therefore” points back to all of it.

A church that hasn’t discovered its vocation still has one. It just hasn’t recognised it yet. And a calling that hasn’t been recognised has nothing in it yet trying to get out.

Discovering the calling is the turning point, but it isn’t the end of it. Paul doesn’t say, “Know your calling.” He says, “Walk worthily of it.” First the calling is recognised. Then it gets out.

Worthily means in a way that matches. Paul uses the same word when he tells the Philippians, “Only let your way of life be worthy of the Good News of Christ” (Philippians 1:27). It means a life that fits what it has been given.

The first thing he says that looks like isn’t a programme or a plan:

with all lowliness and humility, with patience, bearing with one another in love, being eager to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. (Ephesians 4:2–3)

Every one of those happens between people. Humility is how I treat you. Patience is what I give you when you are slow. Bearing with you is what I do when you are hard to be around. Unity is something we keep together or lose together. A church’s calling doesn’t get out through the pulpit first. It gets out in how its people treat each other.

That is why you can sense it at the door. You haven’t heard the sermon yet. What you see is how people greet one another in the foyer, who makes room for whom, and who gets brought a cup of tea.

Paul names the other kind of walk too: “you no longer walk as the rest of the Gentiles also walk, in the futility of their mind” (4:17). The Greek word for futility is the one the Greek Old Testament uses for hevel in Ecclesiastes: “Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.” Breath. Vapour. A walk with nothing behind it.

That is the difference between wandering and walking. A wanderer can cover as much ground as anyone. He just has no reason to be on the road.

When a church has its calling, you know it before you walk through the door.

When I was a boy and someone asked what I wanted to be, the question was about me. Paul asks a church a different question. He doesn’t ask what you want to be. He asks what you were called to be, and whether the way you walk matches it.

Every church has an answer, whether it has found it yet or not. The calling was given before any of us arrived. “The hope of his calling” is the same hope for the oldest member and the newest visitor: one body, one Spirit, one hope.

So here is a question for those of us who sit in the same chairs most Sundays. If a stranger walked through our door this week, what would they sense before anyone said a word?

They wouldn’t see our vision statement. They would see whether we were patient with each other, and whether we made room. They would see whether the person who is hard to be around had someone sitting beside them.
