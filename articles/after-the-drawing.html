Song of Songs 1:4
26 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

After the Drawing

The same verse, and whose voice it is: Draw Me! The Voice Matters. The sheep, the goats and the list: Our God-Sponsored Projects.

Draw me after you; let us run. The king has brought me into his chambers. We will exult and rejoice in you; we will extol your love more than wine; rightly do they love you. (Song of Songs 1:4)

Early in my Christian walk we sang a chorus built on this verse. It was a lively little song, and people clapped along. It opened with rejoicing, took up the line about his love being better than wine, asked in the middle to be drawn and to run together, and came back round to the rejoicing at the end.

I don’t think it caught what she was saying.

The song starts where the verse finishes. In the Song of Songs the rejoicing comes last. First she asks: draw me after you. Then: let us run. Then: the king has brought me into his chambers. Only then does she say, we will exult and rejoice in you.

The joy is real, but it is where the asking arrives, not where it starts. The chorus started with the happy part and skipped the asking.

After You

She does not say draw me to you. She says draw me after you.

To be drawn to someone is to arrive. To be drawn after someone is to follow. It is the Bible’s language for following God: Israel is told to walk after the Lord, and Elijah on Mount Carmel tells the people that if the Lord is God, they should follow after him. Jesus used the same words to the fishermen by the lake. Our Bibles say follow me; the Greek says come after me.

So the first half of the line already points down a road. The second half starts her running down it.

One Word

In Hebrew, let us run is a single word: nārûṣāh. There is no together in it. That came from the song.

The verb is not a gentle one. It is used of Ahimaaz sprinting from the battle with news for David. It is the psalmist’s I will run in the way of your commandments when you enlarge my heart. It is Isaiah’s promise that those who wait for the Lord shall run and not be weary. People in the Bible who rûṣ are going somewhere, and going fast.

The form matters too. It is not a prediction, we will run some day. It is resolve: let’s run. The same voice that a moment ago could only ask to be drawn now wants to move.

So she is not asking for a quiet seat beside him. She is asking for a life in motion. Being drawn does not leave her sitting still. It gets her running.

Being drawn does not leave her sitting still. It gets her running.

And then the word us turns up. She asked draw me, one person. She answers let us run, more than one. Who is running with her?

Who Does What

Look at who does what in this verse.

Draw me: that is his doing. Let us run: that is hers. The king has brought me into his chambers: his again. Her running sits between two things he does. He starts it, and he finishes it.

Watch the small words, too. Draw me. Let us run. He has brought me. We will rejoice. One, then many, then one, then many. She steps into company, steps out of it alone into the king’s chamber, and steps back into company again.

Who are the us? The verse does not say. The nearest people in the poem are the young women of the verse before, who love him too. Spurgeon heard it as one person being drawn and pulling others along behind: All my brothers and sisters will run at once if You do draw me.1 Whoever they are, she does not run alone.

I find myself picturing a couple running in a park, hands held, laughing. The verse is not quite that. Here he is a step ahead, and she is running after him. That picture comes later in the book.

But the running is hers. It is not something done to her. Nobody carries her. She chose it; she said let’s. What the verse makes plain is where her running comes from and where it ends. It starts with him drawing her. It ends with him bringing her in. She does not get herself into the chamber. He brings her.

Made for This

So far I have read this as what it is: a woman speaking to the man she loves. But the longing in it is older and wider than one love story, and the church has always heard its own voice here too.

Augustine put it in one line at the start of his Confessions: our heart is restless until it rests in God.2 C.S. Lewis spent a sermon on the same ache. He called it a longing that nothing in this world quite satisfies, a homesickness for a country we have never visited. In another place he made it an argument: if we find a desire in ourselves that nothing here can meet, the likeliest explanation is that we were made for something beyond here.3

I believe that. Most people carry some sense that their life is meant for something, a calling, a purpose.

Draw me is that longing spoken out loud. But notice that she does not only ask to rest. She asks to run.

In Chariots of Fire, Eric Liddell tells his sister that God made him fast, and when I run, I feel his pleasure.4 It is a lovely line, and true. But it starts from a gift: he was made fast, so he runs. The Shulammite starts somewhere else. She does not say I was made for this. She says draw me. Her running does not begin with what she can do. It begins with being pulled towards someone. And she does not run alone.

Both can be true. The Song puts them in an order. First the drawing, then the running. First him, then what we do.

The List and the Drawing

The church loses that order easily. We begin to think that what we do for God is our relationship with God. We write a mission statement, fill the calendar, and find our sense of purpose in being busy. It feels like the same thing. There is a world of difference.

Jesus told a story about it. At the end of Matthew 25 the nations are gathered and divided, sheep on one side, goats on the other. The sheep are welcomed because they fed the hungry, welcomed the stranger, and visited the sick and those in prison. And they are astonished. Lord, when did we see you hungry? They had no idea. They had not been keeping a record.

The goats ask the same question, but they mean something else by it. If they had known it was him, they would have done it. It would have gone on the list.

I do not think the goats were idle. Earlier in Matthew, Jesus describes people who come to him at the end with a list: did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many mighty works in your name? He answers, I never knew you. They had been very busy. They had been doing things they thought were important. But none of it came out of knowing him.

The sheep did what they did because that is what sheep do. They are shepherded. The relationship was an us, and the doing came out of it without their noticing.

The church at Ephesus had the same problem. In Revelation, Jesus praises their toil and endurance, and then says, you have abandoned the love you had at first.

Jeremiah heard God say the same thing to his people long before. I remember the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride, how you followed me in the wilderness. The word for devotion is hesed, and followed me is literally your going after me. It is the same after as hers: draw me after you. Three verses later the complaint comes: they went after worthlessness, and became worthless. The word is hevel, vapour, breath. They did not stop moving. They went after something else.

So the opposite of her run is not rest. It is running after the wrong thing, or running with nothing behind it. A mission statement can be written by people who have stopped running after him. A list can be kept by people he never knew.

Her run is different. It sits between his drawing and his bringing. It begins with him and it ends with him. That is the difference between a calling and a list. A calling comes from a person. A list comes from us.

A calling comes from a person. A list comes from us.

Side by Side

The Song does not stay at the beginning.

Six chapters on, she speaks again, and listen to her now: Come, my beloved, let us go out into the fields and lodge in the villages; let us go out early to the vineyards and see whether the vines have budded, whether the grape blossoms have opened and the pomegranates are in bloom. There I will give you my love.

There is let us again. But this time the us is the two of them. He is not a step ahead. They are going out together, early, to see what is growing. And it is she who suggests it.

That is the couple in the park. It was there all along, just further down the road. The woman who began by asking to be drawn after him ends up walking beside him, out into the world, with somewhere to go and someone to go with.

That is not the quiet companionship of two people who have stopped going anywhere. It is a life in motion: shared, early, outdoors, looking for what is about to bloom. And it began with two words: draw me.

When I look at what fills my week, I have to ask where it started.

With him drawing me? Or with a list?
