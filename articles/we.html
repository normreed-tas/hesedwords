Article
Ephesians 3:20–21
19 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

We

One of a series on Ephesians. It follows Hidden, on the mystery of chapter 3, and Peace, on the wall that came down.

I learned Ephesians 3:20 early. In the church where I came to faith it was a memory verse, and more than that, it was a promise. God can do exceedingly, abundantly more than I can ask or even think. I said it often. I claimed it often. And I wrote it that way, too, with an “I” in the middle of it.

It was the same season in which I discovered prayer. Someone put Dick Eastman’s No Easy Road into my hands, and I can still see the cover. There were tapes to go with it, in a green vinyl case, and we worked through them in people’s lounge rooms in Canberra and then prayed together. So I had the verse, and I had the praying, side by side. It took me years to notice that they belonged to each other.

Because the verse doesn’t say “I”. It never did.

Now to Him who is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think, according to the power that works in us, to Him be glory in the church by Christ Jesus to all generations, forever and ever. Amen.1

We ask. We think. The power at work in us. In Paul’s Greek every one of those words is plural. There isn’t a single “I” or “me” in either verse. Paul is writing to a church, and he is speaking as one of a church.

And then there’s the line we usually leave off. Look at the posters and the coffee mugs and the little framed prints, and verse 20 nearly always travels alone. But Paul didn’t stop there. He said where the glory goes: in the church. That’s unusual. The New Testament has a dozen or so of these bursts of praise, these doxologies, and this is the only one that names the church. Everywhere else the glory simply goes to God. Here Paul says where God intends that glory to be seen. It’s in us. Not in me alone, in us.

So the verse I claimed as a personal promise turns out to be a family letter. I had been reading the address on the envelope as my name, when it said “the church at Ephesus”.

There’s a second thing I missed. Verse 20 doesn’t stand on its own. It’s the “Amen” at the end of a prayer, and to understand what “all that we ask” means, you have to know what was being asked.

Paul has just told us. For this reason I bow my knees to the Father … He prays that the church would be strengthened by the Spirit in the inner person. He prays that Christ would make his home in their hearts. And then the centre of it: that they, rooted and grounded in love, may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the width and length and depth and height — to know the love of Christ which passes knowledge; that you may be filled with all the fullness of God.

Notice “with all the saints”. Paul doesn’t expect anyone to grasp the love of Christ on their own. It’s too wide for one person to measure. You need the whole church just to get your arms around it.

That’s the prayer. Only then does Paul say that God is able to do far more than all we ask. Alexander Maclaren, the Manchester preacher, said over a century ago that God can give us more than we ask, “when we ask this”.2 The verse isn’t a blank cheque. It’s God’s answer to the church’s biggest prayer, and the answer is bigger than the prayer.

The “far more” was more of us.

I don’t want to be unfair to my younger self. The prayers I prayed with that verse weren’t wrong. I prayed about real things, and God was kind. But I can see now that they were prayed from where I stood. They were shaped by what I saw and what I needed. The frame was too small.

Calvin, writing about this verse, said it should teach us not to fear that faith “of a proper kind” could ever go too far.3 The words that matter are “of a proper kind”. The power does work in each believer. Paul isn’t asking us to pretend we have no needs. But it never works in one believer alone. The “me” isn’t removed. It’s put back where it belongs, inside the “we”.

Think of an orchestra. The second violin has a part of their own, written out on their own stand, and it matters. But tear that page out and play it by itself, and it isn’t the music. It only makes sense with everyone else playing. For years I’d been practising my part and calling it the symphony.

A church that thinks alike and prays alike can go badly wrong.

We don’t lack for people who agree with each other. Every faction agrees with itself. In Peace I wrote about the church I once came to, small and already split into us and them, each side quite sure it was right. They were all united, just with the wrong people. And when agreement like that turns to God, it gets worse. There’s a line in the 1970 film Cromwell. Every man who goes to war, Cromwell says, believes God is on his side: “I’ll warrant God should often wonder who is on his.”4

That isn’t what Paul means. The unity of Ephesians isn’t everyone holding the same opinions. A chapter later Paul talks about different gifts, different callings, apostles and pastors and teachers, all of it building one body. The agreement is about what we want from God, not about agreeing on everything else.

Jesus has a word for it.

Again I say to you that if two of you agree on earth concerning anything that they ask, it will be done for them by My Father in heaven. (Matthew 18:19)

The word for “agree” is symphōneō. It means to sound together, and it’s where we get “symphony”. Look at what comes just before it: if your brother sins against you, go to him. Be reconciled. The agreement in prayer grows out of reconciliation. People who were at odds learn to sound together, and then they pray.

So back to the orchestra. A symphony isn’t everyone playing the same note. It’s many instruments, each with its own part, playing one score.

For years I’d been practising my part and calling it the symphony.

I heard that once, in a lounge room in Canberra.

There were seven or eight of us, working through the Eastman tapes and then praying. Living in Canberra, you were reminded of Parliament every day, and one night someone began to pray for the government. It was a short prayer. When it finished, someone else picked it up and added to it, praying for a different side of the same thing. Then another. Nobody prayed long. Nobody tried to pray the whole prayer.

Looking back, the picture I have is a wall going up. One person lays a brick. The next picks up another and sets it in line with the first, or on top of it. The prayer took shape. It had substance, and everyone in the room could see it.

Then someone prayed for the prayer that opened Parliament each day, that it would be different. Someone added that it would calm the House. Someone else, that it would bring peace and a sense of unity. That it would make a difference.

Paul says the church is “being built together for a dwelling place of God” (Ephesians 2:22). That night we weren’t talking about it. We were watching it happen, one brick at a time.

The early church knew this too. In Acts 4 they raised their voice to God with one accord and asked for boldness. They got boldness, and the place was shaken, and they were all filled.

The next week, when we gathered, someone brought a newspaper clipping.

It was from The Canberra Times. The headline read “Prayer Calms Parliament”. It was a significant article about the prayer that opened Parliament, and as we read it, we realised it said, almost word for word, what we had prayed the week before. That it was different. That it had calmed the House. That it had brought peace and a sense of unity.

I’ve thought about that prayer meeting many times since, and what strikes me now is how far beyond our asking it went. We had prayed that the prayer would make a difference. We hadn’t asked to see it. We hadn’t asked for a journalist to sit in the gallery and write it down, or for a national newspaper to print it, or for someone to cut it out and bring it to a lounge room in Canberra. And we certainly hadn’t asked for what it did to us, to each of us and to all of us together.

That’s verse 20. Exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think.

Then came verse 21. We didn’t just admire the clipping and move on. We met, as the church, and gave thanks, and gave the glory to him. To Him be glory in the church. Without knowing it, we had lived both verses, one week apart, in the order Paul wrote them.

John Chrysostom, preaching on this verse in the fourth century, said that God has already done far more than we asked, because “neither did we ever ask these things, nor did we expect them”.5 He was preaching on a letter about outsiders being brought in, Gentiles made fellow citizens and members of one household. I think I know what he meant. The thing no one in the early church thought to pray for was other people. The “far more” was more of us.

I suspect that’s still true. When I claimed this verse for myself, I was usually asking for more for me. But when a church prays it together, the answer God gives often comes back as more of the church: more people, more reconciled, more built together, more able to see what God is doing.

So I’ve learned to write the verse differently. Not “God can do more than I can ask or think”, but:

Now to Him who is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think, according to the power that works in us, to Him be glory in the church.

What might your church see if it prayed that way, laying one brick on another until the prayer had a shape everyone could see? I don’t know. That’s rather the point. Nor did we.
