Ephesians 4:4–7
22 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

One

One of a series on Ephesians. It carries on from Called, and goes back to the wall that came down in Peace.

Most churches have tried to say what they are for in a single line. It goes on the sign out the front, at the top of the bulletin, on the website.

A line like that can do one of two jobs. It can say what a church hopes to be. Or it can be something the church measures itself against, a line that tells it plainly when it has failed. The first kind is easier to write. The second is harder to live with.

When Paul reaches the turning point of his letter to the Ephesians, he stops describing what God has done and starts telling the church how to live. “Walk worthily of the calling with which you were called” (4:1).1 Then, before he says much more about how, he writes this:

There is one body and one Spirit, even as you also were called in one hope of your calling, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in us all. (Ephesians 4:4–6)

Read it aloud. After the first few words there is hardly a verb in it. Nobody is told to do anything. It is a list: one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father. Seven times, “one.”

It sounds less like an argument and more like something a church would say together. One older commentary called it “an embryo creed.”2 Whether or not Paul was using words the churches already knew, the first readers would have heard it the way we hear a creed. You say those words because they are true, not because someone has just talked you into them.

The Greek makes the repetition stronger. The word for “one” changes its form to match the word it goes with, and it has three forms: heis, mia and hen. Paul’s list uses all three. Body, Spirit and baptism take hen. Hope and faith take mia. Lord and God take heis. Paul didn’t choose that; the nouns did. But if you read it aloud in Greek, you hear “one” seven times, in all three of its forms.

Where does a list like this come from? The clue is in the middle of it: “one Lord.”

A Jewish child in Paul’s world grew up hearing the Shema every morning and evening: “Hear, Israel: Yahweh is our God. Yahweh is one” (Deuteronomy 6:4). In the Greek Bible Paul read, the last words are kyrios heis estin, “the Lord is one.” Paul had been saying that line all his life.

He had used it this way before. Writing to Corinth, he says, “to us there is one God, the Father … and one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6). He takes Israel’s confession that God is one and places Jesus inside it. He does the same here: one Lord, and then one God and Father.

So the list doesn’t start with the church. It starts with God. The church is one body because one Spirit gives it life, it belongs to one Lord, and one Father is over it. Its oneness comes from his.

That explains the verse just before the list. Paul tells the church to be “eager to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace” (4:3). He says keep, not make. The word is tēreō. Luke uses it of Peter being kept in prison (Acts 12:5), and Jesus uses it of keeping his commands (John 14:15). It means holding on to something that has been put in your care.

Paul doesn’t ask the church to create unity. It already has unity, because God gave it. The church’s job is to look after it and not break it.

Later in the chapter Paul speaks of unity again, this time as something still ahead: “until we all attain to the unity of the faith” (4:13). So the church has unity already and is still growing into it. But the growing starts from what has been given.

The difference matters. A church that thinks it has to make unity tends to work at it through control: rules, and the right people in charge. A church that knows it has been given unity works at not losing it.

Imagine the passage without those three verses. Paul would go straight from “walk worthily … being eager to keep the unity of the Spirit” (4:1–3) to the gifts Christ gives his church (4:7–16).

It would still read well: be humble, be gentle, be patient, bear with one another, keep the peace. But it would read like a list of rules, the kind any club could pin on its noticeboard. And it would put the whole weight on us: how hard we try, and how patient we manage to be.

The seven “ones” stop that. Before Paul asks anything more of the church, he reminds it who it is.

He does this across the whole letter. Chapters 1 to 3 tell the church what God has done. Chapters 4 to 6 tell it how to live. Here, at the point where the letter turns, he packs the first half into seven short lines, so the second half has something to stand on.

Without them, “keep the unity” would mean “try harder to get along.” With them, it means “don’t break what God has already made.”

A church doesn’t make people important. The Father already has.

The list climbs. It begins with the Spirit (one body, one Spirit, one hope), moves to the Lord (one Lord, one faith, one baptism), and ends with “one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in us all.”

Paul could have ended with “one God.” He ends with “Father.”

That changes what kind of thing the church is. An organisation has members. A family has brothers and sisters. You can join an organisation and you can leave it, and its members can vote someone out. A family doesn’t work that way. You didn’t choose your brothers and sisters. You can fall out with them and refuse to speak to them for years, but they are still your brothers and sisters, because you have the same father.

That is why unity in the church can only be kept, never made. We didn’t decide who belongs. The Father did.

Paul has already said this once. In chapter 3 he prays to “the Father … from whom every family in heaven and on earth is named” (3:14–15). In Greek, “father” is patēr and “family” is patria: every family takes its name from the Father. So when “one God and Father of all” closes the list in chapter 4, the prayer of chapter 3 comes back as a confession.

But who are the “all”? The Greek word can mean all people or all things, and readers have taken it both ways. Some say Paul means the church, because the list is about the church and “in us all” sounds like God living in his people.3 Others point to how often Ephesians looks beyond the church. God “created all things” (3:9). He plans “to sum up all things in Christ” (1:10). Every family in heaven and on earth is named from him.

I don’t think the grammar settles it. But the letter gives a way to hold both readings. God is Father of all because he made everyone. Being his children in the full sense comes another way: he “predestined us for adoption as children” (1:5). Everyone carries his name, because he made them. Not everyone has come home.

That matters most at the door.

The difficult person in a church isn’t always someone already inside. Sometimes it is someone who wants to come in: a person with a past, a reputation, or a way of behaving that makes everyone uneasy.

Ephesians was written to people who had once been exactly that. In chapter 2 Paul reminds the Gentile believers that they had been “far off” and “strangers,” and that Christ came to them and brought them near (2:12–17). The “one body” of chapter 4 was made by bringing outsiders in.

So when Paul writes “one God and Father of all,” the people at the door are already in view. If the Father made them, they are not strangers to him. They are family who haven’t come home yet.

That leaves the church with a hard question. A church that thinks it has to make unity will be tempted to keep the difficult person out, because they make unity harder. It is easier to stay one if everyone is already like us. A church that knows its unity was given can’t do that. It doesn’t own the door. The house belongs to the Father, and he decides who is welcome in it.

About twenty-five years ago, in Adelaide, our staff went away on a retreat to work out the church’s vision. We settled on a line to describe ourselves: “A church for all people.”

It was a good line. But I suggested a different one, and later we adopted it for the church in Hobart, the same church where the signs came down: “A church where everyone is important.”

“A church for all people” is about the door. It says anyone may come in. That is true, and it matters. But “all” can stay a crowd. You can welcome all people and still not notice the one who sat at the back and left before the coffee.

“Everyone” means each person, one at a time.

It doesn’t own the door. The house belongs to the Father, and he decides who is welcome in it.

Paul makes the same move. He ends his list with “one God and Father of all,” and his next words are “But to each one of us, the grace was given” (4:7). In Greek, “of all” is pantōn and “to each one” is heni hekastō. The word “one” comes back a last time, and now it means a single person. The Father of all doesn’t love a crowd. He gives grace to each one.

For me the Hobart line was never mainly a description. It was a test. I wanted us to be measured against it, and I wanted to say plainly what it would mean: if people came among us and did not feel important, we had failed.

A church doesn’t make people important. The Father already has. The church’s job is to notice each one, and to keep noticing.
