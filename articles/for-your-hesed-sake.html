For Your Hesed’s Sake

Companions: The Turning Left Open, Nobody Wants Nathan, and the word study Zakar.

Most of us carry a simple order in our heads. We do wrong. We are sorry. We say so. God forgives.

It is not a wrong order. Repentance matters, and Scripture calls for it again and again. But the order hides an assumption: that our repentance is what moves God. We turn, and so he acts. Our sorrow is the reason.

If that is true, then repentance has become a kind of payment. Not money, and not good deeds, but something we bring and hand over: the right amount of sorrow, the right words, enough tears to show we mean it. And like any payment, it leaves us wondering whether we have paid enough.

Psalm 6 is the prayer of a man in deep trouble. He believes God is angry with him. He is sick, frightened and worn out with crying. If anyone needed to bring the right payment, he did. Yet in the one place where he gives God a reason to help him, he does not mention his repentance at all.

The psalm

The psalm opens with God’s anger. “O LORD, rebuke me not in your anger, nor discipline me in your wrath” (v.1). David doesn’t argue that he deserves no correction. He asks that it not come in anger.

Then he describes his state. He is “languishing”, and his bones are “troubled” (v.2). His soul is “greatly troubled” too (v.3). Body and soul are failing together. Then he breaks off in the middle of a sentence: “But you, O LORD — how long?” He doesn’t finish it, and nothing in the Hebrew finishes it for him.

By verse 6 he has been crying for a long time. “I am weary with my moaning; every night I flood my bed with tears; I drench my couch with my weeping.” His eyes are wasting from grief (v.7), and he has enemies watching.

In the middle of all this he makes his request:

“Turn, O LORD, deliver my life; save me for the sake of your steadfast love” (v.4).

He makes three requests: turn, deliver, save. He gives one reason. It isn’t his sorrow or his tears, and it isn’t a promise to do better. He gives no confession anywhere in the psalm: he never names a sin and never says “I have done wrong.” The only reason he offers is God’s steadfast love. In Hebrew, that word is hesed.

Turn

The first of David’s three requests is a single word in Hebrew: shuvah, “turn.”

Shuv is the word the Old Testament uses for repentance. When the prophets call Israel to repent, this is the verb: turn back, return to the LORD. It is what people are told to do.

In Psalm 6, David says it to God. He doesn’t say “I will turn.” He asks God to turn.

He isn’t the only one to pray this way. At the end of Lamentations, with Jerusalem in ruins, the people pray, hashivenu YHWH elekha ve-nashuvah: turn us back to you, O LORD, and we will turn (Lamentations 5:21). In Jeremiah, God hears Ephraim grieving and praying, hashiveni ve-ashuvah: turn me back, and I will turn (Jeremiah 31:18). The ESV renders them “restore us” and “bring me back,” so the English reader doesn’t see the same verb used twice.

The order is the same in both prayers. God turns first, and then the person turns. The people’s turning is real and it matters, but it comes second. It is the result of God’s turning, not the cause of it.

David’s psalm sits at the start of that order. Before he says anything about himself, he asks God to turn towards him.

For your steadfast love’s sake

“Save me for the sake of your steadfast love.”

The Hebrew is lema’an chasdekha. Lema’an means “for the sake of,” “because of,” “on account of.” It points to where the reason lies. David places the reason in God, not in himself.

Hesed is hard to put into one English word. The ESV says “steadfast love,” and older translations say “mercy” or “lovingkindness.” It is love that has bound itself: kindness that keeps a commitment, and keeps it when the other person has given it no reason to.

It is also something God said about himself. When Moses asked to see God’s glory, the LORD passed before him and proclaimed his own name: “The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness” (Exodus 34:6). That is God’s own description of himself, and hesed is at the centre of it.

So when David asks to be saved “for the sake of your steadfast love,” he isn’t pointing to anything he has done. He is pointing to what God has said. God will act because of who he said he is.

The next verse gives the reason in another form: “For in death there is no remembrance of you; in Sheol who will give you praise?” (v.5). A dead man can’t remember God or thank him. If God lets David die, there is one fewer voice to tell of his steadfast love. David’s argument is still about God.

How repentance became a payment

If this is so plain in Psalm 6, how did repentance come to feel like a payment?

Part of the answer is in a translation. For a thousand years the Western church read the Bible in Latin. When Jesus began preaching, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand” (Matthew 4:17), the Latin had poenitentiam agite, which could be heard as “do penance.” By the late Middle Ages, repentance had become a sacrament with steps: be sorry, confess to a priest, then make satisfaction by doing what the priest set. Some of that satisfaction could even be bought.

In 1517 a young monk named Martin Luther published his first book. It was a short exposition, in German, of the seven penitential psalms, and the first psalm in it was Psalm 6. Seven months later he posted his Ninety-five Theses. The first one went back to Jesus’ word: “When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said, ‘Repent,’ he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance.” Repentance was not a transaction to be completed. It was a way of life, a continual turning.

Nearly five hundred years later, the New York pastor Timothy Keller began a short piece on repentance with that same thesis. He drew a line between two kinds of repentance. In what he called “religion,” repentance is a way of keeping God happy so the blessings keep coming. “In religion,” he wrote, “we try to earn our forgiveness with our repentance.” In the gospel, repentance receives a forgiveness that has already been earned by Christ.

Psalm 6 was written roughly a thousand years before the cross, but the ground is the same. David doesn’t earn anything with his tears. He asks God to act because of God’s steadfast love.

Keller says one more thing about gospel repentance: that in the end it is sweet. David has no sweetness yet. He is still crying himself to sleep. But his request rests on the right ground anyway. You don’t have to feel the joy of grace before you can ask God to act on his steadfast love.

The weeping that is heard

Then the psalm turns, suddenly. Nothing outside has changed. The enemies are still there, and the bed is still wet. But David says:

“Depart from me, all you workers of evil, for the LORD has heard the sound of my weeping. The LORD has heard my plea; the LORD accepts my prayer” (vv.8–9).

The Hebrew is kol bikhyi, “the voice of my weeping.” The crying itself is something God hears. David hasn’t produced a careful prayer of sorrow, with the right words in the right order. He has wept, night after night, and the LORD has heard the weeping.

So the tears were not wasted. But they weren’t the price either. God did not weigh them and decide there were enough. They were the honest sound of a man in trouble, turning his face towards the God he had asked to turn.

That is where repentance belongs. It matters. Without it we would be pretending nothing was wrong. But it is our response to God, not the reason he acts. David’s tears reach a God who had already declared himself “abounding in steadfast love,” and the psalm ends with David’s enemies turning back in shame, not David.

Seven psalms

Psalm 6 is the first of seven psalms the church has long called the penitential psalms: 6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130 and 143. David did not arrange them as a set. Christians gathered them later and prayed them together, especially in Lent.

But whoever chose these seven chose a set that begins and ends in the same place. The first plea of the first psalm is “save me for the sake of your steadfast love.” The last verse of the last psalm is “in your steadfast love you will cut off my enemies” (Psalm 143:12). The church’s prayers of repentance open and close with God’s hesed.

So when we come to God in sorrow, we can come the way David did, with nothing in our hands. Not with the right amount of tears, or the right words, or a promise to do better. We can ask God to turn, because of who he said he is.

The LORD has heard the sound of my weeping.
