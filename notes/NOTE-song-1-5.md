## The passage

> שְׁחוֹרָה אֲנִי וְנָאוָה בְּנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלִַם כְּאָהֳלֵי קֵדָר כִּירִיעוֹת שְׁלֹמֹה
>
> ESV: *I am very dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem, like the tents of Kedar,
> like the curtains of Solomon.*

## Four things the English does not carry

**1. There is no adversative.** The Hebrew is שְׁחוֹרָה אֲנִי וְנָאוָה — *black I am and
lovely*, joined by a plain waw. ESV's *but* is a decision. So is its *very*: there is no
intensifier in v.5, and the attenuated form שְׁחַרְחֹרֶת (blackish, swarthy) belongs to
v.6, not here.

This is the session's main finding and the one that reshapes the verse. See **The "and"
and the "but"** below, which is where it was actually worked.

**2. Kedar means dark.** קֵדָר is a north Arabian tribe (Gen 25:13, Ishmael's second son)
whose tents were woven from black goat hair — and the name sits on the root קדר, *to be
dark*. The line runs close to *black as the tents of Blackness*. The etymology is not a
modern conjecture: Bernard has it in Sermon XXVI, "Kedar, which means darkness," presumably
via Jerome.
`[verify]` — the root sense, and whether the poet's ear could be expected to hear it.

**3. Both similes are tents.** יְרִיעוֹת is not drapery. It is the technical term for the
tabernacle's tent-curtains throughout Exodus 26 — woven panels, tent fabric. So the verse
names two tent-coverings, not a defect and a compensation: the weathered goat-hair of a
nomad and the costly hangings of a king. And the two are simply juxtaposed, כְּ… כְּ, with
no conjunction between them.

**4. The crowd is finally named.** They have been in the room since 1:3 — *they love you*,
*we will run* — unnamed and unaddressed. Here she turns and speaks to them for the first
time: בְּנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלִַם, no vocative particle. City women. And the first thing she says
to them is a defence.

## What v.6 supplies

The *why*, and it changes the register of v.5.

- **The sun did it.** שְׁזָפַתְנִי הַשָּׁמֶשׁ — the sun *looked at* me. So the sentence is
  *do not look at me, because the sun looked at me*: their staring and the sun's staring
  are the same act. `[verify]` — the semantic range of *shazaph*, a rare verb.
- **Two heats.** נִחֲרוּ־בִי, from the root for burning, of her mother's sons; then the sun
  scorches. Human anger and solar heat in one verse.
- **She softens the word when explaining.** שְׁחוֹרָה in v.5 when defending; the
  reduplicated שְׁחַרְחֹרֶת in v.6 when accounting for it. The unhedged form is the
  confident one.
- **"The sons of my mother,"** בְּנֵי אִמִּי. The Song names no father anywhere in eight
  chapters; the mother recurs. Noted, not interpreted.

**Social geography, not race.** Sun-darkening from field labour, in a world where pale skin
meant indoors and indoors meant exemption from work. The daughters of Jerusalem are *city*
women. The English *black* carries freight the Hebrew does not.

## Where she stops blaming

The last clause of v.6 is the load-bearing one for the whole reading.

כַּרְמִי שֶׁלִּי לֹא נָטָרְתִּי — *my vineyard, the one belonging to me, I did not keep.*

- The possession is **doubled** — not merely *my vineyard* but *my vineyard that is mine*.
- The verb is **first person, active, negated**. Not *it was not kept*, not *they would not
  let me*. She had the construction available to spread the blame and used the one that
  lands on herself.

So: **explanation for the darkness, admission for the vineyard.** She explains what was
done to her and confesses what she did not do, and the line between them is exactly where
she stops blaming.

Norm asked whether there is bitterness, and offered the merciful reading — *couldn't*, not
*didn't want to*. The grammar tilts against it. The excuse was real and available and she
declines it. But **"not bitter" is a reading, not a finding**: the grammar shows she takes
responsibility; whether she takes it warmly is tone, and tone is what Hebrew poetry is
least willing to hand over.

## The "and" and the "but" — the session's main work

Norm's formulation, at the end and in his own words, is the thing to keep:

> The Song is not that we see him because we have changed. It is that what we see in him
> changes us.

The argument that gets there:

**The traditional "but" makes humility the gateway.** She sees her fault, turns, and is
admitted. Beauty compensates for a defect that had to be owned first.

**The "and" relocates humility rather than abolishing it.** She is brought into the chamber
at **1:4**, and describes herself at **1:5**. Nothing in the poem has her clean herself up
and then be drawn. And 1:6 is still a genuine confession — so the self-knowledge is not
skipped. It arrives *after* the chamber, as what being inside produces rather than what
buys entry. Humility as fruit, not as entry fee.

**The second support, and it is stronger than the conjunction.** She calls herself נָאוָה
at 1:5. He does not call her beautiful until **1:15**. She claims it first, unprompted,
sun-darkened, in front of city women. False humility cannot produce that sentence.

**Caution, and it matters.** *The waw alone does not prove the theology.* It can carry a
concessive sense, and ESV's *but* is a decision rather than an error. The case rests on the
**order of 1:4 before 1:5** and on her claiming the beauty ten verses before he does. The
conjunction supports that; it cannot bear it. A piece that argues from the waw alone will
be beaten by the first reader who knows Hebrew.

**Reading within the frame** on all of the above: the movement from chamber to self-sight
is ours, assembled here. The poem does not state it.

## What the resources do with it

All three take the concession reading, and **none is working from the Hebrew waw** — they
have Vulgate/KJV *but comely*, so the decision is made before they begin.

**Bernard — four sermons on this one verse, XXV–XXVIII.** (XXVI breaks off partway when
his brother Gerard dies.)

- He assumes hostility from the start, which corroborates the defence reading independently
  of the Hebrew: she is "obliged to reply to the attacks and reproaches of those who are
  envious of her glory." They have been staring, and he says so.
- **On the address:** she calls them *daughters of Jerusalem* when they "had deserved for
  their wickedness to be called daughters of Babylon, or daughters of Baal." A blessing
  returned for an insult. He turns it into a rule for anyone set over others — pastors are
  "not masters, but physicians of the soul." This is the most usable thing he has here.
- **On the contradiction:** "Is not this a contradiction in terms? By no means." He divides
  **colour from form** — form inherent, colour only a quality. Black pupils, black gems,
  black hair against a pale face. Beautiful in form, dark in colour, "in the place of her
  pilgrimage."
- He refuses to let her say *I was black*. She says *I am*, and he holds the present tense.
- **Paul as the instance** (XXV.5): "his bodily presence is weak, and his speech
  contemptible" — "Black according to your judgment, but beautiful in the judgment of God."
- **He sees the grammatical ambiguity** the doubled kaph creates: do both similes attach to
  *I am black*, or one to each clause? XXVI calls the first "more simple," the second "more
  obscure," and works the harder one first. XXVIII reopens it and offers a reading where
  everything refers to blackness — Solomon's curtains being his campaign tent, sun-blackened
  outside to keep the costly furniture within unspoiled. There: "the Bride does not deny her
  blackness, but makes excuse for it."

**Spurgeon — nothing on 1:5.** Eleven sermons on chapter 1 and not one on this verse.
Nearest are **990** *Self-Humbling and Self-Searching* and **1936** *The Unkept Vineyard*,
both beginning at 1:6. 990 gives the humility reading in a line: she is *fairest among
women* at 1:8, yet "in her own esteem she felt herself to be so little fair and so much
uncomely, that she besought them not even to look upon her." His thesis is that the fairest
Christians are the most shamefaced.

**Hudson Taylor breaks the verse into dialogue** — the only one of the three to do so:

> "Ah," she cries, "I am black" — "But comely," interjects the Bridegroom, with inimitable
> grace and tenderness. "Nay, 'black as the tents of Kedar,'" she continues. "Yet to Me," He
> responds, "thou art 'comely as the curtains of Solomon!'"

**Assessed, not adopted.** He gets the *sequence* right — he places this after the chamber:
"as the rising sun reveals that which was hidden in the darkness, so His light has revealed
her blackness to her." That is the order we arrived at independently.

But the cut runs against the Hebrew, and in a specific direction. One clause, one subject,
one vocative; he cuts between אֲנִי and its second predicate adjective, which is the least
plausible seam in the sentence. No speaker change is marked. And **the cost is that she no
longer says both words about herself** — she says only the dark one and must be told the
other. His added "Nay" has her resist the compliment and double down on the blackness. That
is not the woman the Hebrew has.

**The pattern across all three is worth keeping.** Three separate reliefs from one sentence:
an adversative particle, a colour-versus-form distinction, and a second speaker. Centuries
apart, all easing the same discomfort — a woman saying *I am dark and I am beautiful* in one
breath.

## The arc — Norm's second finding, and the horizon for the book

The poem reuses her own words at the end.

- **The vineyard returns in her mouth.** 1:6: *my vineyard, the one that's mine, I did not
  keep.* **8:12: כַּרְמִי שֶׁלִּי לְפָנָי** — *my vineyard, the one that's mine, is before
  me.* Same doubled possessive, no negated verb. Solomon can have his thousand; hers is her
  own and she is standing in it. The sentence she could not finish at the start is how she
  closes the book.
  `[verify]` — that the two phrases match consonant for consonant, and whether anything else
  in the Song reuses a clause this exactly.
- **The staring reverses.** 1:6, *al tir'uni*, **do not look at me**. 6:13, *return,
  return, O Shulammite, that we may **look upon you***. She never asks for that; the chorus
  arrives at it.
- **The brothers return and she answers over them.** 8:8, *we have a little sister… what
  shall we do for her?* — the voices that burned at her in 1:6, still managing her. Her
  reply: *I was a wall, and my breasts were like towers.*

**Would she rephrase 1:5 at the end?** The session's answer: she would not need to. The
words were true when she said them. What changes is that she no longer has to say them into
a stare.

**What drives the change is what she is looking at.** She is asked to describe him at
5:10–16 and does it at length, head to foot — the fullest sustained looking in the book. And
he calls her נָאוָה from 1:15 onward and *altogether beautiful, there is no flaw in you* at
4:7, which is the word she claimed once, defensively, and then never has to keep asserting.
Someone else is holding it.

**Two cautions on that reading.**

1. **It is Bernard's, and we may have caught it from him.** 2 Corinthians 3:18 —
   *beholding the glory of the Lord, transformed into the same image* — is quoted in Sermon
   XXV describing this exact movement. Confirmation or contamination, and worth knowing
   which before a piece leans on it.
2. **The Song will not let the process complete.** 5:6 has her open the door and he is gone.
   The looking is interrupted repeatedly, and the last verse is *make haste, my beloved* —
   not resolution, still wanting. Which is Norm's own answer of 16 Sep arriving from the
   other end: the wanting deepens with proximity. A piece treating 8:14 as arrival would
   contradict both the book and its author.

## Where this leaves Norm — his words, 17 Sep 2026

Recorded because it decides what a piece can be, and it is his to say:

> The other learning has been the change in her by the end of the song. Not to be looked to
> inviting being looked on. That is a transformation of significant proportion — something
> has changed and that is what we explore going through this book.

So: **the missing "but" is a fact he is still sitting with, and the arc is the direction for
the rest of the reading.** Neither is drafted. The first is not a piece until he knows what
it does to what he was taught; the second is not a piece at all but the frame for the book.

One thing said in session and worth keeping on the record: nobody preached him an error.
*But comely* is in Tyndale, Geneva, KJV and the Vulgate before them, and the Hebrew can be
read concessively. What he has found is that a choice was made and then stopped being
visible as a choice.

## Considered and declined

**Writing a 1:5 piece from this session.** Declined, and by Norm. He named the finding as
unresolved rather than settled, and a piece arguing the "and" while he is still working out
what it costs him would be the project publishing ahead of its author. PROJECT-SONGS makes
the held note the default precisely for this.

**Arguing the theology from the waw.** Declined on the merits. The conjunction is ambiguous
and a piece resting on it is refutable in one sentence. The order of 1:4 → 1:5, and נָאוָה
at 1:5 against 1:15, carry it; the waw only assists.

**Taking Taylor's dialogue reading.** Declined. Right sequence, wrong surgery — see above.

## Loose ends

- **1:4b is still owed, and this session went past it.** The chamber at 1:4 is now
  load-bearing for the 1:5 reading — *brought in first, then honest self-sight* — and 1:4b
  has still never been read. It was already carrying the 1:3 reflection's threshold image on
  credit. It is now carrying two debts.
- **The *alamot* / *benot Yerushalayim* question.** The maidens at 1:3 are עֲלָמוֹת; the
  group addressed from 1:5 is בְּנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלִַם. Whether the poem means one group under
  two names or two groups is unsettled, and it decides the contrast-or-contagion fork left
  open in `NOTE-song-chapter-1-sequence.md`. If they are one group and they travel, the 1:3
  reflection's foil reading is holding still a chorus that moves.
  `[verify]` — every occurrence of both terms, and whether they ever appear together.
- **שַׁחַר at 6:10.** *Who is this who looks forth like the dawn* — same three consonants as
  her שְׁחוֹרָה at 1:5. Whether Hebrew heard one root or two is a lexical question.
  **Suggestive–thin**, and flagged because it is pretty, which is exactly when to slow down.
  `[verify]`.
- **Bernard Sermon XVIII** still carries its visible editorial note — opening sentences
  reconstructed from a disrupted OCR source, wanting a check against a print copy. Not
  touched by this session; recorded so it stays live.
- **Glossary.** No headword exists for *na'vah*, *shachor* or *kerem*. If the "and" ever
  becomes a piece, נָאוָה is the word it turns on and would want seeding.
- **Spurgeon's absence from 1:5 is itself worth noting.** Eleven chapter 1 sermons and he
  starts at 1:6 — at the confession, not at the claim. Whether that is a preaching
  preference or the "but" making 1:5 look like nothing more than a preamble to 1:6 is
  guesswork, but the shape of the gap is suggestive.
