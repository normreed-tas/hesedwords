## 1. Were the acrostics a memory device? — CORRECTED

**What the literature I opened says.** Van der Spuy (2008) surveys the proposed functions and
lists five: mnemonic, enumeration, completeness/totality, aesthetic, and visual/aural. On the
mnemonic reading he writes that it "is the most commonly mentioned feature". His support for
it is two encyclopaedia entries, the *Jewish Encyclopedia* (Abrahams) and the *Encyclopaedia
Judaica* (1971), and one dissertation (Maloney). All three **assert** the function. None of
them argues it from ancient evidence. His own framing is cautious: the features are
"possible", and not all are "in focus in the mind of the poet at all times".

**The completeness reading has the stronger argued support.** It rests on:
- Kimelman, *JBL* 113 (1994), on Ps 145, which unites the form with the theme of *kol*, "all";
- Burden (1987) on Ps 145;
- Wilt, *The Bible Translator* 44 (1993), with Gottwald, on Lamentations: the subject
  "exhausted in its totality".

**The aesthetic reading** (a display of skill) is also held: Gous 1999 and Maloney 2006.

**How settled is it?** Not settled. No position is excluded. The mnemonic claim is the
specific failure mode the brief was set to catch: repeated everywhere, demonstrated nowhere I
looked.

**The text itself argues against a purely mnemonic purpose:**
- Pss 9–10, 25, 34, 37 and 145 all have missing or irregular letters (items 2–4 below).
- Lam 2–4 change the order of two letters.

A memory scaffold that is routinely broken is a weak scaffold. The pull towards totality and
display is stronger.

**For the book.** Change the spine from *remembering* to *completeness*: A to Z, nothing left
out. That also suits the closing exercise better. A twelve-year-old writing an A–Z prayer is
not memorising it. They are being made to bring *everything*, including the awkward letters.
Norm's Ps 119 caution, that the form constrains as well as reveals, sits naturally inside
that.

**Not opened.** Freedman's own text, Soll's *Psalm 119: Matrix, Form, and Setting* (1991), and
the major commentaries (Kraus, Hossfeld–Zenger, Goldingay). If the chapter is going to name a
scholar, one of these should be opened first.

**Shared:**
- both lack *vav*;
- both end with an extra *pe* line after *tav*;
- both extra lines are built on the root *padah*, "redeem".

- The word itself differs: imperative *pedeh* ("Redeem!", a prayer) against participle *podeh*

  Ps 34 says it is done.
- Ps 25 has three further irregularities that Ps 34 does not: *bet* is not first, *qof* is
  missing, and *resh* is doubled.

The pair holds. It is not half-right.

Notes on the table:
- **ayin.** Where it is expected, at 28b, the line opens *le'olam* (lamed). The LXX suggests an
  original *ayin* word. As the text stands, *ayin* is absent.
- **The intervening *tsadi*.** Verse 29 also begins with *tsadi* (*tsaddiqim*), inside the gap.
  That is incidental, not a stanza.
- **tav.** It is the second letter of the line, after the conjunction: *u-teshu'at*.

**The underlying unit** is a stanza of about four lines. The verse divisions came later and
do not always line up with it. "Every other verse" is the usual case, not the rule.

**Summary of the break:**
- Ps 9 is near-regular from aleph to kaf, missing only dalet.
- Ps 10 picks up at lamed.
- The middle of Ps 10 is the gap.
- The sequence resumes cleanly for the last four letters.

### The other evidence

- **No superscription on Ps 10.** Confirmed from the Hebrew: it opens directly with *lammah*.
- **Titles across Book I** (Pss 1–41, checked by the opening words of each): only Pss 1, 2,
  10 and 33 lack a title. Ps 9 and Ps 11 both have one.
- **LXX and Vulgate.** They treat 9 and 10 as one psalm, Psalm 9. That is why their numbering
  runs one behind the Hebrew until Ps 147. *Source: Wikipedia, Psalm 10, citing a parallel
  Latin/English psalter. Well known, but I did not open a primary LXX text.*

### How much depends on emendation?

The "one broken poem" reading does **not** depend on emendation. These are all in the
unemended Hebrew:
- the run from aleph to kaf;
- the lamed opening Ps 10;
- the gap;
- the clean end.

Emendation works in the *other* direction. Scholars who restore dalet in Ps 9, or rebuild
mem–tsadi in Ps 10, are trying to repair the poem. So a twelve-year-old can be told plainly,
without hedging: "The letters run from A almost to the middle, then they stop. A few come
back at the end. Nobody knows why."

The one thing to say out loud is the limit: it is *one poem* on the evidence of the letters,
the missing title and the old Greek Bible. No manuscript has been found that shows it whole.

## Lower priority

### Proverbs 31:10–31 — CONFIRMED
A full, regular acrostic. All 22 letters open verses 10–31 in order, with no gaps and no
doubles.

### Nahum 1 — partial and contested
At verse starts, the text shows:
- aleph at v. 2;
- gimel at v. 4;
- he at v. 5;
- tet at v. 7;
- vav (*u-veshetef*) at v. 8.

The rest falls mid-verse or needs emendation:
- bet is in 3b;
- dalet needs an emendation in 4b;
- zayin is the second word of v. 6;
- after about kaf the pattern is gone.

It is real enough to be noticed, but too reconstructed for this reader. Leave it out, or give
it one sentence at most ("one prophet starts an alphabet and doesn't finish it").

### Lamentations 1–5 — CONFIRMED
- **Lam 1:** 22 verses in regular order.
- **Lam 2, 3 and 4:** 22 verses each, with **pe before ayin**. In Lam 3 each letter opens
  three verses (66 verses).
- **Lam 5:** 22 verses, no acrostic. The opening letters run zayin, nun, yod, mem, ayin, and
  on with no order.

The pe–ayin order in Lam 2–4 is not in the brief, but it may help chapter 8. It is sometimes
linked to an alternative alphabet order seen on early inscriptions. **That link is not
checked.** Open a source before it is used.

### Psalm 145 — not asked, but flagged
- The Hebrew runs aleph (v. 1, after the title) to tav (v. 21) **without nun**: mem at v. 13,
  samekh at v. 14.
- The nun line ("The LORD is faithful in all his words…") is in the LXX and in the Psalms
  Scroll from Qumran (11QPs^a). Many English versions print it as v. 13b.
- **I did not open the 11QPs^a or LXX text for this.** Check it before the chapter relies on
  it.

### Psalms 111 and 112
Both are acrostic by **half-line**, not by verse. My verse-start check is consistent with that
but does not confirm it line by line. If chapter 2 counts letters, check the half-lines
separately.
