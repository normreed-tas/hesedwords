## FLAG — item 1(b): the superlative fails

**What is true:**
- *Berakhot* 4b promises the world to come to whoever says *Tehillah le-David* "three times
  every day". It is the only psalm the Talmud singles out for that.
- Jewish daily prayer does this: twice in the morning service (in Pesukei deZimra, and again
  before Uva le-Tzion) and once to open the afternoon service. Source: Wikipedia, "Ashrei",
  which cites *Berakhot* 4b. I did not open a siddur.

**What is not demonstrable:** "more than any other psalm". There are three problems:
1. **Christian use.** The daily monastic and Anglican offices repeat other psalms every day:
   Ps 95 at the start of the office, and the Compline psalms. I did not open the Rule of
   Benedict or a breviary, so I make no counter-claim. But a league table across traditions
   cannot be won from one Talmud passage.
2. **Being read is not being said.** "Most *read*" is a different and still weaker claim.
   Popular reading would probably favour Ps 23.
3. **Not a ranking.** Wikipedia's Ashrei article makes no comparative claim either.

**The honest sentence, and probably the better opening:**

> There is one psalm that an old Jewish teaching tells you to say three times every day.
> People still do.

It is concrete, a named instruction, and true. **The working title *The Most Read Psalm* does
not survive.** What to call the book instead is Norm's decision.

## 1(a). *Berakhot* 4b — CONFIRMED

The text is the William Davidson Edition, Aramaic/Hebrew and English. Segment numbers are
Sefaria's.

**4b:15 (Hebrew).** *Kol ha-omer "Tehillah le-David" be-khol yom shalosh pe'amim — muvtach lo
she-hu ben ha-olam ha-ba.*

**English:** "Rabbi Elazar said that Rabbi Avina said: Anyone who recites: 'A Psalm of David'
… three times every day is assured of a place in the World-to-Come."

- **Attribution.** Confirmed as reported: Rabbi Elazar in the name of Rabbi Avina.
- **How the psalm is named.** The Talmud names it by its opening words, *Tehillah le-David*.
  The "(Psalms 145)" in the English is the translator's insertion.
- **The promise.** The Aramaic means "it is guaranteed to him that he is a son of the world to
  come".

**What the passage says next (4b:16–21).** The chapter should know this, because it is
better than anything that was planned:
- **Why this psalm?** The Talmud asks the question itself. *Because of the alphabet?* Then say
  Ps 119, which has it eightfold. *Because of v. 16, "You open your hand"?* Then say the
  Great Hallel (Ps 136), which says God gives bread to all flesh. The answer: because this
  psalm has **both** (*it has the two*).
- **Why no *nun*?** Rabbi Yohanan asks, "Why is there no *nun* in *Ashrei*?" His answer:
  because *nun* begins the verse on Israel's fall, Amos 5:2, "Fallen (*naflah*), no more to
  rise, is the virgin Israel."
- **Rav Nahman bar Yitzhak's addition.** Even so, David "went back and supported them". The
  very next line, at *samekh*, is "The LORD upholds all who fall" (v. 14).

**Consequences for chapter 1:**
- **Beat 1 and beat 7 are the same page.** The tradition that told people to say this psalm
  three times a day *also noticed the missing letter* and argued about it.
- **The Talmud's own answer is not "remembering".** It is "the alphabet *and* God feeding
  everyone". That supports beats 4 and 6.
- **The *samekh* reading is a gift for a twelve-year-old.** The letter for "fall" is missing,
  and the next line says God holds up everyone who falls. Present it as the rabbis' reading,
  not as the reason.

## 1(c). Superscription — CONFIRMED

I searched every occurrence of *tehillah* (Strong's 8416) in Psalms. It occurs about thirty
times, but only at **145:1** does it stand as the psalm's title. Everywhere else it is inside a
psalm's body: 9:14, 22:3, 33:1, 100:4, 147:1, 148:14, and others.

Two more points:
- **The Hebrew name of the book.** It is *Tehillim* (Sefaria's index title: תהילים), the plural
  of the same word.
- **Not asked, but usable.** Ps 145 opens *tehillah le-David* and its last verse opens
  *tehillat YHWH* ("the praise of the LORD my mouth will speak", v. 21). The psalm is bracketed
  by its own title word.

## 2. The missing *nun* line

### The readings

**Masoretic text.** Nothing between v. 13 (*mem*) and v. 14 (*samekh*).

**Qumran Psalms scroll (11QPs^a).** The transcription on Seforim Blog runs from the end of one
scroll line into the next:

> … *be-khol dor va-dor. Barukh YHWH u-varukh shemo le-olam va-ed.*
> **נאמן אלוהים בדבריו וחסיד בכול מעשיו** *Barukh YHWH u-varukh …*

The *nun* line reads ***Ne'eman Elohim bi-devarav ve-hasid be-khol ma'asav***: "Faithful is God
in his words, and *hasid* (loyal, kind) in all his works". Three details:
- **The divine name.** The line uses *Elohim*, not YHWH. (The blog prints it with *qof* for
  *he*, as a reverence spelling.)
- **The refrain.** Every line in the scroll's Ps 145 is followed by a refrain, "Blessed be the
  LORD and blessed be his name for ever and ever".
- **The reference.** I have not given a column/line number, because I did not open DJD IV.
  Get the column number from DJD IV before citing it.

**LXX (Ps 144:13a).** *pistos Kyrios en pasi tois logois autou kai hosios en pasi tois ergois
autou*, "The Lord is faithful in **all** his words, and holy in all his works" (Brenton).

**The witnesses differ from each other:**
- Qumran has *Elohim*; the LXX has *Kyrios*, which normally renders YHWH.
- Qumran has "in his words"; the LXX has "in **all** his words".

They are two separate witnesses to a *nun* line, not one copied from the other.

### The twin, v. 17 (Hebrew, MT)

| | First half | Second half |
|---|---|---|
| v. 17 | **צדיק יהוה בכל דרכיו** (*tsaddiq YHWH be-khol derakhav*) | **וחסיד בכל מעשיו** (*ve-hasid be-khol ma'asav*) |
| *nun* line (Qumran) | נאמן אלוהים בדבריו (*ne'eman Elohim bi-devarav*) | וחסיד בכול מעשיו (*ve-hasid be-khol ma'asav*) |

The second halves are **identical** apart from spelling (*bkwl*/*bkl*). The first halves have
the same shape (adjective + God + "in [all] his X") but different words.

### Haplography, tested

Haplography is a copyist's eye jumping between two similar stretches **next to each other**.
The *nun* line's twin is v. 17, four verses away, not its neighbour. The line before the gap
(v. 13b) ends *be-khol dor va-dor*; the line after (v. 14) begins *somekh*. Neither matches the
*nun* line. So a simple eye-skip does not explain the loss.

That is my reasoning from the text, not a source's verdict. The Seforim Blog author does
suggest the v. 17 match made it easy to drop. I think that is weak, for the reason just given.

The near-identity cuts **the other way**. A later scribe filling a gap would naturally build
the line out of v. 17. Wikipedia's "Psalm 145" article attributes this view to Kimelman, *JBL*
113 (1994) 50–51: the line may be a post-facto attempt to "cure" the deficiency. **I did not
open Kimelman.**

### Where the argument stands

Reported positions:
- **A very early copyist's loss.** Wikipedia calls this "the common supposition".
- **A later fill-in.** Kimelman, per Wikipedia. Commenters on the Seforim Blog cite Leiman.
- **Deliberate omission.** Rabbi Yohanan, *Berakhot* 4b, on theological grounds.

According to Wikipedia, the Targum, the Greek versions of Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion,
and the Talmud all lack the line. No position is dominant in what I read. **"Open" is the
honest word.**

### Does the scroll's order matter?

11QPs^a arranges the psalms in a different order from the Masoretic text, mixes in
non-canonical compositions, and gives Ps 145 a refrain on every line. That last point marks
the scroll's Ps 145 as a **liturgical** text, sung with a congregational response. A
liturgical copy has more reason to fill a gap in the alphabet. This lowers the scroll's
weight somewhat as evidence for the *original* psalm, but it does not remove it, because the
LXX independently has the line.

### Wording for a twelve-year-old

> Some very old copies have a line here: "God is faithful in his words and kind in all he
> does." Others don't. People have argued for two thousand years about whether it was lost or
> never there. Nobody knows.

All of that is true.

## 3. Psalms 111 and 112 — CONFIRMED, both clean

**Structure.** Each psalm has:
- 10 verses after *Hallelu-Yah*;
- verses 1–8 split at the *atnach* into two half-lines each (16 half-lines);
- verses 9 and 10 carrying three half-lines each (6).

That makes **22** in each psalm. The letter-openings by half-line are below. Both run
aleph–tav with **no gaps, no doubles, and nothing out of order.** Of this corpus, they are the

### Shared wording: the text's own, all checked in the Hebrew

| Ps 111 (of God) | Ps 112 (of the person) |
|---|---|
| 3b **וצדקתו עמדת לעד**, "his righteousness endures forever" | 3b **the identical clause**, and again at 9b |
| 4b **חנון ורחום** יהוה, "gracious and merciful is the LORD" | 4b **חנון ורחום** וצדיק, "gracious, merciful and righteous" (of the upright man in most readings) |
| 4a **זכר** עשה לנפלאתיו, "he has made a *remembrance* of his wonders" | 6b ל**זכר** עולם יהיה צדיק, "the righteous will be an everlasting *remembrance*" |
| 8a **סמוכים** לעד, "his precepts are *upheld* forever" | 8a **סמוך** לבו, "his heart is *upheld*" |
| 5a טרף **נתן** ליראיו, "he *gives* food to those who fear him" | 9a פזר **נתן** לאביונים, "he scatters, he *gives* to the poor" |
| 10a **יראת יהוה**, "the *fear of the LORD* is the beginning of wisdom" | 1a איש **ירא את יהוה**, "the man who *fears the LORD*" |
| 7a **משפט**, "justice" | 5b יכלכל דבריו ב**משפט**, "conducts his affairs with *justice*" |
| 1b **ישרים**, "the upright" | 2b, 4a **ישרים**, "the upright" |

Notes on the table:
- The *hannun ve-rachum* pair is the Exodus 34:6 attribute language. Elsewhere in Scripture it
  is used only of God, and Ps 112 gives it to a person.
- 112:4b has no subject in the Hebrew. Some read it of God.
- The 111:10a and 112:1a lines are the hinge between the two psalms: one ends where the other
  begins.

**The two endings diverge:**
- 111:10c *tehillato omedet la'ad*, "*his praise* endures forever";
- 112:10c *ta'avat resha'im tovēd*, "the desire of the wicked *will perish*".

The verb *omedet* ("endures") recurs: in 111 it is said of God's righteousness and God's
praise; in 112 of the person's righteousness, twice. The person's psalm ends instead on what
does *not* endure.

The chapter's point, "God's own words, said of the person he has shaped", is the text's,
**verbatim, in at least three places** (3b, 4b, 9b).

## 4. *kol*, *hesed*, *gedol-hesed* in Psalm 145 — CONFIRMED

**kol** (Strong's 3605) occurs **17 times**, at vv. 2, 9 ×2, 10, 13 ×2, 14 ×2, 15, 16, 17 ×2,
18 ×2, 20 ×2, 21. That is 17 in 153 words, about **11 per 100 words**.

Comparison by the same count:

| Psalm | *kol* | Words | Per 100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 144 | 0 | 130 | 0 |
| 146 | 1 | 85 | 1.2 |
| 147 | 2 | 142 | 1.4 |
| 103 (similar theme and length) | 9 | 167 | 5.4 |
| 33 | 7 | 161 | 4.3 |
| 111 | 4 | 74 | 5.4 |
| 136 (the "Great Hallel") | 1 | 166 | 0.6 |
| 119 | 28 | 1067 | 2.6 |

Ps 145 has roughly **double the density of the next-highest** in the sample. It is unusual.

*Corrected 1 Nov 2026, after a count across the whole Psalter: "double" holds only for the sample above. Ps 148 has 10 in 112 words (8.9 per 100), and Ps 117 has 2 in 17. Among psalms of its size, Ps 145 has the most, but not twice the next.*
- *kol* doubles up in the second half (vv. 13–20): "all who fall", "all who are bowed down",
  "the eyes of all", "every living thing", "all his ways", "all his works", "all who call",
  "all who love him".
- If the Qumran *nun* line is counted, it adds one more (*be-khol ma'asav*).

**hesed** (noun, 2617) occurs **once, at v. 8**, and nowhere else in the psalm.

**hasid** (2623) occurs at **v. 10** (*va-hasidekha*, "your faithful ones") and **v. 17**
(*ve-hasid*, of God). It also appears in the Qumran *nun* line.

So beat 6 has a clean shape. *hesed* is said once (v. 8). The word built from it then appears
twice: once of God's people (v. 10) and once of God (v. 17). And *kol* carries it out over
everything.

**gedol-hesed.** v. 8 reads **וּגְדָל־חָסֶד** (*u-gedol-hased*), "great in *hesed*", not the
*rav-hesed* ("abounding in *hesed*") of Exod 34:6.

In the Psalter, the formula's *hesed* clause appears as:

| Reading | Where |
|---|---|
| **rav-hesed** | **86:5, 86:15, 103:8** |
| **gedol-hesed** | **145:8 only** |

The search was every *hesed* immediately preceded by *gadol* or *rav*, across the whole
Psalter. So the variation is unique in the Psalms. I did not search outside the Psalter.
Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2 are worth checking if the chapter makes anything of this beyond the
Psalter.
