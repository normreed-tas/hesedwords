## The idea

Chapter and verse divisions are a finding aid, not a reading unit. Chapters are credited
to Stephen Langton, c. 1205; verses to Robert Estienne, 1551. Both post-date every book
they divide by more than a millennium. They are genuinely useful — nobody wants to lose
them — but the habit of reading *by* them means the seam sometimes falls where the
argument doesn't, and the reader never sees what was cut.

Norm's framing: demonstrate places where chapter breaks present as an obstacle to
understanding.

## Why it has warrant

A published worked case already exists. `articles/different-work.html` (Matthew
11:28 – 12:8) shows the offer of rest and the Sabbath controversy as one movement, split
by a number. That article does not argue the general point — it demonstrates it once. So
the method article inherits an exhibit rather than starting cold.

One sentence already written and available: *the chapters were added much later, for
finding things.* Useful, and not built for reading.

## Candidate cases

Discussed but not tested. Each would need the same close work *Different Work* got before
it could carry a section.

- **Matthew 11/12** — rest into Sabbath. Already published; the strongest case because the
  two halves answer each other directly (*all things have been handed over to me* →
  *lord of the Sabbath*).
- **Matthew 9:13 → 12:7** — not a chapter break, but the same disease. *Go and learn what
  this means* escalating to *if you had known what this means* is invisible if each
  pericope is read as self-contained. Worth including precisely because it shows the
  problem is the habit, not the punctuation.
- **Isaiah 52/53** — the servant song begins mid-52; the break puts the seam in the wrong
  place. `[verify]` — exact start point is disputed and the article would need to say so
  rather than assert one.
- **1 Corinthians 12/13/14** — chapter 13 reads as an interlude on love rather than as the
  answer to the argument it sits inside.
- **Genesis 1/2** — the break is doing theological work nobody chose. Highest risk entry:
  it lands in source-critical territory and could swallow the article.

## The main risk

This becomes a list. Fifteen examples would teach nothing; three or four shown properly
would teach the habit. The piece is a method article, so its output is a way of reading,
not a catalogue of seams.

Second risk: it reads as clever-at-the-expense-of-tradition. The article must be plain
that the divisions are useful and that the fault is in the reading, not the numbering.

## Where it stalled

No structure attempted. No Selah, and none obvious — a method article may not need one,
but the question hasn't been asked. Norm closed the session before the idea was developed
past the candidate list above.

## Loose ends

- Does this want to be an article or a resource-style piece? Its output is a reading
  practice, which is closer to a how-to than the site's usual register.
- Is there a Selah? Possibly in a moment of having preached a passage for years and then
  seeing what sat immediately before it. Not asked, not offered.
- The Estienne verse-division date (1551) is from the *Different Work* build check and can
  be reused. Verified 1 Sep 2026.
- The Langton date **is** a range, so the `[verify]` was well placed. 1205 is the date
  usually given, from his Paris teaching years, but a later date of c. 1227 near the end of
  his life is also argued, and sources concede difficulty in saying what he specifically
  did in any one year. An article should say *early thirteenth century* or *c. 1205, though
  the date is disputed* — not assert 1205 flat. `articles/different-work.html` note 2
  already says "generally credited to Stephen Langton... in the early thirteenth century",
  which covers the range and needs no change.
