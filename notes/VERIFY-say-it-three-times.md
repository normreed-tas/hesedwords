## FLAG A1 — my error in `VERIFY-acrostics.md`

**What is true.** Ps 9 runs aleph (vv. 1–2), bet (3), gimel (5), **[no dalet]**, he (6),
vav (7), zayin (11), het (13), tet (15), yod (17), kaf (18), in the normal order. Verse 4
opens with *ki* (kaf), outside the sequence. My table put the dalet row before gimel.

The table is now corrected, with a dated note. The psalm does nothing unusual here.
Chapter 7's line set (A, B, C, D-bracket, E…) was already right.

## FLAG C2 — CONFIRMED; the paragraph stays

- **Rashi** on Lam 5:22: because the book ends with reproof, "he had to repeat the preceding
  verse", to end on consolation.
- **Pardes Yosef** (on Lev 26:44): *ve-lakhen ha-minhag lachazor al ha-pasuq "Hashivenu"*,
  "so the custom is to repeat the verse 'Bring us back'". It names the four books treated
  this way, with a mnemonic: Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Isaiah and Malachi.
- **Tosafot** on *Berakhot* 31a refers to the practice of repeating *Hashivenu* after the last
  verse.
- **The debate is old.** The Yerushalmi commentators (cited in the Machon HaYerushalmi notes)
  debate whether the verse *should* be repeated. Some say it is unnecessary, because v. 22
  gives the reason for v. 21.

So "Jewish congregations reading it aloud go back and repeat the second-last verse", "for a
very long time", and "they did not change the text" all hold.

**Optional, if Norm wants it:** the same is done at the ends of Isaiah, Ecclesiastes and
Malachi. That would show the book this is a habit, not a one-off.

## FLAG E4 — the exercises

I read all nine asking only, "would a twelve-year-old know what to put on the page?"
**All nine say what to write about and to whom.** One small gap and one false premise:

- **Chapter 1, the premise.** It says: *"In Psalm 145 every line is said **to God**. Not about
  him to somebody else."* **That is not true.**
  - Several verses speak *about* God in the third person: v. 3 "Great is the LORD", vv. 8–9,
    v. 14, vv. 17–20, and v. 21.
  - The manuscript's own paraphrase shows it: line C, "Count **his** greatness"; line S has
    no "you"; the "watch where it goes" section says "finds **him** already close".
  - A careful reader will spot the contradiction on the same page.
  - **Suggested fix:** "Psalm 145 is mostly said **to God**, as if he is standing there. That
    is what you are doing too." The instruction itself ("one line for each, said to God")
    needs no change.
- **Chapter 8, a small gap.** "Pick as many letters as you want. Three, or eight, or all of
  them." It doesn't say *which* letters or in what order. Every other exercise starts at A
  or names the letters.
  - **Suggested fix:** "Start at A and go as far as you want: three letters, or eight, or all
    of them."

## B. Scripture and narrative

**B1. 1 Samuel 21 (Hebrew 21:11–16 = English 21:10–15). CONFIRMED.**
- David flees from Saul to **Achish, king of Gath** (21:11).
- The servants recognise him at once: "Is this not David, the king of the land?" (21:12).
- He changes his behaviour, "made marks on the doors of the gate, and let his spittle run
  down his beard" (21:14).
- Achish: "Do I lack madmen…?" (21:16).
- **Gath.** It is Goliath's city (1 Sam 17:4), so "the same people whose champion he had
  killed" holds.
- **One detail.** 1 Sam 21 does not say he was pushed out; it ends with Achish's question, and
  22:1 has David leaving. The *driving out* is from the **Ps 34 title**: *va-yegareshehu
  va-yelakh*, "who drove him out, and he went". Fine as it stands, since the chapter is
  telling the title's story.

**B2. CONFIRMED.** The Ps 34 title reads *lifnei Avimelekh*, "before Abimelech"; 1 Sam 21
says Achish. Keeping it out is defensible. If Norm wants it in, the usual explanation is
that Abimelech was a royal title among the Philistines (compare Gen 20 and 26). I did not
open a source for that.

**B3. CONFIRMED.**
- v. 8: he sits in ambush, his eyes watch for the helpless.
- v. 9: he lurks like a lion and drags the poor off in his net.
- v. 10: he crouches (*yashoach*).
- v. 11: *amar be-libbo: shakhach El, histir panav, bal ra'ah la-netsach*, "He says in his
  heart, God has forgotten, he has hidden his face, he will never see."

The speaker is the wicked man; it is "in his heart", the same formula as v. 6. The
manuscript's "covered his face… never going to look" is a fair paraphrase.

**B4. CONFIRMED.**
- v. 6: *rachamekha va-hasadekha*, "your mercies and your *hesed*" (plural).
- v. 7: *ke-hasdekha*, "according to your *hesed*".
- v. 10: *kol orchot YHWH hesed ve-emet*, "all the LORD's paths are *hesed* and *emet*".
  **Yes, v. 10 pairs it with *emet*.**

**B5. CONFIRMED.** *hesed* occurs **seven** times in Ps 119: vv. 41, 64, 76, 88, 124, 149,
159. It opens a verse only at **64** (*hasdekha*, in the het stanza, vv. 57–64) and **88**
(*ke-hasdekha*, in the kaf stanza, vv. 81–88). I also confirmed that every one of the 176
verses opens with its stanza's letter: no gaps, no slips.

**B6. CONFIRMED.**
- Counting the eight usual terms (*torah, edot, piqqudim, chuqqim, mitsvot, mishpatim,
  davar, imrah*), only vv. 3, 37, 90 and 122 lack one.
- Adding *derekh* / *orach* ("way", "path"), which the chapter itself lists ("His ways"),
  **only v. 122** lacks one.

The sentence stands as written.

**B7. CONFIRMED, with one note.** *Hasdei YHWH ki lo tamnu, ki lo khalu rachamav;
chadashim la-beqarim, rabbah emunatekha.* *hesed* is the word (plural, "the LORD's acts of
*hesed*"), and "new every morning" and "great is your faithfulness" are there.

**The note:** the Hebrew text as it stands reads *tamnu*, "**we** are not consumed" (KJV:
"It is of the LORD's mercies that **we** are not consumed"). "**His kindness** never runs
out" follows the ancient versions and a one-letter change (*tammu*), which ESV and most modern
versions adopt. The sense the chapter needs is safe; the next clause says his mercies do not
end. But the manuscript's first sentence follows the modern versions, not the Hebrew as it
stands. No change needed in a paraphrase. Noted in case anyone compares it with a KJV.

**"In the middle of it" (not in the brief).** The chapter says Lam 3:22–23 is "in the middle"
of chapter 3 and of the book. It is not:
- **Where it really falls:** it is the **eighth** of 22 stanzas (het, vv. 22–24), about a
  third of the way through.
- **The real middles:** the middle of chapter 3 is v. 33; the middle of the whole book is
  also 3:33.
- **What is true:** the most organised poem sits in the middle of the five.

**Suggested fix:** "And here is what is inside it", or "partway through it".

**B8. CONFIRMED** against `VERIFY-acrostics-2.md` item 3.
- "Lasts forever": 111:3b = 112:3b, the identical clause.
- "Gives" (*natan*): 111:5, 112:9.
- "Held up" (*samakh*): 111:8, 112:8.
- "Remembered" (*zekher*): 111:4, 112:6.
- "Fair" (*mishpat*): 111:7, 112:5.

All five rows are right.

I also checked the sense of every printed line against the verse its letter opens, in Pss

**E2 / E3. The count of holes is inconsistent.**
- **Chapter 7** ends: "That is **five psalms** now with something missing." That counts
  145, 34, 37, 25, and **9–10 as one**.
- **Chapter 9**: "**Six of the nine** have holes in them." That counts 9 and 10
  **separately**: 145, 34, 37, 25, 9 (dalet), 10 (mem–tsadi). That is correct as a count of
  the nine numbered psalms, and the "141 remaining" sum also counts them separately.
- **The problem.** A reader who has just been told 9 and 10 are one poem will count five.
- **Suggested fix** (ch 9): "Six of the nine have holes in them — or five, if you count
  Psalms 9 and 10 as the one poem they really are."

**E5. Other claims found in the read-through (not in the brief).**

1. **Missing *vav*: "nobody has a theory" is wrong.** Ch 3 says "Here, nobody does. It is
   missing, and no one has ever explained it." Ch 9 says "Psalm 34 has no *vav*, and nobody
   has a theory at all."
   - **What is true.** Scholars have argued the opposite, that the omission is **deliberate**.
     The page I read cites Freedman ("Patterns in Psalms 25 and 34", 1992), Skehan (*CBQ*
     1951), Ceresko (*VT* 1985) and Eriksson (1991).
   - **The reasoning.** Drop *vav* and add the extra *pe* line at the end, and the 22 lines
     come out with **lamed exactly in the middle**. The first, middle and last lines then
     begin **aleph, lamed, pe**, which spells *aleph*: the name of the first letter, and a
     verb meaning "**to learn, to teach**".
   - **The text fits.** I checked: Ps 34's lamed line, its exact middle (v. 11 in English),
     is "**Come, children, listen to me; I will teach you** the fear of the LORD". The
     manuscript's own line L reads "**Listen, kids. Come here. I will teach you**…".
     Ps 25 has the same frame: aleph first, lamed in the middle (v. 11), pe last.
   - **Status.** It is a theory, not a settled fact. But "nobody has a theory" is false.
   - **The better beat for a twelve-year-old.** Two psalms leave out the same letter and add
     the same extra letter at the end. When you do that, the first, middle and last letters
     spell the Hebrew word for "learn", and the middle line of Ps 34 is "Come, children, I
     will teach you."
   - **What it changes:** ch 3's "Another bracket", ch 5's "One more line, after the end",
     and ch 9's list. **This is Norm's call, and it is a real change.** Even if he doesn't
     want the beat, the "nobody has a theory" sentences must go.
2. **"About two thousand years" of argument** (ch 1 and ch 9).
   - **What is true.** The earliest named voice in the argument is Rabbi Yohanan (3rd century
     AD), about 1,750 years ago. The Greek line itself is older, but it is not part of an
     argument.
   - **Suggested fix:** "for more than fifteen hundred years", or "since ancient times".
3. **"About twice as often as any psalm its size"** (ch 1).
   - **What is true.** The *kol* count was true for the sample in `VERIFY-acrostics-2.md`,
     but not across the whole Psalter: **Ps 148** has 10 in 112 words. Among psalms of its
     size, Ps 145 has the most *kol*, 17 against Ps 148's 10, but not twice the next. My
     earlier note overstated it and is now corrected.
   - **Suggested fix:** "That is more than any other psalm its size."
4. **"Nobody went back and fixed any of it"** (ch 9).
   - **The problem.** This sits awkwardly with ch 1, which told the reader that "some very old
     copies do have a line" at *nun*. That line may well be exactly such a fix.
   - **Suggested fix:** "The Hebrew copies were made by hand, carefully, for hundreds of years,
     holes and all, by people who could easily have tidied them up. They didn't."
5. **Ch 2: "only these two and one other get all the way through without a gap."**
   - **The problem.** This is true of the **psalms** (111, 112, 119). It is not true of the
     book's alphabet poems, since Lam 1 is also clean, and Lam 2–4 have every letter, just
     two swapped.
   - **Suggested fix:** "Out of all the alphabet **psalms** in this book…"
6. **Ch 8: "Chapters 1, 2, 3 and 4 are all alphabet poems. Twenty-two verses each."**
   - **The problem.** Lam 3 has 66 verses, as the chapter says four paragraphs later.
   - **Suggested fix:** "Twenty-two **letters** each, A to Z."
7. **Ch 5: "From the first line to the last it is a person asking."**
   - **The problem.** vv. 8–10 and 12–14 talk about God rather than ask him; the manuscript's
     lines I, J, K, M, N and O show it.
   - **Suggested fix:** "Most of it is a person asking." Minor; Norm's call.
8. **Ch 7: "Nobody answers it. Not in Psalm 10, and not anywhere else in the book of
   Psalms."** That is a theological reading, not a checkable fact. Leave it if Norm owns it.

## Addendum, 1 November 2026: the missing-*vav* theory (E5.1), checked further

Norm asked for this to be verified before the book takes it.

### Sources

**Opened.** R. Benun, "Evil and the Disruption of Order: A Structural Analysis of the
Acrostics in the First Book of Psalms", *Journal of Hebrew Scriptures* 6 (2006), article 5,
open access, full text read (https://doi.org/10.5508/jhs.2006.v6.a5).

**Not reachable.** Freedman's "Patterns in Psalms 25 and 34" (1992) is not open anywhere I
could find. Its reprint, *Divine Commitment and Human Obligation*, is held by the Internet
Archive only as a restricted lending copy. Skehan, Ceresko and Eriksson were not opened.

### What is now CONFIRMED, and by what

1. **The two psalms are designed as a pair.**
   - *Text, checked.* Both lack *vav*. Both add a final *pe* line built on *padah*, "redeem".
   - *Benun, opened.* He treats the gaps as intentional and the pair as one unit. Ps 34
     answers Ps 25 letter for letter: ayin 25:15 / 34:16 ("my eyes" / "the eyes of the
     LORD"); pe; tsadi; resh; shin; and the final *pe* lines.
   - *Benun, citing Delitzsch.* Delitzsch noticed the parallel structure in the 19th century.
   - *Benun, note 447.* The added *pe* line makes up for the missing one, so each poem still
     has 22 lines.
2. **The teaching line sits at the centre of Ps 34.**
   - *Benun, opened, note 41.* The words of 34:12 (Hebrew; 34:11 in English), "Come, children,
     listen to me; I will teach you the fear of the LORD", are "exactly the center words of
     Ps 34", testifying to the psalm's purpose as "a didactic statement".
   - *Checked, by line count.* The lamed line is line 11 of 22.
3. **The spelling.** Anyone can check this from the text; it does not depend on any scholar.
   - In **both psalms** the first line begins with **aleph**, the lamed line falls at the
     centre (Ps 25:11, Ps 34:11 English), and the extra last line begins with **pe**. *Text,
     checked* (`VERIFY-acrostics.md`).
   - Aleph, lamed, pe (א־ל־ף) spell ***aleph***, the name of the first letter.
   - **The same three letters are a Hebrew verb, "to learn / to teach".** I checked all four
     occurrences in the WLC:
     - Job 33:33, *va-a'allefkha chokhmah*, "and I will **teach** you wisdom";
     - Job 15:5;
     - Job 35:11, "who **teaches** us";
     - Prov 22:25, "lest you **learn** his ways".

### CORRECTED in my own earlier wording

I wrote that the 22 lines come out with "lamed **exactly** in the middle". By line count
that is not exact. With 22 lines there is no single middle line. In Ps 34, lamed is line 11,
with 10 lines before it and 11 after.

The "exact middle" in the literature (Skehan, as reported) is the middle of the **letter
series** aleph–tav plus *pe*: 23 letters, with lamed the 12th. Benun's "exact centre" is by
**word count**.

**For the book, say "at the centre" or "in the middle", not "exactly in the middle".**

### Still a theory

The **intention** remains inference: that the poet dropped *vav* and added *pe* *in order to*
spell *aleph* / "learn". The facts underneath it are not in doubt:
- the pattern;
- the spelling;
- the verb;
- the teaching line at the centre.

I found no source that rejects it, but I did not see the scholarly debate either.

**Wording that is true as it stands:**

> The poem starts at A. It goes to the end, and then adds one more line on a letter that has
> already been used. Right in the middle is the line "Come, children, listen to me; I will
> teach you." Take the first letter, the middle letter and the last one — in Hebrew they are
> aleph, lamed and pe — and they spell *aleph*: the name of the first letter of the alphabet,
> and a word that means **learn**. Psalm 25 does exactly the same. Some people think that
> was the plan all along. Nobody can prove it. But it is there on the page.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED as a pattern in the text, with a scholarly reading behind it (Benun,
opened; Freedman, Skehan, Ceresko, reported). Safe to use if it is presented as "some people
think", which the chapter's own manner already does.**

### A practical note for the English scheme

The book's alphabet maps *pe* to **R**. So in the English the extra last line would be an R
line, or it stays letterless, as now. The spelling only works in Hebrew, so the chapter has to
say the Hebrew letters out loud. It already teaches *nun*, *vav* and *ayin* by name, so that
is in keeping.
