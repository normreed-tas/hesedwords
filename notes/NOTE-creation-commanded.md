# NOTE — Creation commanded to praise

**Held.** No Selah, and the central claim is not yet safe to make.

Arose 10 September 2026 from the Psalm 98 morning that produced *The Asking*,
*What God Remembers* and the *zakar* word study. Deliberately kept out of all three.

## The observation

Psalm 98 is almost entirely imperative, and past verse 6 the imperatives are addressed
to things that cannot obey.

- v. 4 — make a joyful noise to the LORD, **all the earth**
- v. 7 — let the **sea** roar, and all that fills it; the world and those who dwell in it
- v. 8 — let the **rivers** clap their hands; let the **hills** sing for joy together

Commanding Israel to sing means something plain. Commanding a river to clap does not.
Either it is figure — the psalm reaching for scale — or it is a claim about what creation
already is and does.

The psalm gives no signal which, and that is the problem.

## Why it may not be figure

The construction is not isolated. It recurs wherever God's arrival is in view:

- Psalm 96:11–12 — let the heavens be glad, let the earth rejoice, let the sea roar,
  let the field exult, let the trees of the forest sing for joy — **before the LORD, for
  he comes** (same closing formula as 98:9)
- Isaiah 55:12 — the mountains and hills break forth into singing, the trees of the field
  clap their hands
- Isaiah 44:23, 49:13 — sing, O heavens; break forth into singing, O mountains
- Psalm 148 — the whole address is to sun, moon, sea creatures, fire, hail, mountains,
  fruit trees, before it reaches people at v. 11

*Warrant: Established* — the pattern exists and is consistent.

What is consistent is the trigger. In 98 and 96 the command to creation is bolted to the
same clause: **he comes to judge the earth.** The praise is not a general mood. It is a
response to an arrival.

*Warrant: Reading-within-frame* — that the trigger is the arrival rather than the
psalm's general exuberance. Defensible from the shared v. 9 formula in 96 and 98; not
stated by either psalm.

## Where it stalls

To make this a piece I would have to say what the imperative means, and I cannot yet
without reaching. Three options, none settled:

1. **Creation already responds; we are the slow ones.** The command is not instruction
   but summons — calling out what is already the case. Attractive, and it fits Psalm 19
   (the heavens declare, without speech). But it risks a warmth the text does not supply.
2. **The imperative is anticipatory.** Creation will do this when he comes, and the psalm
   speaks it ahead of time. Fits the future-tense judging of v. 9.
3. **It is figure, doing work of scale.** The psalm needs the whole earth in the chorus
   and personification is how it gets there. Least interesting; hardest to disprove.

I do not want to pick one for the sake of having a piece.

## Loose ends

- **Romans 8:19–22** is the obvious New Testament join — creation groaning, waiting,
  subjected in hope. Not yet worked. If the groaning and the clapping are the same
  creature at two points in the same story, that is the piece. If the connection is only
  thematic, it is not.
- **Judgement as good news for creation.** Both 96 and 98 have creation rejoicing
  *because* he comes to judge. That inverts the usual reading of judgement, and it may be
  the real subject of any eventual piece rather than the personification itself.
- **Psalm 148's order** — creation first, kings and peoples last. Worth checking whether
  that ordering is deliberate and whether it recurs.
- ~~**Hebrew check needed:** whether the verbs in 98:7–8 are true imperatives or
  jussives.~~ **Checked 10 Sep 2026** against STEPBible's lemma-tagged text. The psalm
  distinguishes them, and the seam falls exactly where this note locates the problem:
  - **v. 4**, to *all the earth* — `Vhv2mp`, `Vqv2mp`, `Vpv2mp`. True imperatives,
    second person plural. Shout, break forth, sing, make music.
  - **v. 7**, the sea — `Vqi3ms`. Third person. *Let it thunder.*
  - **v. 8**, rivers and hills — `Vqi3mp`, `Vpi3mp`. Third person. *Let them clap, let
    them sing for joy.*

  So the psalm never commands a river in the second person. It shifts to third-person
  jussive at precisely the point where the addressee stops being able to obey. That is a
  grammatical seam, not a slide into figure — and it means the "commanding a river to
  clap" framing at the head of this note is slightly too strong. The psalm does not do
  that.

  Bearing on the three readings: it weakens (1) as stated, since a jussive is not an
  instruction to the thing itself; it is neutral to (2); and it complicates (3), because
  pure figure has no reason to change person. The seam is now the most concrete thing in
  the note, and may be where a piece starts rather than the personification.
