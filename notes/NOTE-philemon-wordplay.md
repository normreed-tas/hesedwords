# Philemon — two puns, four verses apart — held note

**Status: held.** Opened 21 September 2026 out of the verification work for the article
*Knowing Where He Came From*, which quotes Philemon 10–11. The article stays as written:
the second pun is not in it, and neither is the Χριστός question below.

---

## The observation

Paul plays on the slave's name twice in one short letter, and the two plays work
differently. The famous one is not the one Paul makes most directly.

**Verse 11 — the pun on what the name means.** Ὀνήσιμος means *useful*. Paul writes
τόν ποτέ σοι **ἄχρηστον** νυνὶ δὲ σοὶ καὶ ἐμοὶ **εὔχρηστον** — formerly useless to you,
now useful to you and to me. Both accusative singular masculine, from ἄχρηστος and
εὔχρηστος. The joke is on the *sense* of the name, and it is made with a different root
entirely (χρη-, not ὀνη-).

**Verse 20 — the pun on the name itself.** ναί, ἀδελφέ, ἐγώ σου **ὀναίμην** ἐν κυρίῳ —
let me have benefit of you in the Lord. ὀναίμην is from **ὀνίνημι**, which is the root
Ὀνήσιμος is formed from. This is the direct play, and it is the one usually passed over.

**Verse 10 — what he calls him.** περὶ τοῦ ἐμοῦ τέκνου, **ὃν ἐγέννησα** ἐν τοῖς δεσμοῖς
— my child, *whom I fathered*, first aorist active of γεννάω. Not taught, not helped.
Fathered, in chains, with nothing to give.

## Why it is a piece and not a footnote

Because the three do different work and they are in order.

1. **v10** establishes the relation — and it is a *father* relation, formed by a man with
   no standing, no freedom and nothing to offer.
2. **v11** is about the man's usefulness to his owner. Outward, economic, the category
   Philemon already has for him.
3. **v20** turns the same root back on Philemon: *let me have benefit of you*. Paul asks
   for from the master exactly what the master lost in the slave.

So the letter takes a name that means *useful*, denies the usefulness, restores it, and
then asks the owner to become it. That is a shape, and it is available to anyone reading
in English except for the v20 link, which only shows in Greek.

## The Χριστός question — deliberately not taken

Some hear an echo of **Χριστός** in **ἄχρηστος**, on the grounds that χρηστός and Χριστός
were homophones in Koine.

**The phonetic fact is very well attested.** Suetonius writes *impulsore Chresto*;
Tacitus' manuscripts read *Chrestianos*; Tertullian remarks on the confusion directly in
the *Apology*; Justin plays on it. That much is not in doubt.

**That Paul intends it here is not attested, and is a minority reading.** It requires
ἄχρηστον to carry *without Christ* alongside *useless*, in a sentence whose evident joke
is already the name. Norm's decision, 21 September 2026: **it stays out.** Recorded here
so the same question is not reopened as though it were new, and so the reason is on
file — the sound change is real, the authorial intention is not established, and the
piece does not need it.

## Loose ends

- Whether ὀνίνημι appears anywhere else in the New Testament. **Not checked.** If v20 is
  its only occurrence that is worth knowing before drafting.
- Where Onesimus goes after the letter, and whether the Ignatius connection (a bishop of
  Ephesus of that name, early second century) is worth a paragraph or is a distraction.
  Almost certainly a distraction, but it will be asked.
- The relation to the existing site material on Onesimus — the name is the foundation's
  name, and *Knowing Where He Came From* already tells the 2014 notebook story. A piece
  on the wordplay must not re-tell it. Material does not migrate between companions.
- Whether this is an article or a word study on ὀνίνημι / χρηστός. Leaning word study,
  because the finding is lexical, but the letter's shape is the interesting part and a
  word study may be too narrow a container.
- The slavery question. Any piece on Philemon has to say something about Paul returning
  a slave, and this note does not attempt it. That is a decision, not a gap to be filled
  quietly while writing about vocabulary.
