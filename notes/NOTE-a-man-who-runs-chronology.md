# A Man Who Runs — working chronology

**Status: the book is published and this document is behind it, not ahead of it.**
Filed 18 Sep 2026. It was written as a check against the dates and ages the text asserts,
and it did its job: it found three internal contradictions, all three of which were
corrected before publication. It is kept because the book makes dated claims a reader may
want to test, and this shows the working — what is established, what is tradition, what is
inference, and what is simply chosen.

**The three problems below are fixed.** Read them as a record of what was caught, not as
faults still in the text. Verified against the published page on 18 Sep 2026:

| Flagged | State in the published book |
|---|---|
| "eleven or twelve" at the first visit | **Fixed.** The word *eleven* appears nowhere. It reads *fourteen* at both points and *seventeen* in the garden. |
| "thirteen years" since Perga | **Fixed.** The number is gone; the sentence carries it without arithmetic. |
| "forty years" at chapters 19 and 22 | **Fixed.** Both read *thirty years*. |

The surviving *forty* is a count of readings, not years — *"I had read it aloud to myself
perhaps forty times"* — and is correct. The *thirty years* this document says to leave
alone, Peter telling it and still laughing at the dust, is untouched.

## Three internal problems in the present draft

**1. He cannot be eleven.** The text has him eleven or twelve at Jesus' first
visit and seventeen in the garden — six years. The ministry runs about three.
Either he meets Jesus before the public ministry begins, or the ages are wrong.
Cleanest fix: **fourteen** at the first visit, which keeps him a boy at the fig
tree and seventeen in the garden. Three occurrences: chapters ten, eleven and
seventeen ("since I was eleven years old").

**2. "Thirteen years" since Perga is wrong.** Perga 47 to Rome 66 is nineteen
years. Chapter twenty-one says thirteen. Fix to **nineteen**, or drop the number
— "neither of us had said that word out loud since the day it happened" carries
it without arithmetic.

**3. "Thirty years" and "forty years" disagree.** The garden to this conversation
is thirty-three years. The garden to Peter's death is thirty-two. So:
- "I told nobody for thirty years" — fine, round.
- "written it down thirty years ago" — fine.
- "Forty years" at the end of chapter nineteen — too long. **Thirty years.**
- "Peter never asked me a question in forty years" (chapter twenty-two) — same. **Thirty years.**
- Peter "telling it for thirty years and he still laughed at the dust" (chapter three) — correct as is.

## Two things the draft gets right and should not be adjusted

**Mark's age.** Fifty is the right age for this book. Old enough to have spent a
working life at it, young enough to outlive Paul and finish the Gospel.

**The Gospel's date.** The story needs Mark still writing in 66 — Peter dead,
Paul alive. Much scholarship puts composition at 65–75, which brackets this but
mostly sits later. The early end is a defensible minority position: Papias has
Mark writing from Peter's preaching, and the ancient sources disagree over
whether that was before or after Peter died. **[verify]** This is a choice, not
an error, and it is load-bearing. It should be made knowingly and not defended
in the text.

## One thing to leave alone

Paul's *"I do not know"* in chapter twenty-four. Both reviews flagged it as the
line that keeps him from becoming the man who understood it. It stays exactly
as written.

## The two `[verify]` marks — Claude Code, 18 Sep 2026

### Peter's death — the range is right, and 65 is defensible

Correctly marked here as tradition rather than text. **Nothing in Scripture dates it.**

The earliest non-canonical witness is **1 Clement**, c. 95 — within one generation, and
the only first-century document outside the New Testament attesting the deaths of Peter and
Paul. It reports the martyrdom and gives **no date**: *"Peter, because of unjust envy,
endured not one or two but many fatigues, and thus, with martyrdom, he arrived at the place
of glory."*

The date comes from fitting that to **Tacitus**, whose *Annals* place the Neronian
persecution in **October 64**. The argument for the early end is negative and reasonably
strong: between late 64 and Nero's death on 9 June 68 there is no other period of
anti-Christian persecution of the kind Tacitus and Clement describe.

So **64–68 is the honest range**, the weight sits at its early end, and the **65 assumed
here is inside it**. It is a choice among defensible options, not a fact, and the table was
right to mark it.

### The Gospel's date — the disagreement is real, and the book sits between the two

The claim that the ancient sources disagree is **exactly right**, and both are worth having
by name:

- **Irenaeus**, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1 — *"after the death of these"* (Peter and Paul),
  Mark transmitted in writing what Peter preached. Most read *departure* as death. So:
  **after both.**
- **Clement of Alexandria**, preserved in Eusebius — Mark wrote **while Peter was alive**,
  at the request of those who heard him preach in Rome, and when Peter learned of it he
  *"neither hindered nor encouraged it."*

**The book's position is neither.** It has Peter dead and Paul alive, which is later than
Clement and earlier than Irenaeus. That is not a weakness: it is close to the standard
reconciliation of the two, that Mark began the work in Peter's last years and finished it
after his death. But it should be understood as **its own position**, not as following
Irenaeus.

Which strengthens rather than weakens what this document already says: *it is a choice, not
an error, it is load-bearing, and it should be made knowingly and not defended in the text.*
That remains the right instruction. The book does not argue for its date, and should not
start.
