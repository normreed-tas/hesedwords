# FUTURE-ARTICLES.md

**Standing register — reviewed regularly, not a queue.**

This file lists article and reflection candidates that have been identified but not
written. It exists because ideas surface mid-session, get discussed properly, and then
vanish when the conversation closes. Most entries are one paragraph plus a link to their
own `-NOTE.md` where the thinking is longer. An entry may instead hold its thinking here
as bullets while it is still short enough to fit — it earns a note file once it grows
findings, loose ends, and a record of where it stalled. Do not "tidy" a bulleted entry
into a paragraph; the bullets are the content.

Nothing here is a commitment. An entry earns removal two ways: it becomes a piece, or a
review concludes it was never a piece and it is struck out with a line saying why.

**How to use this in a drafting session:** paste or fetch this file at the start of any
session about what to write next. Do not treat an entry as approved structure — the
notes record where the thinking stopped, not where it should resume.

**Review cadence:** every six to eight weeks, alongside the protocol review.

**Last reviewed: 6 September 2026.** Bump this line at each review.
`node tools/check-doc-dates.js` warns once it is more than eight weeks old, so an
overdue review surfaces on the next commit rather than depending on anyone noticing.
Re-paste this file into the drafting project whenever an entry is added or struck,
or the copy in context stops matching the register.

## Open candidates

### Reading Scripture by chapters
*Added 1 Sep 2026. Detail: `NOTE-chapter-divisions.md`*

The chapter and verse divisions are a thirteenth- and sixteenth-century finding aid that
has become an unexamined reading unit, and in several places the seam falls where the
argument doesn't. Not an argument against the numbers — an argument against reading *by*
them. Has a published worked case already in `articles/different-work.html`, which
demonstrates the Matthew 11/12 break hiding a single movement. Main risk is becoming a
list of examples; three or four cases shown properly will teach the habit where fifteen
will teach nothing.

### Glory as fullness — the invitation, and what glorifying God looks like
*Added 6 Sep 2026. Held. No Selah yet. No note file yet — the bullets below are the whole
of it, and it becomes a `-NOTE.md` of its own if it grows findings, loose ends, and a
record of where it stalled.*

- Glory is weight (*kavod*) and fullness — the same measure from two sides. Nothing
  lacking, so nothing can be added.
- John 17: the glory Jesus had with the Father before the world. Never held alone. The
  prayer asks not that we be given glory but that we be brought inside the one already
  there.
- Exodus 33 is not a refusal. Face to face as a man speaks to his friend, but the seeing
  Moses asked for would have killed him. The cleft and the hand are concession, not
  withholding. The Incarnation answers it — "we beheld his glory," the face in a form
  that doesn't kill.
- Fullness answers the objection that a relational glory makes God dependent. The
  invitation comes out of having, not lacking.
- Glorifying God can't mean increase. It can only mean disclosure — the Son makes the
  Father visible by doing what the Father is doing. "I have glorified you, having
  finished the work you gave me to do."
- Three strands, kept distinct: **sent** (work not self-chosen), **pleasing** (delight
  declared at the baptism, before any public work — so not performance), **one** (so the
  visibility is accurate).
- Test the definition at the cross before it hardens. If disclosure holds there, it holds.
- Isaiah 6 — "the whole earth is full of his glory," and John says Isaiah saw his glory.
  Fullness and lifting up in the same passage.

**Sub-section — Mark 10:35–40**

- The brothers ask for right and left "in your glory." Mark supplies the answer in 15:27:
  two crucified, one on his right, one on his left. He doesn't explain it.
- Cup and baptism both already point at the passion before glory is mentioned.
- Complication: Mark's *coming in glory* language elsewhere (13, 14:62) is the parousia,
  not the cross. The irony may be the point rather than a problem.
- The Father's appointing then reads as *this is not what you think you're asking for*.
- **[verify]** Greek of Mark 15:27 against 10:37 before drafting.
