A Hesed Words Reflection

In the Midst of the Temple

Psalm 48:9–11

· · ·
Norm Reed

The psalm builds the picture first. Jerusalem. The city of God. The mountain, beautiful in elevation, the joy of the whole earth. The refuge, the palaces, the sight of it that makes the kings assemble and marvel and hastily retreat. All of it real, all of it present, all of it worth singing about.

And then Selah. Stop. Sit with what you have just heard.

I have learned something new about what that word does. I have always understood the Selah as a pause on what came before, a moment to let the previous words settle. And that is true. But there is more. The Selah is also a breathing space that opens toward what comes next. It is the hinge. The pregnant poise where the question becomes possible because you have stopped long enough to ask it.

When the pause ends, the question surfaces. Not what does the city look like? but what is it for? Why does this place matter?

We have thought on your steadfast love, O God, in the midst of your temple.

There it is. The hesed. The covenant faithfulness. Not the walls. Not the beauty, though that is real. The reason the city stands. The reason it matters to the ends of the earth. Because God’s steadfast love dwells there. Because his righteousness fills his right hand. Because in that place, his very character, his loyalty, his refusal to abandon, his faithfulness across generations, becomes visible.

I have read long enough now to see this thread everywhere. Hesed. It surfaces again and again in every psalm, in Micah, in Hosea, woven through the whole testimony of Scripture. The steadfast love of God is not a doctrine to be believed. It is the foundation on which everything stands. It is what God is.

And then the question arrives, quietly but with weight. If that is what Zion is built on, if that is what the nations see when they look at the city of God, then what about me? Does my life exude it? Does the person who has been kept, who has been held, who has received this steadfast love again and again, does that person show it? In how I sit with the fractured families, in how I maintain presence when the system grinds, in how I move through the world?

But the question is larger still. We are the temple. The church is the living stones. What about the church? Does it exude hesed? Does it show God’s faithfulness?

I think of the years at Risdon Vale. A church that opened its doors. A church that made space for the prisoner and the child of the prisoner, for the correctional officer and the person they supervised, for the ones the system had marked as Other. A church that attempted—imperfectly, incompletely, but genuinely—to be a place where God’s steadfast love became visible. Where the fractured family was held. Where the excluded were brought into the same room. That was what we were trying to do. That is what the church should be.

The knowing of God and the living of that knowing, they are not the same thing. And I do not yet know how to close the distance between them. But I am learning to sit in the question. To let the Selah do its work, not rushing toward resolution, but pausing long enough to feel what the difference might ask of me. That pregnant poise is where the real work begins.

What about the church at large? It is sad that the church is often known for what it is against rather than what it is for. What does it mean for the church to exude the hesed of God in a world that desperately needs to know it? In a society where the fractured and the excluded are still waiting to be brought in from the cold?

The question remains open. And in the asking, something shifts.
