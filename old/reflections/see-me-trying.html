See Me Trying

A reflection on Nehemiah's prayer

It was early morning, still dark, and I was lying in bed trying to coax myself back to sleep. The mind, though, had other ideas. It drifted to the church I had handed on, to the years of stewardship, the choices made, the things given. And a small thought surfaced, unbidden: the long service leave I had forgone, the thousands of dollars that could have been mine. Why did I do that?

Not criticism of what comes next. Not regret. Just the honest human question that surfaces in the dark when you're not defending anything. A genuine not-knowing. My wife and I had made the choice freely, gladly even, to leave the church unburdened, to not lay that weight on it as we handed it on. I had wanted to do it. But lying there in the half-light, the question arrived fresh: What was I asking for in that giving?

I had written about Nehemiah before, about the way his final prayer seemed to ask God to remember his deeds, to account for his faithfulness. I had read it as a kind of ledger-keeping, a man at the end asking for credit. But this morning, moving through chapters 4, 5, and 6, I found the phrase again. Remember for my good, O my God, all that I have done for this people. And I stopped.

It wasn't only at the end. The prayer returns throughout the book — four times the phrase appears1 — a refrain beneath the whole of his work, not something added as an afterthought, but something that had shaped how he moved through each day of the restoration.

And as I sat with it, something turned over in me. I had been lying in bed an hour earlier, asking myself the same question he was asking God. Not in those words. But the same prayer, nonetheless.

And then it hit me. Not gently. Like recognition arriving all at once.

I had spent that morning, lying in the dark, essentially praying Nehemiah's prayer. See what I've done. See that I cared. See that it cost me something. Not asking for payment. Not demanding God owe me gratitude. But asking, simply, to be seen.

The long service leave I had forgone — that was my offering. My wife and I had made the choice because we wanted the church unburdened as we handed it on. We wanted to care for it, not burden it. And in that caring, in that refusal, I was saying something to God that I had not quite known I was saying until Nehemiah's words found me in the text.

Remember me. Not because I deserve reward. Because I wanted to give this. Because it mattered to me. Because I love these people. See that I tried.

It was worship. I understood that only as I read his prayer and recognised my own.

What I had misread in Nehemiah before was precisely this: I had mistaken the prayer for pride, for a man keeping ledgers, demanding God account for his deeds. But the prayer is something altogether different. It is the vulnerability of worship itself.

Think of a child drawing in crayon — wobbly lines, colours outside the boundaries, nothing that would hang in a gallery. The child carries it to their father and says, "Daddy, I made this for you." The father doesn't examine it for technical skill. He sees the love in the offering. He sees that his child wanted to make something, wanted to give it, wanted to be seen in the giving.

That's Nehemiah. That's what I was doing in the dark that morning.

Not asking for payment. Asking to be known. Asking God to see that the stewardship came from a real place, that the refusal of comfort came from love for the people, that the offering was worship.

And here is what I have come to understand: this longing is not sin. It is not pride masquerading as humility. It is one of the deepest human needs — to be known by the one we love, to have our love witnessed. C.S. Lewis wrote about this in The Weight of Glory — how we spend our lives chasing smaller recognitions, the regard of people who matter to us, because we are trying to fill a longing that only God can satisfy. The longing to be liked by God, not merely forgiven by him. To be known.

Nehemiah prayed that prayer throughout his life because he understood something true: that service to God is not service in the dark. It is service before God. And before God means seen by God.

I had misjudged Nehemiah. Not in seeing the complexity of his prayer, but in missing what lay beneath it. I thought I was reading the prayer of a man asking God to settle accounts. I was actually reading the prayer of a man offering his heart.

And perhaps that is the invitation here, for all of us who serve, who give, who lay down comfort for the sake of others. The desire to be known by God in what we do — it is not something to suppress or apologise for. It is the shape of love itself. A child wants to be seen by their father. A servant wants to be known by their master. A worshipper wants the one they worship to witness their worship.

The prayer Nehemiah prayed — the one I found myself praying in the dark — is the prayer of anyone who has given something and wanted God to see that the giving was real.

Remember for my good, O my God,
all that I have done for this people.

Not for payment. For witnessing. For love to be seen.

1 Nehemiah 5:19, 13:14, 13:22, 13:31.
