The Piece I Keep Picking Up

Norm Reed
Zechariah 14

I finished reading Zechariah today and didn't finish it at all.

I do jigsaws on the dining room table, and I do them fast — a day, two at most, because the table is needed. When one defeats me I have a box with shallow drawers for sorting, and the puzzle comes off the table and goes to one side with the trays still in order, the greens with the greens, the sky with the sky.

That is where Zechariah 9 to 14 is. I have read to the end and I have not finished.

The way I sort is by what announces itself. Some pieces you don't have to think about — you turn them over and they are obviously edge, straight along one side, and they go in their own tray before anything else.

Zechariah has a lot of those. A king coming to Jerusalem on a donkey. Thirty pieces of silver thrown to the potter in the house of the LORD. Strike the shepherd and the sheep will scatter — the one line in these chapters Jesus quotes about himself, on the night he was arrested, before anything had happened to make it obvious. They shall look on him whom they have pierced.

I recognise those pieces because I have seen the picture they belong to. Someone else would sort differently. My wife would knit this book rather than assemble it; another reader would work it like a crossword, or read it straight through the way you read a novel and wait for the ending to explain the beginning. I don't think any of that is wrong. It is only worth admitting that the tray I reach for first says as much about me as it does about Zechariah.

The edge closes quickly. It closes better than I expect it to, every time.

Then there is everything else.

Hadrach and Damascus and Tyre and the cities of the Philistines. Sons of Zion set against sons of Greece. Two shepherds and two staffs, one called Favour and one called Union, both of them broken. A third of the land left, put through fire, refined like silver. Nations gathered, nations scattered, a city taken and half of it going out into exile before anyone is rescued.

I turn these over and I can group some of them. Water goes with water. Jerusalem goes with Jerusalem. Anything to do with shepherds ends up in the same tray. But grouping is not placing, and I know the difference. A better historian than I am would do more here — would know which empire is rising in which verse, whose armies came down that coast and in what year, and would place a whole run of pieces I can only sort by colour. I have read enough to know that help exists. I have not read enough to use it.

So the middle stays open. Not because the pieces are missing. Because I am.

And then the last chapter.

It begins badly. The nations gather against Jerusalem, the city is taken, and it is God who gathered them. There is no version here where the trouble comes from somewhere else and God arrives to sort it out. Then his feet stand on the Mount of Olives and the mountain splits east and west, and the point of the tearing is not the spectacle. It opens a valley. People run through it. He splits a mountain to make a door.

After that a day that will not behave — no cold, no frost, neither day nor night, and light at evening. Water going out of the city east and west, in summer and in winter, in a place where the streams die every summer and always have.

And then it ends. Bells on the horses, engraved the way the high priest's gold plate was engraved: Holy to the LORD. Every pot in Jerusalem as holy as the bowls at the altar. Anyone who comes to sacrifice may take one and boil meat in it.

That is the piece I keep picking up.

Not because I can't find where it goes, though I can't. Because I don't know what it means and I feel that I should. It is a verse about kitchenware. Bells and pots and a man who wants to boil some meat. And none of them account for the weight of it in my hand.

I can tell you what it seems to mean. Holiness has been a matter of fences — this pot yes, that pot no, this room, this day, this man. At the end the fences are not defended better. They are unnecessary. Everything is holy, so nothing needs guarding. That is a good reading and I believe it, and it does not explain why I keep coming back to the table.

The book had every reason to finish somewhere else. On the throne. On the Mount of Olives with the door open in it. On the nations coming up year after year to keep the feast. It finishes in a kitchen, with someone reaching for a pot.

I have put the box away. The trays are in the drawers, the greens with the greens. The table is clear except for the one piece, and I have left it sitting there, because I am not done with it and it is not done with me.

Hesed Words. Written for the road.
