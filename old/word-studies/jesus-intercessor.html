פָּגַע

Is Jesus an Intercessor?

A word study on פָּגַע (paga)

❦ ❦ ❦

I have quoted Isaiah 59:16 for years as a call to prayer — a verse about the shortage of intercessors, a summons to become one. Then I looked at the word underneath it, and found it was not, at root, a praying word at all. It was a word about being struck.

Ask most people what an intercessor does and they will tell you: he prays. He stands a little apart from the trouble and lifts it up to God. The word suggests a certain safety — the intercessor is the one not in the fire, speaking on behalf of those who are.

The Hebrew will not let you keep that picture.

The word is paga. And before it ever means to intercede, it means to fall upon. To strike. To meet with force. When the enemy fell upon the priests of Nob and cut them down, that is paga. When a boundary line runs until it reaches and strikes against the next territory, that is paga. It is a word with a body in it — contact, collision, the place where one thing comes hard against another.

And this is the word Hebrew chooses for intercession.

Established: the lexical range of paga — to strike, fall upon, meet, reach, entreat, intercede — is standard (Brown-Driver-Briggs, H6293). Not a reading peculiar to this site.

So the intercessor, in the oldest sense of the word, is not the one standing apart. He is the one who steps into the place of contact. Where the blow is landing, he meets it. To intercede is to put yourself in the gap between — and a gap between two forces is not a safe place to stand.

Isaiah felt the weight of that gap. In chapter 59 he watches God survey a society come apart — no justice, no truth, no one willing to stand between the wrong and its victims. And then the line that should stop you: He saw that there was no man, and was appalled that there was no one to interpose. The word is paga — a participle, mapgia, “one interposing.” God looks for a single person willing to step into the breach and take the weight, and finds no one. The gap stays empty. So, the verse says, his own arm brought salvation. God, finding no one to stand in the place of contact, resolves to stand there himself.

Hold that unanswered gap, and turn back six chapters.

In Isaiah 53, the Servant is described in his suffering — despised, stricken, pierced for the wrongs of others. And at the end of it, one last line about what he was doing in all that suffering: he bore the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors. The word is paga again — the same interposing. The Servant does not intercede from a safe distance. He intercedes by being struck. The suffering is the intercession. He steps into the place of contact and lets it land on him.

Established: Isaiah uses the same verb in 53:12 and 59:16, both in the interposing (Hiphil) sense — the connection is Isaiah’s own vocabulary, not imported.

Now read the two together, the way Isaiah wrote them. Chapter 59: a world with no one willing to stand in the gap, and God resolving to do it himself. Chapter 53: a Servant who stands in the gap by being broken in it. The empty place in 59 has a shape. The Servant of 53 is shaped to fill it.

So — is Jesus an intercessor? Yes. But not the way the word had let us imagine, standing a little apart with lifted hands. He interposes. He steps into the place where the blow is falling and lets it meet him. The prayer and the wound are the same act. That is what paga had been carrying all along, waiting for someone to read it slowly enough to see.
