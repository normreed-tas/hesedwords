Reflection

The Sacrifice of Thanksgiving

Psalm 50

Norm Reed

Psalm 50 opens like a courtroom. God summons heaven and earth as witnesses, gathers his people, and speaks. What he says first is not what you expect from a God calling his people to account.

He tells them he is not hungry.

If I were hungry, I would not tell you, for the world and its fullness are mine. Every animal in the forest already belongs to him. The cattle on a thousand hills. The offerings were never supply. He was never running short.

So what does he want?

Offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving. And at the end of the psalm the loop closes: the one who offers thanksgiving glorifies me. The psalm has defined its own terms. Whatever else glorifying God involves, it is this — gratitude, spoken, that names where everything came from.

Glory means weight. That is what the Hebrew word kavod carries — heaviness, substance, the sense that something is a big deal. Glorifying God does not make him bigger; he is already the biggest thing there is. It means letting him be as big in my life as he actually is.

Think of the opposite. If someone drove through the night to come and get you, and the next day you told your friends you'd got a lift — you would have made him small. He did something huge and you made it sound like nothing. Glorifying God is the reverse of that. Letting the true size of what he has done show.

Beside that command sits another, easy to miss. Call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me. A door left open for anyone with nothing to bring. The cry counts. God is honoured by being asked.

That is the part that caught me this morning. Not thankfulness as a virtue — thankfulness as the thing that glorifies God. The Reformers said it in three words: soli Deo gloria, God's glory alone. The catechism I grew up on called it the chief end of man. If that is true, and if this psalm means what it says, then thanksgiving is not a pleasant habit sitting somewhere near the edge of the Christian life. It is the point.

Which raises a harder question. I think I do this. The church thinks it does it. I am not sure either of us does. We have a great deal of activity that we describe as glorifying God, and Psalm 50 does not condemn the activity — not for your sacrifices do I rebuke you — but it does ask which way the traffic is running. Am I bringing something to God, or receiving from him and saying so? Thanksgiving is the test, because it is the one thing that cannot be faked into a programme.

I have wondered what that looks like in a person rather than in an argument.

In my early Christian life I served on a mission station in Papua New Guinea, and part of my work was caring for visiting ministries. Australians came through constantly, some of them well known. Two of the older men I have never forgotten — Norm Armstrong and Gordon Gibbs. Not for their preaching, which was outstanding. For the way they said thank you.

I never did the smallest thing for either of them without being thanked for it. And in the thanking, they made me — a young man carrying bags — feel important.

I knew something of their stories. Both had been hurt in ministry, in the ways ministry hurts people. It never got to be the heaviest thing in the room. Not because they had forgotten it, but because something else weighed more.

These two men had grounds for grievance and carried thanksgiving instead, and standing next to them you could tell which of the two weighed more.

Both are gone now. God was glorified in them.

I am slow to notice what I have been given, and slower to say so. Opportunities to thank God, and to thank the people in front of me, go past me all day. I want to be like them.

Hesed Words. Written for the road.
