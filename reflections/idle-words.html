Idle Words

The surprise was small and it has not left me: Ezekiel was mute for a season. Not that he could not talk — but that his speech was reserved for God alone. When the Lord gave him a word, he spoke it. The rest of the time he kept silence.

And yet the man never stopped communicating. His life became the conversation. He lay bound on his side, he ate measured bread, he stood dry-eyed at his own wife’s grave — and the message went across without a sentence. In addition to the words God gave him, he lived them.

There is a sacred practice of silence. Wayne Cordeiro once spoke of days spent in a monastery under an enforced quiet; I went so far as to find a contact and arrange the same, and then did not go. The aim of such silence is not emptiness. It is to teach us the weight of the words we do spend.

Jesus said we will give account for every idle word — and the word repays a second look. Argos: literally without work. Not only careless words, but workless ones — words that carry nothing, do nothing, employ nothing. Ezekiel’s silence was the opposite of idle. His silence worked.

James gives a whole chapter to the tongue. The Proverbs hand us a plain test for the fool: much talking. And more than once we are simply told to keep our words few.

I need the reminder constantly. I am thankful I do not have to endure what Ezekiel endured — the muting, the sign borne in his own body. But the message has not changed. Let my words be few, and let the few of them work.

Keep my words few
