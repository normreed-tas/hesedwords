Church Leadership — Marching Orders

Norm Reed

A fragment of transliterated Hebrew arrived on my desk this morning, ahead of our leadership meeting: chokmah elohim asah mishpat. The wisdom of God does justice.

It came from someone who had gone looking. Not a Hebraist — the grammar wobbles a little — but someone who wanted the words to carry more than English was giving them, and went and found them. That instinct is worth more than the accuracy. Most of us never bother. She did.

And the phrase is right about the thing that matters.

The mind reaches first for what it already owns. Wisdom becomes something quiet and interior. Justice becomes a courtroom — rules, compliance, sentences, the ledger balanced. Put the two together and you land somewhere comfortable: God's wisdom will help us apply the rules correctly.

But mishpat was never a verdict handed down from the bench.

It is restoration. To do mishpat is to step into the fracture — to stand between the vulnerable and the people exploiting them, to put right what has gone wrong. The prophets bind it, again and again, to the widow, the orphan, the poor, the stranger. Not because those are the deserving cases, but because that is where the health of a community can actually be read.

And chokmah is not what happens before mishpat. It is the root of which mishpat is the fruit. Wisdom that leaves no restoration behind it isn't wisdom yet. It's opinion, held devoutly.

I have spent the morning with it, turning it, and it kept resolving into two — two ways the same words could sit among a group of leaders. Chokmath Elohim be-yad manhigim osah mishpat — the wisdom of God, in the hands of leaders, does justice. Or: Manhigim chachamim le-Elohim osim mishpat — wise leaders, unto God, do justice. The first makes us the instrument. The second makes us the agent. And I have not been able to put either of them down, because leadership goes wrong in both directions. Claim to be only the instrument and we are absolved of the decision. Claim to be only the agent and we forget whose wisdom it was.

So the phrase stops being a motto and becomes marching orders. We are not a board managing an institution, and not custodians of a code, working out who has breached it. We are the hands through which something moves that isn't ours. And when we gather, the question is not whether a decision is defensible or efficient or clean. The question is whether it heals. Whether it protects the people who can't protect themselves — the ones in the room, and the ones not in the room at all.

That is not a courtroom. That is the shape of a ministry we have watched before: a man who kept stopping, kept turning aside, kept moving toward the people the system had already finished with. Wisdom with dirt on its hands.

Selah

I have sat in a lot of leadership meetings. Good ones, mostly. Faithful people, praying before they voted, wanting to get it right.

And I cannot remember one where we settled what we meant by justice.

We meant it. We used the word. But when it came time to decide something, justice quietly became fairness — did we follow the process, did everyone get a hearing, is the decision defensible if someone asks. And fairness is not nothing. But fairness can be achieved without anyone getting up from the table and going anywhere.

Mishpat cannot. Mishpat has to walk.

I think about what would happen if a church took that seriously. Not as a mission statement — we have plenty of those — but as the actual test applied to actual decisions. What is our budget doing for the widow? What is our governance doing for the person with no power in this building? Who is not in the room, and how would we know?

Imagine that in a thousand churches. Not louder churches. Not busier ones. Just churches where the question at every table was not have we been fair but has anything been healed.

The words came to me in imperfect Hebrew. They are the right words.

Lord, we will gather soon around a table, with our papers and our agenda and our good intentions. Be present in that room. Give us wisdom that does not stop at the meeting's end. Teach us to measure what we decide by whether it heals — and to ask, before we rise, who was not in the room. Make us wise where wisdom costs us something, and keep us from calling it wisdom when it costs us nothing. For the sake of the people you have given us to serve. Amen.
