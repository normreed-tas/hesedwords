2 Corinthians 11–13
10 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

I Know You're Busy, But…

Companion pieces from the same letter: Jars of Clay on the treasure carried in throwaway crockery, and Death in Us, Life in You on what presence costs a leader that a programme does not.

Paul spends the last three chapters of 2 Corinthians defending himself, and he hates doing it. He calls it foolishness, apologises for it twice, and does it anyway, because men have come to Corinth after him and the church has begun to prefer them.

He gives them a name: the super-apostles. It is his own coinage — hyperlian apostoloi, something like the extra-special apostles — and it is not a compliment.

The obvious question is whether he means the Eleven. Peter, James, John; the men who had walked with Jesus and whose authority Paul could hardly match. A few readers have taken it that way, and it would explain the edge in his voice. But a few verses earlier he says what these men brought to Corinth: another Jesus, another spirit, another gospel (11:4). Paul never speaks of the Jerusalem apostles like that, not even at Antioch when he opposed Peter to his face. And in 11:13 he drops the irony and calls them false apostles, masquerading. These are newcomers.

What can be seen of them is scattered through the chapters. They were Hebrews, Israelites, Abraham's offspring (11:22). They carried letters of recommendation (3:1). They took payment, and they were used to being obeyed (11:20). They had visions to report (12:1). And they were impressive in person, which Paul was not — his letters carried weight, people said, but face to face he amounted to little and his speaking was poor (10:10).

That last detail is the uncomfortable one. These were not charlatans with a sideshow. They had the pedigree, the paperwork, the platform presence and the experiences to tell. If you had sat in Corinth and heard both men on consecutive Sundays, you would not have needed to be gullible to prefer the visitor.

The Corinthians were not taken in by something false. They were persuaded by something more attractive.

Apostolos before the capital letter

We hear apostle as a title. Paul's readers heard a job description. Apostolos is simply a sent one — someone dispatched with a commission, answerable to whoever sent him. The word was in ordinary use before the church took it up, and the church kept using it in the ordinary way for a long time.

It is wider than the Twelve all through the New Testament. Luke calls Barnabas an apostle alongside Paul (Acts 14:14). Paul calls Epaphroditus the Philippians' apostolos to him — their sent one, carrying their gift and their care (Phil 2:25).

And it is in this same letter. Three chapters before the super-apostles, Paul commends the brothers travelling with Titus to collect the offering. They are, he says, apostoloi ekklēsiōn — apostles of the churches (8:23). Most English Bibles render it “messengers”, because by the time the translations were made the other word had acquired a halo that would not sit on a man carrying a collection bag. Same letter. Same word. Three chapters apart.

So when the visitors arrived in Corinth claiming to be apostles, they were not claiming to be the Twelve. They were claiming to be sent. The question was who had sent them, and what a sent life looks like.

The measure they brought

They came with three things, and all three were reasonable.

They had letters. Commendation from somewhere credible was how a travelling teacher established himself, and it is clear Paul's rivals had theirs, because he opens the subject himself: are we beginning to commend ourselves again, or do we need letters to you or from you? (3:1). His answer is that the Corinthians are his letter, written on hearts rather than parchment — which is a beautiful sentence and no use at all to someone checking credentials.

They took payment. Paul did not. He worked, and he accepted support from Macedonia while he was with them, and he says plainly that he robbed other churches to serve Corinth free of charge (11:7–9). He meant it as a gift. They read it as a verdict on the value of what he was offering, and underneath it a worse suspicion: that a man who will not receive from you does not want to be bound to you. Which is why his defence of it ends in a question rather than an argument — why? Because I do not love you? God knows I do (11:11).

And they had presence. Paul's opponents said his letters were weighty and forceful, but in person he amounted to nothing and his speaking was not worth listening to (10:10). He does not deny it.

Set beside those three, Paul looks like what he was: unrecommended, unpaid, and unimpressive on his feet. Every test the Corinthians applied was a sensible test. He failed all of them.

Professionals and amateurs

Amateur comes from amator, a lover: one who does the thing for the love of it. That is derivation, not current usage — in ordinary English today the word means unpaid, and usually means not much good. We have a word for someone who does a thing out of love, and we use it to mean someone who has not yet learned to do it properly.

Everywhere else that makes sense. The amateur is the apprentice stage. Nobody wants an amateur surgeon, and nobody should. So the movement from amateur to professional reads as growth, and anything that resists it reads as a refusal to grow up, or a refusal to take the work seriously.

What the professional has, and the amateur does not, is transferability. A method can be taught, packaged, carried to a new place and run again. That is what competence means and why it is worth having. It is also what a conference sells: this worked there, here is the structure, take it home.

Paul's credentials do not transfer. Five times the thirty-nine lashes, three beatings with rods, a stoning, three shipwrecks, a night and a day in the open sea; rivers, bandits, his own countrymen, Gentiles, false brothers; hunger, cold, exposure (11:23–27). Nobody is running a conference on that. There is no session in it, no model to take home, and nothing in it for an appetite for results.

After the beatings and the shipwrecks he adds the thing that comes every day: the pressure of his concern for all the churches (11:28). Not an incident. A weight that does not resolve, carried for people he cannot stop thinking about.

That is an amateur's list. It is a record of what he loved and what it cost him, and there is nothing in it anyone would want to copy.

If the amateur is the lover, then the pipeline is saying that love is the entry level.

Selah

Some years ago I burned out. I was Executive Pastor of a large church, and the work asked more than I was able to give.

I used to quote a line: if you have something that needs doing, give it to a busy man — the rest haven't got the time. I liked it. I was busy, and privately proud of it. It looked like competence. It looked responsible.

After a period of recovery I began building my activity back towards what it had been. We had taken on the care of a church in Hobart — they had asked for help, and they came under our covering — and as the new leader I was flying from Adelaide once a month: over on the Friday, two weekends there, home on the Monday.

On one of those trips I was in the Virgin lounge in Melbourne, waiting on the Hobart leg, reading Eugene Peterson. He says that busy set in front of pastor ought to sound the way adulterous sounds in front of wife, or embezzling in front of banker.1

Then the second thing. I thought about how people asked me for things, and almost every request began the same way.

I know you're busy, but…

It was not only what I thought of myself. It was what everyone around me could see, and had learned to apologise for before they asked. They had been telling me what I had become every time they opened their mouths, and I had been hearing it as a compliment.

I had taken it for the measure of success. It was a symptom of a terminal illness.

The cost of the measure

The most quoted statistic about pastoral ministry is that fifteen hundred pastors leave every month. Nobody has found a study behind it, and Lifeway Research calls it a myth.2 It has been repeated for nearly thirty years, growing as it went.

The real figures say something else, and something harder.

Lifeway Research puts annual attrition among evangelical and Black Protestant pastors in the United States at a little over one per cent, excluding retirement and death, and the rate has barely moved since 2015. Almost nobody leaves. In Australia there is no equivalent attrition figure.

What there is in Australia is the National Church Life Survey, and it points at the right thing. Between 2016 and 2021 the proportion of senior leaders reporting stress in the upper half of the scale rose from 46 to 54 per cent, while the proportion rating their own ministry effectiveness in the upper half fell. One of the researchers put it as leaders moving into unsustainable territory rather than into collapse — still effective, increasingly tired. And in a smaller Australian survey of clergy in 2023, around a third said they had seriously considered quitting in the previous twelve months.3

Put those together and the shape is clear enough. They are not leaving. They are considering it, and staying, and carrying it.

The measure has no column for that. A man who is effective and unwell passes every test the professional measure applies. His attendance is up, his preaching is strong, his board is satisfied, and he is in the upper half of the stress scale and has thought about quitting this year, and none of that appears on any report he will ever file.

John Piper's argument, written for pastors, is that this pull is not simply personal ambition — that the culture of ministry has professionalised the things that cannot be professionalised: tone, timing, atmosphere, the banter before the sermon. Nobody decides to become a professional. The system rewards the behaviours, and the behaviours redefine the work.

There is no survey that counts the men who go home from a conference with a new strategy.

The pipeline

Nobody argues for the amateur. The apprentice becomes the tradesman, the registrar becomes the consultant, the junior becomes the senior. That is how competence works, and it is how it should work.

The church borrowed the pipeline without noticing it was borrowed. We have senior pastors and next-level leadership and campuses and the language of growth applied to everything at once. A small congregation becomes a place you serve on the way to somewhere, and a country church becomes an apprenticeship. Nobody says this. It is in how we speak about appointments.

Most of what a church does can be professionalised and should be. Money ought to be handled by people who know how. Child safety is not an area for improvisation. Buildings, rosters, governance, the audit — competence there is a form of care, and the amateur version of any of them does real damage.

But the pipeline does not stop at the tasks. It keeps going until it reaches the part that cannot be done professionally without becoming something else. Professionalise the loving of people and it turns into a service: delivered to a standard, measured by satisfaction, scaled by delegation, and reported quarterly. It still works. It is still good.

And if the amateur is the lover, then the pipeline is saying that love is the entry level — the thing you begin with and grow out of as you acquire the real competencies.

Paul had the credentials. He lists them when he has to: circumcised the eighth day, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews, a Pharisee, faultless as to the law (Phil 3:5–6). It is a professional's résumé, and it was genuine. He set it down, and he calls the setting-down a reckoning.

In Corinth he will not use it at all except as foolishness, and when he finally does boast he boasts about a basket.

The basket, and the thorn

The fool's boast ends with an escape. In Damascus the governor under King Aretas had the city watched so that Paul could be arrested, and he was let down through a window in the wall in a basket and got away (11:32–33). That is the last item on the list. Then he changes the subject to visions.

A man claiming to be sent by God left town hidden in a basket and through a hole, because he could not use the gate.

Then the thorn. Whatever it was, he asked three times for it to be taken, which is the number of times Jesus asked in the garden, and the answer was no. What he was given instead was that grace was sufficient, and that power is brought to its end in weakness (12:7–9). The one credential he actually wanted removed was the one he was told to keep.

I was flying to Hobart once a month to care for a handful of people. By every measure I had been using, that was a step down — fewer people, a longer journey, less of everything I had been counting. I had come from a large church where I had been good at the work and nearly finished by it.

It turned out to be the space in which the problem could be worked on. Away from the demands of the large church, with a few people to care for and nothing much to run, I could see what church was meant to be. It had been there the whole time. I had been too busy to notice it.

The diagnostic

Burnout was years ago and the recovery was real. The measure that produced it has not gone anywhere, and neither has my liking for it. It is still what I reach for when I want to know whether I am doing well. A full diary still reads to me as evidence.

There is no point at which this is dealt with. Every conference, every appointment, every well-meant compliment says the same thing: take more on. It is the water.

So this is not a cure, and I have not got one. What I have is a test, and the useful thing about it is that I cannot administer it to myself.

Paul's opponents brought letters. Mine arrived as apologies. I know you're busy, but… — said by people who wanted something and had learned to expect a no. It was the most accurate assessment of my ministry anyone gave me in those years, and nobody was trying to tell me anything.

That is the diagnostic. Not what I think of my workload. What the people around me have learned to expect before they open their mouths. I'm learning to pay more attention to what people say before they ask me for help.

The church in Corinth had a man who would not take their money, who was unimpressive in person and poor on his feet, and who told them that his power was being perfected in his weakness. They preferred the visitors. I understand why.
