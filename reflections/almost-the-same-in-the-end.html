2 Chronicles 36:23; Matthew 28
23 Aug 2026
Norm Reed

Almost the Same in the End

I finished the Old Testament this year for the first time in Hebrew order, which meant I finished it in Chronicles.

My reading schedule had planned that. I hadn't, when I started.

Every other time I have read through the Old Testament it has ended in Malachi, on the same two verses about Elijah and the fathers and the children. I know how that feels. It feels like standing in a corridor with the door not yet open. Four hundred years of nothing, is how I have heard it preached, and how I have preached it.

This year the last page was Cyrus. A foreign king, giving permission.

It was not what I expected the end of the Old Testament to feel like. It took me a while to work out what the difference was.

Jewish Bibles and Christian Old Testaments hold the same books. They just stack them differently. The Jewish order goes in three piles — Law, Prophets, and a third pile called the Writings, where Chronicles sits at the bottom. So Chronicles is the last thing you read. The Christian order came from the Greek translation the early church used, and it sorts by type: law, history, poetry, prophets. Prophets go last, because prophets talk about what is coming.

That is the whole difference. Two ways of shelving the same library, and each one makes its point by where it stops. Malachi leaves you waiting for someone. Chronicles lets you go.

Here is how the Old Testament finishes.

Thus says Cyrus king of Persia: The LORD, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth, and he has charged me to build him a house at Jerusalem, which is in Judah. Whoever is among you of all his people, may the LORD his God be with him. Let him go up.

Let him go up. And that is the end of the book.

The same decree turns up in Ezra, word for word, except that Ezra keeps going: let him go up to Jerusalem and build the house of the LORD. Chronicles stops one clause short, before it says where.

Then you turn the page and Matthew begins with a list of names.

I have skimmed that list before. This time it read differently, because Chronicles begins with a list of names too — nine chapters of them, back to Adam. Matthew is doing the thing the last book did. He counts the generations in three sets of fourteen, and the middle one stops at the exile. Chronicles was written by people on the other side of that exile, trying to work out what was left.

And at the end of Matthew there is a mountain, and a man saying this:

All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations.

Read those two side by side and the same sentence is being said twice.

Cyrus: the God of heaven has given me all the kingdoms of the earth — therefore, go. Jesus: all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me — therefore, go.

Neither of them is speaking on his own authority. Both received it first and say so in the same breath. Which means the going is a permission before it is an order. Nobody can send you anywhere unless they have the right to, and both of these men stop to explain where the right came from.

Then everything else changes.

Cyrus sends them up. One direction, one road, one city, and a building at the end of it. Jesus sends them out, and the mountain he says it from is in Galilee. There is no address in his sentence at all. All nations.

Cyrus could give them a site and a permit. He could not go with them.

I have been a long time in the Old Testament these past months. I did not expect to be sad to leave it. It has been better company than I was prepared for, and I have come out of it knowing more of God than I went in with.

And now Matthew. I am starting the New Testament having been let go rather than left waiting, which is not how I have started it before.

I am with you always, to the end of the age.

Not a destination. A companion for the road.
