The Love That Kindles

On desire, idols, and the hesed that will not wait

There is a question most serious Christians carry quietly for a long time: why does desire for God rise and fall? Why does what burned with such urgency on one day sit so faint on another?

The Song of Songs is more honest about this than almost any other book of Scripture. Its heroine is a woman of genuine love — we watch her desire mature through chapter after chapter, from longing into belonging into open-handed offering. By chapter 4, she throws wide the gates of her garden, calls every wind to blow, and cries: let my beloved come. Her desire is real. Her invitation is complete.

And then, in chapter 5, she is asleep.

She hears the Beloved at the door. She knows his voice. Her heart was awake, she tells us — the desire was still there. But present comfort won. The warm bed, the washed feet, the small effort required to rise. Nothing dramatic. Nothing wicked. Just the ordinary weight of the immediate.

Desire is influenced by the present. The longing that is real at depth can be overridden by what is comfortable and close at hand.

John understood this. His final word to the churches — the last sentence of a letter entirely about love — was not a doctrinal summary. It was a warning: Little children, keep yourselves from idols. We imagine idols as dramatic rivals. But the Song’s portrait suggests they are more often just what fills the present: whatever occupies the space between the soul and its deepest desire, comfortable to remain with, requiring nothing. Not a false god. Just the warm bed that seems easier than the door.

This is the challenge the whole of Scripture names. The prophets call it morning dew love — Hosea watching Israel’s devotion evaporate before the day had properly begun. Paul names the structure of it in Romans 7: two laws at war, not between wanting and not-wanting, but between the deep desire of the renewed mind and the immediate pull of the body in the present. The woman’s heart was awake. But her feet were warm and clean.

· · ·

But the Song does not leave us with the closed door.

In chapter 5, verse 1 — just before the sleeping — the Bridegroom has already arrived. I came to my garden… I gathered my myrrh with my spice, I ate, I drank. He came before she rose. The feast was ready before her delay. And the voice that closes that verse breaks out of the poem altogether: Eat, friends, drink, and be drunk with love. A divine invitation to a fullness that exceeds what we can generate from within ourselves.

In chapter 8, the love he brings is named shalhevet Yah — the flame of the LORD. Not a human love calibrated to the quality of our response. Divine fire. The hesed that is strong as death, fierce as the grave, burning with God’s own heat.

What revives the woman after her failure is not a renewed resolution. It is the thrill — my heart was thrilled within me when his hand touched the latch. A presence broke through the comfort of the bed and she rose.

This is how it works. Not the generating of fresh desire from depleted resources, but an encounter — a touch at the latch, a whisper, a kindling from outside — that makes present comfort look suddenly insufficient. The Spirit’s work is to make near what has grown distant, to rekindle what the stream of the immediate has dimmed.

Hesed does not wait for us to want it enough. It kindles the wanting.

His hand is still at the latch.
