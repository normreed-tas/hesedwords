Blue Blazers

Two kings I read this week both finished badly.

Asa should have been safe. He came to a kingdom full of foreign altars and took them out. He deposed his own grandmother over an image and burned it at the brook Kidron. He gathered the nation into a covenant to seek the LORD with all their heart and all their soul. Seeking is his word — Chronicles uses it of him again and again, and closes his best years with a line that sounds final: the heart of Asa was wholly true all his days.

Then, thirty-nine years in, he was diseased in his feet, and the disease became severe.

Yet even in his disease he did not seek the LORD, but sought help from physicians.

Same verb. The man whose whole reign was built on seeking God, sick and frightened at the end of it, sought somewhere else. Not a different god. Just not him.

Uzziah had longer than anyone — fifty-two years, and Chronicles says where the good ones came from: he set himself to seek God in the days of Zechariah, who instructed him in the fear of God. Somebody taught him. Then a long, contented list of a man doing his job well for decades. Towers. Cisterns, because he had large herds. Farmers and vinedressers in the hills, for he loved the soil. Engines on the wall, invented by skilful men, to shoot arrows and great stones.

Then:

But when he was strong, he grew proud, to his destruction.

Not tired. Not disappointed. Not ground down. Strong. The thing that undid him was the thing he had built, arriving on schedule.

He did not take a foreign wife or raise an altar to Baal. He went into the temple to burn incense. Azariah and eighty priests went in after him and stood in front of him — this is not for you, Uzziah. He was holding the censer, angry with them, when the leprosy rose on his forehead.

And King Uzziah was a leper to the day of his death, and being a leper lived in a separate house, for he was excluded from the house of the LORD.

Fifty-two years, and the last of them in a house on his own. The man who forced his way into the holy place spent the rest of his life as the one man in Judah who could not go near it.

Neither of them changed gods. Both of them stopped asking.

If pride had taken Uzziah out to a high place and a foreign altar, we would recognise it. It took him into the temple, holding the right equipment, doing something that looked like worship.

Selah

I was a young Christian, and I admired my pastor. He was dynamic. He exuded faith. And he was proud.

I did not recognise it at the time. Looking back, the signs were there. The sign out the front of the church told the city that we were the fastest growing church in it. We dressed a certain way. We met in the best hotel in town. The same thing was in our language.

I enjoyed that church, and I was proud of it. I would not have used the word. I knew enough, even that early, to know it was not a word you applied to yourself. What I had not worked out was that not saying it did not mean it was not there.

It was a Saturday. A combined youth meeting, all the churches in the city, and our young people were there in force. The girls in skirts or dresses, the boys in blue blazers. I expect we were all wearing ties.

I looked around the room and sized up the competition. Jeans and T-shirts, most of them. I remember thinking how much better we looked. How good are we.

And then it happened. Not an audible voice. An internal one — clear, and cutting. God spoke; or more accurately, the Spirit divided.

I saw it for the first time, dressed in religious clothing, disguised in pious words. Pride.

I cannot describe adequately how that felt. Sitting in a room full of people, I was alone, naked, and sick to the stomach.

At the time I could not think of anything worse that could have happened to me. Looking back, it is one of the best.

When I think of that first pastor I am thankful to God for the good deposit he made in my life. He was a gifted man. The Spirit of God was on him, and he raised up leaders who pastor significant churches to this day. But most of what I learned from him was not what to do.

I do not mean that critically. After that Saturday I simply saw him in a different light.

I wish I could say the experience made me immune. It did not. Pride is still there, and it still finds church clothes to wear.

But this I know. If my heart is soft, and I let the Holy Spirit put his light on my life, pride becomes evident. It shows up the way blood shows up under a UV lamp — invisible in ordinary light, unmistakable the moment the right light is turned on it. What I cannot see, he can, and he will show me if I am willing to look.

Asa stopped asking. Uzziah stopped asking. The prayer is the asking, kept up: search me, and show me what is in here.

You might read those two and conclude that pride is what comes for a man after a long time on top. Uzziah had fifty-two years before it surfaced. On that reading a young person is safe for a while yet, having nothing much to be proud of.

I had a blazer and a Saturday afternoon. It was already there, fully grown, sitting in me and looking around a room.

It was not waiting until I became someone. It was waiting for something to work with.
