Psalm 102
15 Sep 2026
Norm Reed

Somebody Else Was Awake

Psalm 102 has a line in it I have read many times without stopping.

I lie awake; I am like a lonely bird on a housetop.

Here is the psalm as I would tell it to an eight-year-old.

I can't sleep.

It's the middle of the night and everyone else is asleep and I am not. My eyes are open. I'm listening. I don't know what I'm listening for, but I'm listening anyway, the way you do when you think you heard something.

I go up to the roof. In my country the roof is flat and it's where people sit in summer, where you eat and talk and sleep when it's hot. But there's nobody up here now. Just me.

There's a bird on the roof with me. One bird. All by itself.

That's strange, if you think about it. Sparrows don't do that. You never see one sparrow. They come in crowds, fighting over crumbs, twenty of them at once. A sparrow on its own has gone wrong somehow.

I feel like that bird.

I feel like the birds that live out in the desert too, the ones nobody wants near them. The ones that move into towns after everybody's left. I've seen those places. Broken walls and nothing growing and one bird sitting in the middle of it, making its noise.

I'm so thin my bones stick out. I can't eat. My days are going away like smoke off a fire — you can watch it go and you can't grab it. I'm like grass that somebody stepped on.

People are laughing at me. They say my name when they want to curse someone.

So I'm up here on the roof in the dark, wide awake, like a man on the wall keeping watch over a city.

Except there's no city. There's nothing in front of me. There's nothing to watch.

And then I say this:

But you, God. You're still there.

Not you'll come and sit with me. Not you'll make me feel better. Just — you're still there. You were there before the mountains and you'll be there after everything wears out like an old coat. You stay.

And then I say something I don't think I understood when I said it.

God looked down. From up high, God looked down at the earth. He was listening for prisoners.

Listening. In the night. From high up.

Somebody else was awake.

I never knew it, up there on my roof. I never heard him and I never saw him. I finished the night the same way I started it, alone with the bird.

But he was awake the whole time, and he was listening, and he was listening for exactly the sound I was making.

Something happens when you have to say a thing simply. You find out whether you understood it.

I had always read that line as insomnia. A man lying awake because he is miserable, the way anyone does. But the Hebrew word is shaqad, and it is not the word for not sleeping. It is the word for keeping watch. It is what a sentry does on the wall. Jeremiah hears God say I am watching over my word to perform it — shoqed. Another psalm says the watchman stays awake in vain. Same word.

So the man is not lying there empty. He is at his post. Eyes open, alert, checking every sound.

And there is nothing in front of him. No wall. No gate. No city to guard.

That is a better description of three in the morning than insomnia is. You are not idle at three in the morning. You are watching. You just do not know what for.

He asks for one thing at the start. Do not hide your face from me.

Then the psalm turns and starts talking about Zion — the broken city, the stones, the dust, a generation not yet born. The man has more or less gone out of it.

And when it comes back to him, it does not give him his face. It says God turned. He regarded the prayer of the destitute and did not despise it. The verb is panah, and it is the same three letters as panim, face. He asked God not to hide his face. What he is given is God turning.

You could read the whole psalm and not notice he was answered.

Then the ending. He asks one more thing — take me not away in the midst of my days. He is afraid he will not last.

And the psalm closes: the children of your servants shall dwell secure.

He asked not to be taken halfway, and the houses go to the children.

There is one more thing in the psalm, and the man on the roof does not get it.

For he looked down from his holy height; from heaven the LORD looked at the earth, to hear the groans of the prisoners.

Someone else was awake. On a height. Watching, all night, and listening — not for him particularly, but for a sound he was making.

He never finds this out. There is nothing in the psalm where the man notices. He finishes the night the way he started it.

We are told. He is not.

Selah

I was recently on holidays in Melbourne and Cathy, my wife, and I were window shopping in Sorrento. We'd stopped to buy lunch from a bakery and decided to eat it in the sun at the outdoor tables.

No sooner had we sat down than the birds started arriving. The noisy seagulls that circled overhead and the sparrows. While the seagulls kept their distance the sparrows didn't. They possessed a degree of boldness that belied their size. Unafraid, they landed on the table and inched themselves closer.

They were duly rewarded with crumbs from our meal. I was rewarded with pictures. Each one filled the frame.

I reached for those photos this morning looking for a lonely sparrow.

There isn't one. I framed them one at a time, but in the wider shot there are two more behind, and there were more than that off to the side.

I have never seen a sparrow alone.
