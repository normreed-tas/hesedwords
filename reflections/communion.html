Communion

This morning I led communion at church.

The words come without effort now, which is its own kind of warning. I took the bread, and I broke it, and somewhere between my hands the thing I have said so often went quiet and strange. Broken for you. My own hands were doing the breaking.

I don’t think the people saw anything. There was nothing to see. A man at a table, doing what he always does.

That night in the upper room, the bread was broken too. He broke it. Broken for you, he said — for them, the men at the table.

They did not understand. We dress that room in candlelight and lowered voices, but the men in it were arguing about which of them was the greatest. He gave them his body in bread and they reached for status.

I find that comforting. The first communion was received by men who missed it.

Days later, two of them were walking to Emmaus. Not even the eleven — two from the wider circle, leaving the city, talking it over and getting it wrong. A stranger walked with them and opened the Scriptures, and their hearts burned, and still they did not know him.

Then they sat to eat. He took the bread, and blessed it, and broke it.

And they knew him.

Not in the teaching. Not in the burning. In the breaking. The same three movements from the upper room — take, bless, break — done by the same hands, and this time the doing was enough. He had explained everything on the road and stayed hidden. He broke bread and was known.

I have wondered what the breaking came to mean for them, after. The hands that received the bread that night would soon be the hands breaking it — in Jerusalem, in homes, the breaking of bread becoming the name for the gathering itself. They had been given the gesture. Now they made it.

And then their own bodies began to break. Stephen under the stones. James under Herod’s sword. Paul, near the end, writing that he was already being poured out like a drink offering. The bread they had broken with their hands was teaching their hands a shape. Broken for you, he had said. In time it became broken by them. And in the end, for some of them, broken as them.

The body given became the body giving became the body given again.

Selah

As I stood there this morning I saw something I had said for years without seeing. I was breaking the bread for them — that is how I have always held it, the one at the table doing the thing for the many in the pews. And it came to me, between my hands, that this was wrong. Not wrong as in mistaken. Wrong as in too small.

The loaf I was breaking was the one we were all in. Not bread I gave them. Bread we were. I was not serving the body from outside it. I was a piece of it, broken with the rest, sharing the one loaf because we are the one loaf.

I have administered that table for a long time. This morning, for a moment, I was a guest at it.

If the breaking taught their hands a shape, what is it teaching mine?

And when the bread goes quiet and strange in my hands, is that the deadness of ritual — or the gesture asking to be understood again?
