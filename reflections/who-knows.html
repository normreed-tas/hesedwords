Jonah 3–4; Matthew 12:38–42
20 Sep 2026
Norm Reed

Who Knows

Companion pieces: What Is Already Yours, on Jonah saying the true thing that fits himself, and Different Work, on the offer this one ends at.

On Sunday the pastor preached on Jonah, and the word judgement stuck.

I sat there wondering what it actually means. When God judges someone, does he then punish them? Or does he let them reap what they have been sowing? I thought I knew. Halfway through the sermon I found out I did not.

This is not a piece that settles it. It is the wondering, written down.

Jonah’s sermon is five words long in the Hebrew.

Forty days, and Nineveh overthrown.

That is the whole thing. No unless. No if you change. He does not tell them how to escape, because he does not tell them there is a way out. The city takes three days to walk across. He goes in one day, says his five words, and stops.

He could have said more. He knew the rest. He proves it in chapter four, when he quotes God’s own name back at him, word perfect, as a complaint: gracious and merciful, slow to anger, full of steadfast love, and one who relents from disaster. Jonah knew all that before he opened his mouth.

Then the king of Nineveh does something nobody asked him to do. He gets off his throne, sits down in the ashes, and says: Who knows? God may turn and change his mind.

Nobody told him that. A pagan king had to guess at the thing the preacher could have told him.

And his guess was right. Chapter three ends with God changing his mind about the disaster he had said he would do.

God had said he would do it. He did not.

So the forty days were real. And they never happened.

Which means the warning was not a weather report. God was not telling Nineveh what was already on the calendar. Telling them was the rescue. The warning comes, and the thing it warns about does not. Nineveh got forty days for one reason. Somebody came and told them.

We usually think of judgement and mercy as two different things God might do, and then wonder which one he likes better. Here they are not two things. Telling the city about the disaster is how the city gets saved from it.

And that explains the strangest thing in the book.

Jonah is a preacher who will not preach. Not because he thinks it will not work. He never says that. He says the opposite, to God’s face, in chapter four: I knew you were gracious and merciful, slow to anger, full of steadfast love, and one who relents from disaster. That is why I ran.

He ran because he knew how this works.

Then there is the plant.

It works the same way round the other way. Nineveh got the words first, and the disaster never came. Jonah gets the disaster first, and the words come after. The plant dies, the worm does its work, the hot wind comes in off the desert — and only then does God ask him anything.

Nineveh was told what was coming. Jonah has to have it happen to him before he will listen to a question.

But look at what the plant actually does. It does not turn Jonah into a man who cares more about shade than about people. He was that already. He was sitting east of the city under a shelter he had built himself, waiting to see what would happen to it. The worm took away the last comfortable thing, so there was nothing left standing between him and what he really was.

In chapter one God uses a storm, the sea and a fish. For Jonah he uses a plant. The smallest possible loss, aimed at the one thing this man would actually miss, so that the question could land.

Here is the part I would rather not write.

Jonah was right about Nineveh.

The Assyrians were not misunderstood. They skinned men alive and stacked the skulls where travellers would see them. There is a whole other book in the Bible about that city, and Nahum is not gentle and Nahum is not wrong either. Jonah did not get his facts muddled. He had the facts. He had the theology too.

So the problem is not that he judged badly. His judgement was accurate.

The problem is what he did with it. He told them they were guilty. He did not tell them God was merciful.

That is worse than being wrong, and much harder to get out of. If the problem were bad information you could go and check — and if you turned out to be right, you would walk away feeling better about yourself. Jonah shuts that door. He was right about them. Everything he said was true. Five words, and not one of them false.

God’s last move is not an argument. He never tells Jonah he was wrong about Assyria. He asks whether there is any pity in the man at all.

Hundreds of years later, some very religious men ask Jesus for a sign. Show us something, and then we will decide about you.

He says no. The only sign they get is the sign of Jonah. And then he says this:

The men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgement with this generation and condemn it, because they repented when Jonah preached.

Look at what Nineveh actually does. They stand up. That is all. They are standing in the same place as the people they are showing up. They do not accuse anybody. They do not say a word. They stand there, and that is enough.

Put the two groups next to each other and nobody needs to speak. One lot had a foreign preacher who did not want to be there, five words, no Bible, no covenant, no history with God — and they turned. The other lot had all of it, and asked for proof.

What Nineveh does is take away the last excuse. We did not have enough to go on. You did. They had less.

Then the Queen of the South, doing the same job from the other side. She travelled from the ends of the earth to hear Solomon. Between the two of them, both excuses are gone: not enough given, and too much trouble.

Then Jesus says the line.

Something greater than Jonah is here.

Not someone. Something. That is what the Greek has, and he does the same thing about Solomon in the next verse. I do not know whether Matthew meant anything by it, and I would not build much on one word. Read plainly, Jesus is not telling them he outranks Jonah. He is telling them to look at what is in front of them.

There are three things in the passage that show the difference.

The preacher. Jonah ran the other way and hated the people he was sent to. This one came on purpose.

The message. Jonah’s was five words with the mercy left out. This one is come to me, all of you who are worn out.

The sign. Nobody tells us what Nineveh’s was. Matthew says the sign of Jonah is the three days in the fish, but he says it pointing forward, at what is coming, not back at what Nineveh saw. Luke says only that Jonah became a sign to them, and stops there. The book itself never mentions the fish again once Jonah is put ashore. So we do not know what they were given. What these men are being offered is going to happen in front of them.

Nineveh was given less, and they turned. These men are being given more, and they want it proved first.

Somewhere in all this my original question changed shape.

I had been asking whether God punishes people or lets the consequences run. But a courtroom is for working out who is guilty and who is not, and in Jonah nobody is in any doubt about that. Nineveh is guilty. Jonah is not much better, and the book knows it. Nobody in the story is waiting to find out who is guilty.

So the judging is not about deciding. It is about what God does next.

And what he does, in this book, is send somebody. Everything God uses in Jonah is aimed at getting a person to see something — the storm, the fish, the plant, the worm. None of it is punishment. Nobody is punished anywhere in these four chapters.

I am not saying the whole Bible reads like this. It does not. But this is what happens in Jonah.

The book ends on a question, and nobody answers it.

There is no last scene. Jonah does not say sorry. He does not agree. He does not speak again at all. The last words are God’s:

You are upset about a plant that you did not grow and did not look after, that came up overnight and died overnight. Should I not care about Nineveh, that great city, where there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left?

Then it stops.

I used to think a page had gone missing. Now I think the silence is doing the work, because anyone who can answer that quickly has not understood what is being asked. It is not a question about Nineveh. It is whether a man who is right can stand to watch mercy given to the people he was right about.

I have not got this settled. What I keep finding is that mercy is where God starts. Nobody has to argue him into it. Jonah knew that, and walked into the city anyway with five words.

Selah

It dawned on me this morning how much I am like Jonah. Not in the running. In the sitting.

Almost every day I am under that plant. The driver in front of me who cannot merge, and the quiet satisfaction of being right about him. A conversation with a friend about someone we both know, where nothing I say is untrue and every word of it is the five words without the mercy.

I know what God is like. I just do not pass it on.
