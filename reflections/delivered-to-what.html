Delivered to What?

Will you steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, make offerings to Baal, and go after other gods that you have not known, and then come and stand before me in this house, which is called by my name, and say, “We are delivered!” — only to go on doing all these abominations? Has this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes? Behold, I myself have seen it, declares the LORD. Jeremiah 7:9–11 · ESV

Jesus walks into the temple and starts overturning tables. Coins scatter across the stone floor. Doves flap out of their cages. People scramble.

And he shouts two things from the Bible.

Most people who read this story think: Jesus was angry about the buying and selling. And that’s partly true. But there’s something much bigger going on. And to see it, you need to know what he was actually quoting.

· · ·

The first quote is from Isaiah: “My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations.”

The second is from Jeremiah: “But you have made it a den of robbers.”

Two prophets. One sentence each. But both of them carry far more than they appear to.

· · ·

Start with Jeremiah’s line. The word we translate as “robbers” is a stronger word than that in the original Hebrew. It means violent breakers — people who smash through limits, who use force to get what they want. Not pickpockets. Crash-through-the-wall people.

And the word translated “den” means a cave. Not a cosy den. A wild cave. The kind of place where dangerous people hide.

Here is the picture Jeremiah is painting. A gang of violent men goes out, does terrible things — stealing, cheating, hurting people. And then they come back to their cave. Their hideout. The cave isn’t where the crimes happen. It’s where they go afterwards to feel safe and normal again. We’re okay. We’re home. We’re covered.

Jeremiah says that’s what the temple had become. People were breaking every commandment God had given them. And then they’d come to the temple, offer their sacrifice, say their prayers — and walk out feeling delivered. Like God had stamped their card. And then they’d go out and do it all over again.

The temple wasn’t where the crime happened. The temple was the hideout between crimes.

That’s what Jesus is calling out. Not the market stalls, specifically. The whole system — where religion becomes a way of feeling safe while living as though God’s covenant doesn’t matter.

· · ·

Now the Isaiah quote. The temple had different sections. The further in you went, the more restricted it was. At the very centre: the Holy of Holies — only the High Priest, once a year. Then courts for priests, for Jewish men, for Jewish women. And right on the outside edge — one court for everyone else. People from other nations. People who weren’t Jewish but who wanted to pray to Israel’s God. That was the one space in the whole temple where they were allowed.

Guess where the money-changers and dove-sellers had set up shop.

That court.

The one space for the outsiders — packed with market stalls.

So when Jesus quotes Isaiah — a house of prayer for all nations — and then Jeremiah — den of violent breakers — he’s saying: you have taken the one space that was meant for everyone, and turned it into a barrier that shuts them out.

· · ·

Here is the thing that every adult in that crowd would have known — and that most modern readers miss entirely.

Jeremiah 7:11 is not a stand-alone verse. It comes from a famous sermon. And everyone there knew how that sermon ended. Jeremiah had stood in the temple gate and told the people: if you keep on like this, I will destroy this temple the same way I destroyed Shiloh.

Shiloh was a holy place God had once let the enemies level. Every Jewish person knew that story. It was a warning from history.

So when Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11, the crowd doesn’t just hear verse 11. They hear the whole chapter — including the ending. It’s like someone standing at an ANZAC Day service and beginning: They shall not grow old… You know the rest. You feel the weight of the whole.

Jesus isn’t making a complaint about commerce. He is making a prophetic announcement. This temple is going to be destroyed. Again.

That is why the priests immediately start planning to kill him. They didn’t miss the point. They heard it clearly.

· · ·

And one more thing. Jeremiah 7:11 contains a line that tends to disappear in translation.

God says: “I myself have seen it.”

The original Hebrew presses hard on the word “I.” Even I, I have seen it. In the ancient world, the most powerful person in any court case was an eyewitness. God is presenting himself as the witness who has stood in his own temple and seen what has been done there.

When Jesus overturns the tables, he isn’t losing his temper. He is the embodiment of that divine witness — the one who has seen, and who acts from what he has seen.

And when he quotes Isaiah in the first person — my house — he is making a claim that the priests cannot miss. Whose house is it? The one whose name it carries. And if it is my house, then I have the authority to judge what is done in it.

“By what authority are you doing these things?” they ask him, in the very next scene.

They already knew the answer. That’s why the question frightened them.

Selah

The image that stayed with me was not the temple. It was the road back to it.

Marauding bands returning to their base. The countryside behind them bearing the marks of what they had done. And then — the cave. The place they came back to. To breathe, to settle, to feel themselves again.

What they did when they got there is what I find hardest to look at.

They sang.

Not silent, guilty men slipping in the back. They came with sacrifices and songs. They saw themselves as the delivered ones — victims of enemies, recipients of grace, people God had rescued. The psalms were on their lips. And they meant every word.

That is the most searching thing in Jeremiah’s indictment. Not that they were pretending. But that they were not. They genuinely believed the songs. They were the righteous in their own story. And from out there — from the road behind them, from the people who bore the cost of what they had done — the view was entirely different.

There is a church that sits next to a prison. I know it well.

And I have found myself asking — with some discomfort — whether the families filing through the prison gates on visiting day feel the doors of that church open to them. Whether they know they have a court there. Whether the songs sung inside reach them as welcome or as distance.

Because those families are not strangers to God’s house. They are the ones for whom the outer court was always meant. And the question God asks — the one who says I myself have seen it — is not whether we have a programme for them. It is whether they belong.

I have had to ask myself what we sing. And what those outside hear when we sing it.

Jesus sees it. He always does.

Do the people on the other side of that prison fence experience the church next door as a house of prayer — or as a den of the delivered?

What would it look like for the outer court to be genuinely theirs?
