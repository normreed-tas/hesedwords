Telling David's Census to an Eight-Year-Old

Australia counted itself on Monday.

The form was on the kitchen table for a few days before that. How many people slept here on Tuesday night. Ancestry. Religion. Whether anyone in the house needs help with self-care. It took twenty minutes and then it was done, and somewhere in Canberra a number is getting larger.

There is a chapter in Chronicles where a king counts his people and seventy thousand of them die.

I want to tell it the way I would tell it to an eight-year-old, because the story has a reflex attached to it, and the reflex gets in the way.

David wanted to know how many soldiers he had. Not so he could look after them. So he could feel strong. He wanted to see the big number.

Joab, who ran his army, told him not to do it. Notice what Joab objects to. He does not say a census is unlawful, or that the paperwork is impossible. He does not object to Israel being large — may the LORD add to his people a hundred times as many as they are. He would like them larger. What he says is: why then should my lord require this? Why should it be a cause of guilt for Israel? He is not arguing about the method. He can see the motive from where he is standing.

David made him do it anyway. Joab went through all Israel and came back to Jerusalem, and the answer came in: one million one hundred thousand men who drew the sword, and four hundred and seventy thousand in Judah.

And then David looked at the number and felt sick.

I have sinned greatly in that I have done this thing.

Nobody told him. No prophet had arrived yet. He got the total he had spent all those months chasing, and the moment he had it in his hand he knew what it was.

So what is wrong with counting?

Nothing. That is the first thing to say, because the story gets preached as though arithmetic were the sin. God orders a census twice in the book of Numbers, which is where the book gets its name. Chronicles itself opens with nine chapters of names and hardly stops counting after that. A book that lists the gatekeepers by household does not object to lists.

The difference is in the one law that governs it. When you count Israel, Exodus says, every man gives a ransom for his life — that there be no plague among them. Half a shekel. And this: the rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less. The one payment attached to counting Israel is a flat rate, so that the count tells you nothing whatever about how strong you are. Every man weighs the same. The money went to the tent, so that the people would be brought to remembrance before the LORD.

They were remembered by God, not tallied by the king.

David skipped the payment. And the plague that arrived is the one Exodus had named.

Which is the whole thing, in a sentence an eight-year-old can hold: the census form asks what you need. David's asked what he had.

Then Gad the prophet arrives with something strange. Not a sentence. A choice.

Three years of famine. Three months of your enemies running you down. Or three days of plague.

Look at what each one takes away. Famine: the land cannot save you. Enemies: your million soldiers cannot save you. Plague: nothing you own can save you. David has just spent months adding up his resources, and he is handed three doors, and behind every one of them is a different resource being removed.

He says the most honest thing he ever said.

I am in great distress. Let me fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is very great, but do not let me fall into the hand of man.

He picks the one with no human being in it.

And here is why the choice was offered rather than the sentence simply handed down. If God had sent the plague, David would have been punished, and that would have been the end of it. Because God asked him to choose, David had to stop and think about God. Months he had been thinking about his army. Now he had to say out loud what he thought God was like. And what he said was: God is kind. I would rather be in his hands than anyone else's.

Now the verse the chapter ends on, which reads like a footnote and is not.

David had somewhere to go. The tent Moses made in the wilderness was standing at Gibeon, and the altar of burnt offering was with it. That was the address. He knew the way.

But David could not go before it to inquire of God, for he was afraid of the sword of the angel of the LORD.

The thing standing between David and God was God. Not distance. Not a locked gate. Not shame in the ordinary way we mean it. He had looked up and seen a drawn sword over the city, and he could not make his legs walk him toward the one who was holding it.

Tell an eight-year-old and it comes out like this: David was too frightened of God to go and see God.

That is a real thing that happens to people, and it is not the same as not caring. It is what being properly caught out does to you. You know exactly where God is. You know the way. And you stop.

Now watch what God does about it.

He does not say come to Gibeon, that is where the altar is. He does not wait at the right address for a man too frightened to travel.

The angel tells Gad to tell David to go up to the threshing floor and build an altar there. A working farm on the edge of the city, with a man on it threshing wheat and four sons hiding behind him. Not the tent. Not the place with the history and the furniture. The nearest ground, in the middle of the plague, directly under the sword. God names the meeting place, and the meeting place he names is one David can reach.

Then something happens that tells you David has understood.

Ornan tries to give him the ground for nothing. Take it, he says — the oxen, the threshing sledges for firewood, the wheat, all of it. And David will not have it.

No, but I will buy them for the full price. I will not take for the LORD what is yours, nor offer burnt offerings that cost me nothing.

Put that next to how the chapter began. Counting Israel came with a price on it, a half-shekel a man, small and identical for everyone — and David didn't pay it. Now the altar is being handed to him free, and he refuses it and pays.

The two amounts have nothing to do with each other. What has changed is not the sum. It is that he has stopped treating God's things as his to take.

He builds the altar. He calls on the LORD. And fire comes down out of the sky onto it.

He couldn't ask at the tent. He asked on the threshing floor, and was answered.

The threshing floor did not stay a threshing floor.

Solomon built the temple there. Chronicles is the book that tells us so, and it gives the address with the reason attached:

Solomon began to build the house of the LORD in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah, where the LORD had appeared to David his father, at the place that David had appointed, on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite.

Think about what that means for everyone who came afterwards. For four hundred years, every man who came up to Jerusalem to worship stood on the exact ground where a king could not make himself go to God, and God came to him instead. The building is not a monument to David getting it right. It is built on the spot where he was too frightened to move.

Which is worth having if you have ever wondered what a building is for. Nobody needs a building to inquire of God — David proves that on a farm, under a drawn sword, with no tent and no altar and no priest. But he is also the man who proves how easily a person stops. The building is not there so that God can be reached. It is there so that a frightened man on a bad day has somewhere his feet already know to go.

And there is one more thing about that hill.

Moriah is where Abraham took Isaac. Same mountain, told by the same book. Abraham lifted a knife over his son and God stopped his hand. David saw a sword over the city and God said, it is enough; now stay your hand. Twice on that hill, a raised blade came down on nobody.

Then the temple was built, and it grew, and the way in was arranged in courts — how far you could go depended on who you were. Outer court, then nearer, then nearer, and at the last a curtain that one man went through one day a year. That was the design, not a decline. But you can see the shape of it: the closer you got to God, the fewer people were allowed to move.

And a man stood inside that building and said, destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.

They thought he meant the stonework. John tells us what he meant: he was speaking about the temple of his body.

That is the same move as the threshing floor, made once more and made permanent. God relocating the meeting place. Not a building you travel to — a person who comes to you. And when he died the curtain in that building tore from top to bottom, which is the last thing anyone needed to be told about how far in they could walk.

Selah

There was a time I sat on a bed in the front room of a house in Adelaide, weeping. I was more than sorry. I could not undo what I had done.

I don't think I was afraid. I just couldn't go to church.

So I rang a friend, and he came. He cared. We prayed there and then, in that room.
