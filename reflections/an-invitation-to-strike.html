An Invitation to Strike

The asking

He is in a hurry.

O LORD, I call upon you; hasten to me! Give ear to my voice when I call to you!

Then he asks for something a man standing in the temple would not need to ask for: that the prayer itself be counted as incense, and his lifted hands as the evening sacrifice.

There is no altar in this psalm. There is a man and his voice, asking that the words do what the smoke did.

That is most of my praying. Not the hurry, though sometimes that. The wanting to be received. I bring God what has happened and what I think about it, and underneath all of it is the same request David opens with: hear me.

The turn

A verse later he is asking for the opposite.

Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch over the door of my lips! Do not let my heart incline to any evil …

He has gone from hear me to restrain me, and the psalm does not mark the change. No but, no second petition announced. The man who wants to be heard is the same man who does not trust what he is about to say, and he has put both in the one prayer, in the one breath.

Hesed

Then verse 5.

Let a righteous man strike me — it is a kindness; let him rebuke me — it is oil for my head; let my head not refuse it.

The word under “kindness” is hesed. The same word the prophets use for what God keeps when his people do not: covenant loyalty, the thing you can count on because of who the other party is rather than because of what you have earned that week.

David puts it on a blow.

And he sets the blow beside oil for the head — the anointing, the welcome, what you pour on a guest. Both of them land in the same place.

The man who delivers it is a tzaddiq, a righteous man. So the correction is not simply good advice that happens to be right. David takes it as God’s doing.

The provision

Let my head not refuse it.

He expects to flinch. He is praying before it happens, about how he will take it when it comes.

That is not a feeling about correction. It is a provision for it — a space left open in the programme, with a request already lodged about his own reaction. He is not asking to become the sort of man who enjoys being told. He is asking not to turn his head away.

Asking amiss

James says we ask and do not receive because we ask wrongly, to spend it on our passions.

I have always read that as a warning about the wrong sort of request. It is also a description of the ordinary sort. What I want weighs more than anything else when I am the one praying. I am not usually asking for something disgraceful. I am asking for something I want, and there is nobody else in the conversation to say otherwise.

Verse 5 asks for correction as well. Then a correction is an answer, not a refusal.

Selah

Early in my time pastoring the church I invited a well-known evangelist to come and speak. I was driving with two pastor friends when they told me not to have him. There was a reputation I did not know about.

I resented it. I can remember the resentment better than I can remember the conversation.

I listened to them, and I rang him, and I withdrew the invitation.

They were right. The church was better for it. And at the time, in the car, what I felt was that two men had taken something from me.

I pray to be heard. I have prayed it for years and I mean it every time.

What I have not often prayed is the second half of David’s sentence. He asks to be heard, and then he asks to be struck, and he calls the striking hesed.

A God who only ever agreed with me would not be listening to me. He would be humouring me.

So this is the one I have to put in: let a righteous man strike me. Let my head not refuse it.

Companion: Struck by Kindness, on inviting correction before you need it, and on becoming the friend who gives it.
