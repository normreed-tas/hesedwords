Psalm 131

Norm Reed

The Quiet Soul

A Song of Ascents

But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me. Psalm 131 · 2

Psalm 130 left us at the wall. The watchman, cold and alert, straining for the first grey of morning, certain the sun would rise but living through the dark until it did. The cry had gone up from the depths. The waiting had been named. And at the last, the personal had become pastoral — O Israel, hope in the LORD — the one who had waited through the night turning to speak to those still in the dark.

Psalm 131 is what comes after the night watch ends.

Not triumph. Not the fanfare of arrival. Something quieter and stranger than either: a soul that has stopped straining. A heart that is not lifted up. Eyes that are not raised too high. And the image the psalmist reaches for — to describe this interior condition that is so difficult to name — is a child who has been weaned, resting against its mother. Not nursing. Not hungry. Not reaching. Simply present, simply held, in the particular quality of rest that belongs to the one who has learned that what they need is already given.

It is three verses long. It may be the most concentrated psalm in the entire collection. And it arrives here, between the de profundis and the great covenant psalm of Psalm 132, as the still small voice arrives after the earthquake and the fire — not in the dramatic register, but in the silence that the drama finally makes possible.

· · ·

The psalm opens with a triple negative that is worth reading carefully. My heart is not lifted up. My eyes are not raised too high. I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvellous for me. Three renunciations. Three things the psalmist is not doing. And the cumulative weight of them is the portrait of a particular kind of spiritual failure that is rarely named as failure — the failure of the overreaching soul, the person who cannot rest in what they have been given because they are always pressing toward what lies beyond it.

The Hebrew word for lifted up is gabah — to be high, to be exalted, to be proud. A heart that is gabah is a heart that has placed itself above its station, that has assumed for itself a perspective and an authority that do not belong to it. The raised eyes — ramah — are eyes that look past what is given toward what lies beyond their proper reach. And the occupation with things too great — niphlaʼot, marvellous things, wonders — is not the crime of curiosity but the restlessness of the soul that cannot accept the limits of its own creatureliness.

This is, quietly, a psalm about pride. Not the obvious pride of self-congratulation or the grasping pride of ambition. The subtler pride of the person who must understand everything, must control everything, must carry everything — who cannot lay anything down because laying down feels like defeat.

Against this the psalmist sets the weaned child. And it is worth sitting with the precision of that image, because it is not the nursing child — the child crying for the breast, reaching for what it needs. It is the weaned child. The child who has passed through the crisis of weaning — who has learned, through a process that is not painless, that the hunger will not destroy it, that the need will be met in other ways, that the mother’s presence is not contingent on the feeding. This child is not passive. It has arrived somewhere. It rests not from inexperience but from the knowledge that comes on the far side of the wanting.

The soul that Psalm 131 describes is a soul that has been weaned from something. From the need to have all the answers. From the compulsion to carry what was never theirs to carry. From the restlessness that mistakes striving for faithfulness. It has passed through the loss of those things — and the loss was real, as weaning is real — and arrived at a quality of rest that could not have been reached without the passage.

The communal summons at the close — O Israel, hope in the LORD from this time forth and forevermore — returns us to the pattern we have seen in Psalms 124, 129, and 130: the personal arriving at the pastoral. But here the movement is different in register. In Psalm 130 the watchman turned to the community with urgent words: the morning is coming, wait for it. In Psalm 131 the turning is quieter. The weaned child simply rests against the mother — and invites Israel to the same posture. Hope in the LORD. Not strive. Not press. Not occupy yourself with things too great. Hope. Rest your weight against the one who holds you. This is enough. This has always been enough.

There is a connection here to the batach thread we traced in Psalm 125 — the leaning of full weight, the trust that is bodily rather than merely intellectual. Batach is what the psalmist was doing in Psalm 125 when the encircling hills were named as the LORD’s own surrounding. Psalm 131 is batach arrived at through weaning — through the discovery, on the far side of striving, that the weight can be laid down and the holding will not fail.

And there is a connection to menuha — the rest-word that has been present since Psalm 127’s sleep of the beloved, since Psalm 122’s prayer for Jerusalem’s peace, since the deep background of Psalm 132 that is coming. Menuha is not inactivity. It is the rest of the one who knows where they are and whose hands are at work. The weaned child is not inert. It is fully present, fully alive, simply no longer grasping. That is menuha in its most human form.

Selah

This is the psalm I did not know I needed until the cardiac event made it available.

I had spent a long ministry occupied with things too great for me. Not wrongly — the work was real, the calling was genuine, the things that needed doing needed doing. But there is a way of doing necessary work that is gabah — that lifts the heart above its station, that takes on the weight of outcomes that were never in human hands to secure, that cannot distinguish between faithful effort and anxious control. I had not known, until the body intervened, how much of what I carried was the latter dressed as the former.

The weaning is the right image for what the cardiac event accomplished. It was not gentle. Weaning is not gentle — ask any mother, ask any child. There is a period of protest, of reaching for what is no longer available in the old way, of genuine loss. The loss of a certain kind of energy. The loss of the pace that had felt like faithfulness. The loss of the assumption that the work depended on my particular effort in the particular way I had always brought it. These were real losses. I did not arrive at the weaned child’s rest without passing through the weaning.

But what the weaning revealed — what I could not have seen without it — was that the weight I had been carrying was not mine to carry. The outcomes I had been anxious to secure were not in my hands. The things too great and too marvellous — the transformation of families, the interruption of intergenerational patterns, the slow turning of a system that grinds on — these were never going to be accomplished by my striving. They were the LORD’s work, and I had been present to it, and the presence was enough. The weaned child does not produce the mother’s presence. It simply receives it.

I think of the people in the work who have modelled this for me — who have sat in the depths of their own circumstances with a quality of stillness that I could not account for theologically until this psalm named it. The person who has been through the justice system and come out the other side not grasping for vindication, not pressing for restitution, not occupied with the things too great — simply present, simply resting against whatever has held them through it. That is not resignation. That is not defeat. That is the weaned child. That is the soul that has learned what the weaning is for.

There is a particular temptation in ministry — and I suspect in any vocation that is genuinely costly — to mistake the striving for the faithfulness. To assume that the weight of outcomes is a measure of the depth of the calling. To believe that laying down the anxious labour is a betrayal of the people who need the work done. Psalm 131 quietly refuses all of that. The psalmist who writes this is not a person who has given up. They are a person who has arrived somewhere. The heart not lifted up is not a defeated heart. It is a heart that has found its right size — the size that fits inside the hands that are actually doing the building.

The recovery gave me back something I had not known I had lost: the capacity to sit still in the presence of what I cannot fix, without the sitting still feeling like failure. To be with a family in the depths of their circumstances without needing to resolve those circumstances before I leave the room.

There is a misreading of this psalm that needs to be named. The weaned child’s rest is not distance. It is not the peace of the person who has retreated from the complexity to a safe place where the outcomes no longer press. The weaned child is still in the room — still present to the mother, still in the world, still fully alive to what is happening around it. What it has laid down is not the engagement but the illusion that the engagement is what secures the holding. When I leave a prison visiting room without a resolution — when the family is still fractured, the system still grinding, the circumstances unchanged — the sitting-still is not abandonment. It is the most honest thing available: the refusal to perform a rescue that is not mine to perform, the insistence on remaining present to what I cannot fix, the acknowledgment that the hands doing the deepest work in that room are not mine. This is not a retreat from mishpat. It is mishpat without the messiah complex — the fierce, quiet tenacity of the person who stays because the staying matters, not because the staying will resolve everything. The weaned soul does not abandon the fight. It abandons the exhausting pretence that it is the one the fight depends on. And in that abandonment — which costs something, which is not comfortable, which is its own kind of discipline — it discovers that it can stay longer, go deeper, and bear more than the striving soul ever could. Because it is no longer mistaking its own limited hands for the hands of the Keeper.

Sit with this:

What are you still occupying yourself with that is too great and too marvellous for you? And what would it mean to lay it down — not as defeat, but as the weaned child lays down the reaching, having learned that the holding does not depend on the grasping?

The soul that has been quieted is not an empty soul. It is a full one — full of what the striving never had room to receive. Rest against the one who holds you. This is the posture the ascent has been moving toward all along. Not arrival at a destination. Arrival at a way of being held.

Psalm 131 is one of the fifteen Songs of Ascent. The road they make together is Songs for the Road.
