Matthew 4:1-11; Matthew 27:32-54
6 Sep 2026
Norm Reed

If You Are

He had eaten nothing for forty days. The voice comes when he is at his weakest and there is nobody else there.

If you are the Son of God, command these stones to become loaves of bread.

If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down.

Three years later he is on a hill outside the city with a crowd watching, and the words come back.

If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross.

I had read both passages many times without hearing the second as an echo of the first. Matthew says it plainly enough. If you are the Son of God appears three times in his gospel and nowhere else — twice in the desert from the devil, once at the cross from people walking past, shaking their heads. Matthew was not a careless writer. He has put the tempter's line in the mouth of the crowd.

Strip both scenes back and the offer is the same one.

In the desert: you are hungry, and you do not have to be. Stand on the temple and let them catch you. Take the kingdoms now, without the long way round.

On the hill: He saved others; he cannot save himself. Come down now from the cross, and we will believe in him.

Come down. Use what you have to get out of where you are. Every version of it is reasonable, and every version of it ends the obedience early.

The second offer was the better one. In the desert he was alone and hungry. On the hill he was dying, and they were promising him the thing he had come for. They claimed it was the only evidence they needed.

There were angels available.

In the desert the devil quoted the psalm at him — he will command his angels concerning you, and on their hands they will bear you up — and when it was over, Matthew says the angels came and were ministering to him. They were real. The psalm was not a lie.

The night before he died he said it himself. Do you think that I cannot appeal to my Father, and he will at once send me more than twelve legions of angels? He could have. He says so.

He did not ask.

That is the whole distance between the two scenes. Not that heaven refused him. He had not come to appeal to his Father. He had come to please him — and he had been told at the Jordan, before he had done anything at all, that he already did.

He dies. Matthew gives it plainly — he yielded up his spirit — and then the ground moves.

The centurion has been there the whole time. He is not a man who has been following the argument. His men stripped him and dressed him up and knelt down in front of him for a joke. His men drove the nails. Then they sat down and kept watch, which is what he was there to make them do.

Truly this was the Son of God.

No question in it, and no condition. He was given nothing the crowd was not given. He was standing closer, and he had done it.
