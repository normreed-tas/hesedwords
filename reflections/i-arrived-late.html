Acts 9:32 – 10:48
10 Oct 2026
Norm Reed

I Arrived Late

A man in Lydda who has not got out of bed in eight years. A woman in Joppa who dies, and the widows standing about holding the clothes she made for them. A Roman officer in Caesarea who sends for a man he has never met.

I have read these as three stories. Three good ones, each with its own point, arranged one after another because that is the order they happened in. Acts does a lot of that. Things occur, and Luke writes them down.

These three are one story, and it has a direction.

Jerusalem sits high. Lydda sits on the plain below it, and Joppa is at sea level, on the coast.

Peter goes down to Lydda, across to Joppa, and up the coast to Caesarea. Caesarea is not a Jewish town. Herod built it, named it for the emperor, and gave it a harbour deep enough for the grain ships; it is where Rome keeps its governor. Peter walks out of the city of the Temple and into the administrative capital of the occupation, and sleeps there as a guest.

It happens in stages, and every stage is somebody else's errand. He goes to Lydda because the saints are there. He goes to Joppa because two men come and ask him to. He goes to Caesarea because three more arrive at the gate.

Nobody asks him to choose a direction. He is answering invitations.

At Lydda, before any of that, he says nine words to Aeneas and the man gets up and makes his own bed.

At Joppa they take him upstairs to a room full of weeping women and he puts them all out, and kneels, and says Tabitha, arise, and she opens her eyes. There is no one else in Acts doing this. Whatever authority looks like, he has it.

Then Luke says where he stayed. Many days in Joppa, with Simon, a tanner — a man who worked with the skins of dead animals, in a house by the sea.

Luke will mention him three times more.

The roof is Simon's roof.

Peter goes up there to pray, and he is hungry. A sheet comes down with every kind of animal in it, and a voice says: kill and eat.

By no means, Lord. I have never eaten anything common or unclean.

He says it standing on the house of a man who handles dead animals for a living, in rooms he has been living in for days, with the smell of the work underneath him.

He cannot mean both of those. If he is Lord, there is no by no means. Peter has been with Jesus through three years, a denial, a resurrection and an ascension, and he has just raised the dead in his name, and when the voice tells him to do something he will not do it.

It happens three times. Then the sheet goes up and he is left on the roof wondering what it meant.

And while he is still wondering, there are three men at the gate who left Caesarea yesterday.

He goes down to them, and the next morning he goes with them. In Caesarea, the first thing he says in that house is this: you know how unlawful it is for a Jew to associate with a foreigner.

Only the word is not the word for breaking the Law. It is athemiton — not done. Forbidden, but by custom and decency, not by any command.

There is no command anywhere in Torah against entering a Gentile house. Peter had kept a rule God never gave him, and had kept it so long that when he explained it out loud he called it law.

And then he finishes the sentence: God has shown me that I should not call any man common or unclean.

Not any food. Any man. He had been looking at a sheet full of animals for three days and it was never about the animals.

Cornelius falls down at his feet and Peter pulls him up. Stand up. I myself am also a man.

Then he starts to preach, and partway through he says a thing that sounds like a conclusion and is really a confession: truly I perceive that God shows no partiality. Now. In this room. After the vision, after the interpretation, after the journey, standing in the house — now he perceives it.

He is still speaking when the Spirit falls on everyone listening.

He had not finished. There was no appeal, nothing asked of them, no moment where Peter opened a door and they came through it. God did not wait for the end of the sentence.

All that is left for him to do is notice. Can anyone withhold the water?

It is a question, and it is not addressed to God.

Selah

I have been led, and known I was being led, and still not seen what it was for.

Hobart. A church next to a prison. Fourteen years working in the justice system. A Churchill Fellowship that took me into twenty-one prisons around the world. Each of those arrived looking like a step. I took them as steps. It was a long time before I understood that they were not separate. They had been arranged.

I got there the way Peter got there. Late, and after it had already happened.

Since my cardiac arrest I have pushed back on any suggestion that God “saved me for a reason.” But this morning I am not so certain. This too may just be yet another thing that the Holy Spirit has arranged.

Perhaps I am still late.
