My Oath — and What It Really Means

A reflection on Jeremiah 4:2

I have been a Justice of the Peace for many years. I have watched people place their hand on a Bible and swear to tell the truth. I have administered statutory declarations and witnessed signatures that carry legal weight.

I have never, until today, stopped to ask where it all came from.

The Formula

The court oath in its classic form is a formula. Words arranged in a particular order, spoken aloud, before a witness.

I swear by Almighty God that the evidence I shall give will be the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth.

The formula works — legally — when the words are spoken correctly. What happens inside the person speaking them is, in a legal sense, not the court’s business. The formula has been satisfied. The record shows: oath administered.

I have been part of that moment many times. Pen on paper. Words spoken. Done.

Jeremiah has questions about this.

The Prophet’s Conditions

I was reading through Jeremiah today when I stopped at chapter four, verse two.

“And if you swear, ‘As the LORD lives,’ in truth, in justice, and in righteousness…” Jeremiah 4:2

The phrase “As the LORD lives” is an ancient oath formula — chai-YHWH in Hebrew. It appears dozens of times across the Old Testament. It is the most solemn swearing available: grounding the oath in the existence of God himself. Nothing more fundamental could be invoked.

But Jeremiah adds three conditions.

Emet. Truth — but not merely accurate words. The Hebrew means reliability, integrity, the quality of being a person whose yes means yes. It shares its root with the word we say at the end of every prayer: amen. The solid thing.

Mishpat. Justice — the restoration of right relationship. Not the cold arm of the law but the active work of setting things right between people.

Tzedakah. Righteousness — not a legal verdict but a movement toward whoever is missing from the circle. God’s own word for the impulse that goes looking for the one who has been left out.

The prophet’s point is devastating in its simplicity. You can say chai-YHWH — “as the LORD lives” — perfectly. You can have the formula word-perfect. And it can still be empty.

The oath is only as real as the person swearing it.

One chapter later, Jeremiah shows us what this looks like in practice.

“Though they say, ‘As the LORD lives,’ yet they swear falsely.” Jeremiah 5:2

The right words. The wrong life. The oath hollowed out from within.

Superman and the Ancient Triad

There is a phrase from American popular culture — the old television series where Superman stood for “Truth, Justice, and the American Way.”

The phrase was added in the 1950s. But the triad it echoes — emet, mishpat, tzedakah — is 2,600 years older. Jeremiah wrote it in the sixth century before Christ.

And Jeremiah’s version is richer.

Superman’s justice upholds the law. Jeremiah’s tzedakah goes looking for the person the law forgot. Superman’s truth is about accurate reporting. Jeremiah’s emet is about the character of the one in the room. The American Way offers a cultural standard. Jeremiah offers something that no culture can contain.

The echo is charming. The original is better.

My Oath — and What It Really Means

I come back to my own role.

When I administer an oath, I am doing something more than witnessing a legal formula. I am standing at the intersection of two things: the ancient conviction that truth-telling is a sacred act, and the stubborn human tendency to perform the form while abandoning the substance.

Jeremiah’s triad is not a checklist for the person swearing. It is a description of character. The oath does not produce integrity. It reveals it. Or fails to.

Which means the most important question I can ask, as a JP, is not whether the words were spoken correctly. The question Jeremiah presses on me is this: am I the kind of person before whom an oath means something?

Do I carry enough emet — enough personal reliability — that my presence in the room adds weight to the side of truth? Is there enough mishpat in my way of living that my witness to another’s declaration is more than a signature? Does tzedakah — the movement toward those the system has forgotten — shape how I hold this role?

There is a detail at the end of Jeremiah 4:2 I find quietly astonishing. The verse ends: “then nations shall bless themselves in him, and in him shall they glory.” That is the Abrahamic promise — Genesis 12:3 echoing through the oath. An oath sworn well, in truth and justice and righteousness, is never merely a private transaction. It has a witness bigger than the courtroom.

I knew what the words of the oath meant.

I am still learning what it means to be a person who swears them.
