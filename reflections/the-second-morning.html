Psalm 90
2 Sep 2026
Norm Reed

The Second Morning

The small book of Psalms I pray from is vinyl-covered and about the size of my hand. It has been with me since 2016, and by now the margins are full — dates, initials, single words, lines drawn beside verses. Psalm 90 came up this morning. Above the psalm, in smaller type than the rest, sits the line the translators put there: A prayer of Moses, the man of God.

Of the hundred and fifty psalms, that is the only one Moses's name stands over. Jewish tradition gave him more, because the psalms that follow carry no headings of their own. But only the one is titled.

The heading also tells you what kind of thing it is. Not a song. Tephillah — a prayer. There are five so labelled in the whole Psalter. Somebody, a very long time ago, sorted this psalm into the pile marked: this one is for praying.

So teach us to number our days, that we may get a heart of wisdom.

Verse twelve is one I have come back to for years.

On the thirteenth of February my heart stopped. I died. I came to the end of my days.

Then this morning I came to Psalm 90 — the first time I have read it prayerfully since. The words were the same words. Now they were mine.

It helped me to see where Moses was standing when he prayed it. He was not offering a general reflection on how short life is. In Numbers 14 the verdict had already been handed down: this generation would fall in the wilderness, and not one of them would go in. They were not wondering whether their days were numbered. They knew the number, and every funeral in the camp counted it down in front of them.

And Moses does not ask for the sentence to be lifted. He asks to be taught by it. Teach us to count. Not spare us the count — teach us what it is for.

The middle of the psalm is grass.

In the morning it flourishes and grows up; in the evening it is cut down and withers.

Anyone who has lived through a Tasmanian summer knows what he means. The green comes up after rain and by February the paddocks are the colour of paper. It grows in the morning. It is gone by evening. That is the whole span the psalm allows us, and it does not soften it.

Then verse fourteen.

Satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love, that we may rejoice and be glad all our days.

Morning again. The same word he used of the grass. The morning that was the grass's brief hour is the one he now asks God to fill with something else, and the something else is hesed. The verb behind satisfy is a word for food — fill us, the way you fill the hungry. He is not asking for a mood.

Selah

The reason I am awake early writing this is that nothing else will do it. I have tried the other things. I will only be satisfied by his steadfast love.

This day is a gift, and if it holds nothing else, it can hold that — an hour spent appreciating the hesed of God. In eternity that is what we will be worshipping him for. Because of the road I have been on, I have been given the privilege of starting now.

Postscript

Psalm 90 opens Book IV of the Psalter. Book III ends at Psalm 89, with the covenant with David apparently in ruins: you have renounced the covenant with your servant. The next voice we hear is Moses — the man who prayed before there was a throne to lose.
