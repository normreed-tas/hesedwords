Reflection

Kept Anyway

1 Chronicles 4:9–10 · Norm Reed
Jabez was more honorable than his brothers; and his mother called his name Jabez, saying, “Because I bore him in pain.” Jabez called on the God of Israel, saying, “Oh that you would bless me and enlarge my border, and that your hand might be with me, and that you would keep me from harm so that it might not bring me pain!” And God granted what he asked. 1 Chronicles 4:9–10

I prayed one of my most important prayers in a room full of people, and I am the only one who remembers it.

It was in the United States, in the house of the man leading that week’s training, during a week I had come to learn the New Testament seminar for Walk Thru the Bible. I had already opened the ministry in Papua New Guinea and had been running the Old Testament seminar there for some years, in villages you reached on foot or by river. There were people in that house from a dozen countries, none of us having taught this one before.

One evening there was a time of prayer. When my turn came, I prayed for reach — not for the villages I already knew, but past them, nation to nation. I used David Livingstone’s image: smoke rising from a thousand villages.

When the training ended I went back to Papua New Guinea. I stayed there five more years before I came to Australia.

Nobody wrote any of it down. Not that prayer, not that evening — not by me, and not, as far as I know, by anyone else in the room. It survives, if it survives at all, because I have just told you about it, decades later.

1 Chronicles opens with nine chapters of names.

Adam. Seth. Enosh. Then generation after generation, tribe after tribe, until the list reaches a city being resettled after exile. It reads like the least important part of the book, and I used to treat it that way.

In the Hebrew Bible’s own arrangement, Chronicles is not an appendix tacked onto Kings. It is the last book of the whole Tanakh — the final word of the entire Hebrew Bible, in the order the tradition itself settled on. The very last thing that whole collection does, at its close, is send a scattered people back toward the land, on a foreign king’s decree. But it will not let them go until it has told them, for nine chapters, who they are.

The people who first received this book had lost everything a nation has. Land. Temple. King. Most of them had been born somewhere else, in exile, and had only heard about home from people older than them. What they were handed, before any of the rest of the story, was not a summary of what they’d lost. It was a list of names going back to Adam, proving they had never stopped being kept.

Most of those names get nothing else. No deed, no line of dialogue, sometimes not even a father named alongside them — just a name, set down, and then the next one.

Somewhere past the middle of it, in the tribe of Judah, one name gets four verses instead of one line.

His mother named him Jabez, and named him for the pain of bearing him. He is called more honourable than his brothers, for no reason the text bothers to give, and then prays that God would keep him from the very thing his name records: bless me, enlarge my border, keep your hand on me, keep harm far enough that it doesn’t bring me pain again.

The doubling in his prayer falls on bless, not on border — oh that you would truly bless me. It simply means he is not asking delicately. And border, in this book, is never abstract. Chronicles spends whole chapters on which tribe held which pasture, which family kept which town after exile had scattered all of it. A man asking this insistently for his border to grow, in a book obsessed with border, is not reaching for a figure of speech. He is asking for the one thing that had just been taken from everyone this book was written for.

God granted what he asked.

The text does not follow him afterward. It returns to the list — to names that get one line, or none — and keeps going. Four verses happen to be enough to see what was already true of every bare name on either side of him.

There is an old word for this kind of keeping: zakar, to remember — not the recalling that happens in your head, but the kind that moves. God zakars Noah, and the flood recedes. God zakars Abraham, and Lot is pulled out of a burning city. To be remembered, in that sense, is to have something done on your behalf. The names in this genealogy carry no deeds of their own. What they carry is the verb underneath the whole book: they were remembered enough to be kept, whether or not anyone can tell you why.

Bruce Wilkinson, whose house I had prayed in, wrote a book about those same four verses years later and called it The Prayer of Jabez. It sold in the millions. People who had never read another word of Chronicles learned to pray Jabez’s exact request as their own — enlarge my border — without knowing they were quoting a man named for someone else’s pain.

I had prayed almost the same request, in the same house, before that book existed. Mine never became anyone else’s prayer. For a long time I believed that was the difference that mattered.

It wasn’t. Jabez was not kept because his four verses eventually sold in the millions. He was kept the moment God granted what he asked, centuries before there was a scroll, let alone a bestseller. Whatever kept my own prayer, if anything did, was never going to be a book either.

Selah

I read the chapter about me before I read anything else in that book. I remember looking for my own name before I’d taken in a single sentence around it — the way you do with a class photo.

The last word of the whole Hebrew Bible spends nine chapters remembering people nobody wrote a story for, before it lets any of them go home. I am one more name further down a list I will never see the whole of.

I still think, sometimes, of that room — the villages I already knew, and the smoke I asked God to multiply past them, nation to nation, a thousand fires I never saw and never will. Every one of them a name I don’t know, kept or not kept, the same way I am.
