Reflection

Out of the Depths

Psalm 130
Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD! O Lord, hear my voice! Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleas for mercy. Psalm 130 · 1–2

We have come a long way from Meshech. The pilgrim who began in exile, among deceitful tongues, reaching for shalom and meeting war — has survived the road, arrived at Jerusalem, endured contempt, escaped the flood, rested in the encircling hills, planted seeds in tears, built a house, sat at a table, and narrated a long history of affliction that did not prevail. The collection has covered extraordinary ground.

And now, without warning or explanation, the bottom drops out.

Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD.

No context is given. No cause is named. No enemy is identified, no affliction described, no history narrated. The psalm opens in the abyss and offers nothing but the cry itself. This is the psalm for the moment when there is nothing to say except: help. When the road, the survival, the table, the testimony — none of it is currently accessible. When the only available movement is downward, and the only available language is the raw voice calling from below.

The psalm’s Latin title — De Profundis — has given it a life beyond the Psalter. Augustine preached it. Luther called it one of the Pauline psalms, the ones that go deepest into the human condition before the gospel. Bonhoeffer sat with it in prison. It has been read at bedsides and gravesides across twenty centuries because it does something almost no other text manages: it gives language to the place where language fails.

And it sits here, in the Psalms of Ascent, at the opening of the final movement toward Zion. Before the pilgrim arrives at the temple courts, before the anointing oil and the night blessing, the collection requires this: descent. The de profundis is not a setback in the pilgrimage. It is part of the route.

The psalm’s opening word in Hebrew is mimma’amaqqim — out of the depths, from the deep places. The word shares its root with ma’ameq, the deep waters, the abyss. It is the language of Sheol, of the place beneath all places, of the condition in which there is no footing. The psalmist is not at the bottom of a difficult season. They are at the bottom of everything.

And they cry. The verb is qarati — I have called, I am calling, my calling goes up from here. Even from the depths, the direction of the cry is upward. This is not passive resignation and it is not despair. It is the most concentrated form of prayer available to a person with nothing left: the voice, sent upward, from the place where nothing else can be sent.

The psalm then makes its most surprising theological move — the one that has stopped careful readers across the centuries. If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand? The psalmist introduces the question of sin at the very moment of crying for help. Not to confess guilt. Not to explain the suffering. But to clear the ground for what comes next.

The argument is precise. If God kept a full accounting of iniquities — if the relationship between God and the human creature were purely transactional, purely forensic, governed entirely by what is owed and what is deserved — then no one could stand before him. The question who could stand? is rhetorical. The answer is: no one. Not the psalmist. Not Israel. Not anyone.

But — and here is the hinge on which the entire psalm turns — with you there is forgiveness. The Hebrew word is selichah — pardon, forgiveness, the releasing of what is owed. It appears only three times in the entire Hebrew Bible, and this is the most theologically concentrated of those occurrences. Selichah is not cheap. It is not the dismissal of what is owed. It is the costly absorbing of it by the one who forgives.

And then the most counterintuitive clause in the psalm: that you may be feared. Forgiveness produces yirah — awe, reverence, the proper orientation of the creature before the Creator. This runs against everything we might expect. We assume fear drives us to seek forgiveness — we are afraid, so we repent. The psalm reverses the sequence. The experience of being forgiven is what generates the proper posture of awe. Grace produces fear, not the other way around. The one who has been forgiven much knows, in a way that the self-sufficient never can, exactly who God is.

The waiting section — verses 5 and 6 — is among the most carefully constructed in the Psalter. I wait for the LORD, my soul waits, and in his word I hope. The Hebrew word for wait is qivviti — from qavah, which carries the sense of stretched expectation, of something taut with anticipation. This is not passive endurance. It is the active, sustained orientation of the whole self toward the one who has not yet spoken but will.

The simile that follows is precise and worth inhabiting: more than watchmen for the morning, more than watchmen for the morning. The repetition is not accidental. The watchman on the city wall through the long night — cold, alert, straining for the first grey at the horizon — knows with certainty that morning is coming. The sun does not fail to rise. But the night is long and the waiting is real and the watching requires everything the watchman has. The psalmist’s waiting is like that: certain of the morning, but living through the dark.

The psalm then makes a move that is structurally significant. What began as a solitary cry — I cry, my soul waits — widens in its final verses into communal address: O Israel, hope in the LORD. The personal has become pastoral. The one who has cried from the depths and waited through the dark has arrived at a place where they can turn to the community and say: you too. The morning is coming. Wait for it. The depths are not only survived. They become the ground from which the most credible words of hope can be spoken — because they come from someone who has been there.

Selah

There is a reason this psalm has been read at bedsides and gravesides for twenty centuries. It does not require explanation or context. It simply opens, and the person who is in the depths recognises where they are, and knows they are not the first person to have been here, and finds — in the very act of the psalmist’s cry going upward — that upward is still a direction available to them.

I have sat with people in the depths. Not the depths of theological abstraction but the specific, bodily depths of incarceration, of family fracture, of the accumulated shame that the justice system can carve into a person across years of contact. The person who has been processed by the machinery of justice — who has had their name become a case number, their story become a file, their family become a collateral consequence — knows something about mimma’amaqqim that cannot be learned from the outside.

What I have learned, sitting with those people, is that the psalm’s first movement — the cry itself — is often the hardest thing. Not the waiting. Not even the suffering. The cry. Because crying requires the belief that there is someone to cry to, and that belief is precisely what the depths work to destroy. The depths say: there is no upward. There is no one listening. Your voice does not carry from here. And the psalmist’s first act is to refuse that verdict — to send the voice upward anyway, before the answer comes, before the morning arrives, before anything has changed except the direction of the cry.

The forgiveness clause — but with you there is selichah — has particular resonance in the context of the work. The people the Foundation serves carry shame that is not always proportional to what they have done, and sometimes shame for things that were done to them before they ever made a choice. The justice system is not well designed for distinguishing between these. It marks iniquities — that is, functionally, what it does. And the person who has been marked learns to mark themselves, to absorb the system’s assessment as self-knowledge, to become in their own interior life the judge who found them guilty.

What selichah does — what the God of Psalm 130 does — is refuse to let the ledger be the last word. Not by pretending the ledger does not exist. But by absorbing what is on it and returning the person to a standing they could not have earned. Who could stand? No one. But with you there is forgiveness. Everyone who receives it.

But selichah is not only an interior transaction. The psalm’s question — who could stand? — is answered not merely in the private conscience of the forgiven person but in the community where they are received. The state’s sentence ends. The marking does not. The post-release world — its closed doors, its unavailable housing, its employers who will not call back — continues to mark iniquities long after the official ledger has been closed. Society keeps its own accounts. And the person who has served their sentence discovers that standing is not restored simply because the law says it is. What they need is not a programme of restoration administered from a safe distance. They need someone to sit down with them. The church’s deepest historical vocation — older than its institutions, older than its theology faculties, older than its arguments about social justice — is precisely this: to be present at the place of affliction without an agenda of rescue. To sit with the woman at the well. To receive the one who pleads for crumbs from the table. Not fixing. Not judging. Simply there — attending, remaining, refusing to leave. This is what the community of selichah looks like in practice. Not the announcement of forgiveness from a position of safety, but the willingness to sit in the depths alongside those who are still crying from them. The Onesimus Foundation’s work is that sitting-down — maintaining presence across the barrier of incarceration, refusing to let the system’s logic of severance be the last word about a family’s future.

After the cardiac event, I understood the watchman simile differently than I had before. The long night — the uncertainty, the slow recovery, the not-yet-knowing what the future held — was its own kind of darkness. Not the darkness of guilt or shame, but the darkness of limitation, of the body’s sudden visibility, of the future’s sudden contingency. And the watching for morning — the taut, stretched waiting of qavah — was what the recovery required. Not certainty that everything would be restored. But the watchman’s knowledge: morning comes. The sun does not fail to rise. Wait for it.

The movement from personal cry to communal address — O Israel, hope in the LORD — is the movement the cardiac event eventually made possible. The person who has waited through a long night and seen the morning has something to say to those still in the dark. Not advice. Not a programme. Just the watchman’s word: I have been at this wall through the night, and I can tell you that the morning comes. Wait for it.

Sit with this:

Where are you in the psalm’s movement — still in the depths, or in the waiting, or beginning to turn toward the community with the watchman’s word?

There is no wrong answer. The psalm holds all three positions. What it does not permit is the pretence that the depths are not real, or that the morning is not coming, or that the person who has waited has nothing to say to those still watching.

Out of the depths, the voice goes up. That is enough to begin.

This psalm is one of the fifteen Songs of Ascent; the whole ascent is walked in Songs for the Road.
