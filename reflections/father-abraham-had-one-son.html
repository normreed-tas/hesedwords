12 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Father Abraham Had One Son

When I first came to faith, we sang a lot of choruses at church. They were short and catchy, and we sang them over and over again. One of them came with actions.

I can still see it. Nearly a hundred people turning around, arms pumping, feet stomping, singing at the top of their voices. The song was about Father Abraham and his many sons. And then came the part we sang loudest, every one of us claiming to be one of those sons.

I sang it often. I never stopped to ask who Abraham’s promise was actually made to.

Paul wrote about that same promise in a letter to the churches in Galatia, in what is now Turkey. This is what he said:

Now the promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. He doesn’t say, “To descendants”, as of many, but as of one, “To your offspring”, which is Christ. (Galatians 3:16)

The English uses two different words here, offspring and descendants. Paul’s Greek uses one word for both. The word is sperma, which means seed. The first time it is plural: seeds. The second time it is singular: seed. God promised Abraham a seed, and Paul says that seed is one person. The person is Christ.

You might think Paul has made a mistake. “Seed” can mean one child or a whole family line, the same way “offspring” can in English. Paul knows that. Thirteen verses later he uses the same word for a whole crowd of people:

If you are Christ’s, then you are Abraham’s offspring and heirs according to promise. (Galatians 3:29)

So Paul is not arguing about grammar. He is pointing at a person.

A few verses later Paul says it again. He is explaining why God gave the law through Moses, and he says the law was only meant to last for a while:

…until the offspring should come to whom the promise has been made. (Galatians 3:19)

Put verse 16 and verse 19 together, and the promise has two names on it: Abraham and Christ. My name is not on it, and neither is yours.

Long before Paul, God made this promise to Abraham in a strange way. Genesis 15 tells the story.

God told Abram (his name at the time) to bring some animals and cut them in half. Abram laid the halves in two rows with a path between them. In those days this was one way of sealing a solemn agreement. The people making it walked down the path between the pieces. It was like saying, “If I break my word, let this happen to me.”

But Abram did not walk down the path.

When the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram. (Genesis 15:12)

…a smoking furnace and a flaming torch passed between these pieces. (Genesis 15:17)

Abram was asleep. God went through alone.

Centuries later, the prophet Micah looked back on God’s promises to Abraham:

You will give truth to Jacob, and hesed to Abraham, as you have sworn to our fathers from the days of old. (Micah 7:20)

Hesed is the Hebrew word for God’s loyal love, the kind of love that keeps its word. God swore it to Abraham, and God kept it himself.

If the man the promise was spoken to was asleep while it was made, then we were nowhere near it. We were never asked to sign it. It was never ours to keep.

People sometimes say, “Abraham’s promise is mine.” Paul says something that sounds almost the same, but the order is different:

If you are Christ’s, then you are Abraham’s offspring and heirs according to promise. (Galatians 3:29)

First you belong to Christ. Then the inheritance comes. Paul does not say the promise belongs to you. He says you belong to Christ.

That order mattered in Galatia. Some teachers there were telling the new Christians who were not Jewish that they had to become Jewish first before they could count as Abraham’s children. They talked as if the promise was theirs, and as if it was up to them to decide who else could have it. Paul answered them:

There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus. (Galatians 3:28)

Jesus told a story about the same problem. A younger son took his share of the family money, wasted it, and came home with nothing. His father ran to meet him and threw a party. The older brother stayed outside, angry. This is what he said to his father:

…you never gave me a goat, that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours came… you killed the fattened calf for him. (Luke 15:29–30)

He talks about himself and his friends. Then he calls his brother “this son of yours.” He will not say “my brother.”

The father answers him:

Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. But it was appropriate to celebrate and be glad, for this brother of yours was dead, and is alive again. (Luke 15:31–32)

Everything the father owned already belonged to the older son. He had lost nothing. What he would not accept was that his brother came with it.

“Abraham’s promise is mine” can be true. But it is easy to say it the way the older brother did, with a fence around it. The promise is not mine. I am Christ’s, and so is the person standing next to me.

I still think about that crowd, turning and stomping and singing that we were Abraham’s many sons. We were not wrong that we belonged. Galatians 3:29 says we do. But we sang it as if the promise came straight to each of us, one by one, a hundred times over.

Paul says it came to one. Abraham had many children, but the promise was made to the one who would come from him.

If I sang it now, I would change one word. Father Abraham had one Son.

The rest of us are in the family because we are in him.

Scripture quotations are from the World English Bible. Italics in a quotation mark a word changed for readability: hesed for “mercy” in Micah 7:20, and “son of yours” and “brother of yours” in Luke 15.
