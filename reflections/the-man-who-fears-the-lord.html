The Man Who Fears the Lord

Psalm 111 and Psalm 112 sit next to each other, and they are built the same way. Each one has twenty-two lines, and each line begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet — aleph, then bet, then gimel, all the way to tav. Two poems of exactly the same length, made to exactly the same pattern.

Psalm 111 is about God. It is his works that are great, his righteousness that stands forever, his covenant that he remembers, his name that is holy.

Psalm 112 is about a man. Blessed is the man who fears Yahweh. And from there the psalm describes him: his children, his house, his lending, his giving, his steady heart, his lack of fear when bad news comes.

One about God, one about a man, written to the same skeleton and set side by side.

Read them together and the same words keep turning up in both.

Righteousness צְדָקָה

Psalm 111:3, of God: his righteousness stands forever.

Psalm 112:3, of the man: his righteousness stands forever.

Not similar. The same three words in both. And because the two psalms follow the alphabet, they fall on the same letter, in the same place in the poem. Then it comes back again at 112:9.

Gracious and merciful חַנּוּן וְרַחוּם

Psalm 111:4, of God: Yahweh is gracious and merciful.

Psalm 112:4, of the man: gracious and merciful.

Upheld סָמוּךְ

Psalm 111:8, of God's precepts: they are upheld forever.

Psalm 112:8, of the man: his heart is upheld.

Gave נָתַן

Psalm 111:5, of God: he gave food to those who fear him.

Psalm 112:9, of the man: he scattered, he gave to the poor.

God gives to the man. The man gives to the poor.

Gracious and merciful is not a phrase either psalm invented. It comes from Exodus 34, where God passes in front of Moses and says who he is: merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abundant in hesed.

Psalm 111 takes the first two words, turns them around, and stops. Psalm 112 takes the same two words and stops again.

That pair of words turns up ten times in the Old Testament. Seven of those ten go on to say slow to anger and abundant in hesed. Psalm 111 and Psalm 112 are two of the three that do not. The third is a prayer in Nehemiah 9.

Hesed does not appear in either psalm.

Hesed is love that keeps its commitments — the kind you can rely on, that turns up and does something. Psalm 112 never uses the word. It just shows a man doing it. He is good, and he lends. He provisions his affairs with justice. He scatters, he gives to the poor.

Psalm 112:4 starts plainly. Light dawns in the darkness for the upright.

Then three words: gracious, and merciful, and righteous.

Gracious and merciful — the two words from Psalm 111, the two words from Exodus 34. But here they are just sitting there. The Hebrew does not say whose they are. There is no name and no verb to join them to anything. They could describe the light that comes up in the dark. They could describe the man it comes up for.

Translators have had to make a choice about who is being spoken about here — whether these words describe Yahweh, as they do in Psalm 111, or the man of Psalm 112. They do not all agree.

So here is where the two psalms touch. One psalm is about God. One is about a man. And at this verse the poem stops telling you which of them it is talking about.

Psalm 112 opens by saying this is a man who fears Yahweh. Then it tells you what he is like.

He is gracious and merciful and righteous. He lends. He gives to the poor. He handles his affairs justly. His heart is steady and he is not frightened when bad news comes.

That is the whole description. The psalm never says he avoids anything. It never mentions temptation, or sin, or the things he keeps away from.

Ask most people what it means to fear God and you will get a list of things a person does not do. Psalm 112 answers the same question with a list of things he does.

Charles Sheldon's book has been in Christian hands for more than a century. For a while the question was on wristbands and bumper stickers and everything else, and plenty of us meant it. It is a good question. You are standing in front of a decision and you ask what Jesus would do in your place, and then you go and do that.

Psalm 112 is not asking that question. It is not giving instructions at all. It is a description. Here is a man; this is what he is like. Set beside Psalm 111, it is a portrait of someone who has come to look like the God he stands in front of.

The difference matters because you can do the right thing for a long time without being that man. The psalm's list is a list of actions, and actions can be produced.

Selah

David is a friend now. He was not always a friend. For a long time he was a project.

His wife was in hospital at New Norfolk, about thirty minutes from the church, and I drove out there with him most weeks. Sometimes he drove. The hospitalisation put a strain on the marriage that it did not survive, and when she was discharged she went to live with her mother. David stayed in the house. Most Sundays I picked him up in the church bus.

I never used the phrase "What would Jesus do?" about David, but it was underneath most of it. David was a project, and I was working on him.

Then David started driving up to the house to see me, almost daily, usually at the worst possible time. His car had a loud muffler and you could hear it coming up the road a long way off.

One day I heard it and I hid. I went into the study, shut the curtains, and waited for him to give up and go home.

He did not give up. When he could not raise me he went next door. My neighbour, who went to our church, assumed I would want to see him, and knocked, and called out, and when that did not work he rang my phone.

I made an excuse and went out to meet David, so that my neighbour would not get the wrong idea.

What I was doing for David was not coming from my heart. It was coming from somewhere else, and I had told myself for a long time that it was what Jesus would have done.

That day was like going through God's x-ray machine, and I saw what was actually inside me.

One Sunday I told the church about it. David was sitting in front of me while I told it, and I apologised to him.
