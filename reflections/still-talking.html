Psalm 94:16–19
7 Sep 2026
Norm Reed

Still Talking

Some people hurt other people and get away with it. Not once. Over and over, and nothing happens to them.

The song says who they hurt. Widows. People who have come from somewhere else and don’t have anyone here. Children with no parents. The ones with nobody to stand up for them.

And the people doing it say something out loud. They say: God isn’t looking. He doesn’t notice things like this.

The man singing this song has been watching them.

He knows they’re wrong about God. But he looks at what’s in front of him, and it’s the same thing they’re looking at. The people are still being hurt. The ones doing it are still doing well.

So he shouts at God. How long? How long do they get to keep going? How long do they get to be pleased with themselves?

God doesn’t answer that.

Have you ever asked for something to be fixed, and nothing happened?

Then he starts arguing.

God made ears, he says. Do you think he can’t hear? He made eyes. Do you think he can’t see?

It’s a good argument. If someone built the thing you hear with, they can hear.

But listen to who he’s saying it to. The people doing the hurting aren’t there. They can’t hear him. He’s saying it out loud anyway, over and over, like someone who needs to hear himself say it.

Because he nearly agreed with them.

So he asks for help.

Not from God this time. He looks around at everybody else and says it straight out: who is going to stand up for me? Who is going to get up and do something about these people?

And nobody does.

The song doesn’t say anyone came. It doesn’t say his friends turned up, or someone important took his side. He asks, and the next thing in the song is not an answer.

Here is what he says next.

When I thought, “My foot slips,” your steadfast love, O LORD, held me up. (Psalm 94:18)

He says it out loud. Not I fell over — he hasn’t fallen. He’s the person who feels their foot start to go and knows what’s coming. Anyone who has ever been on wet rocks knows the feeling. There’s a moment where you know.

And he tells someone. That’s the part worth noticing. He doesn’t wait until he’s down. He says the thing while it’s happening.

The word under steadfast love is hesed. It means love that doesn’t let go. Not love that feels warm about you — love that stays, and keeps staying, when there’s no reason left to.

Look at what it does here. It doesn’t get rid of the people hurting the widows. They’re still there. Nobody stood up for him. Nothing got fixed.

He just didn’t go down.

You’d think the song would stop there.

It doesn’t. The next thing he says is that he’s still worried. Lots of worries, all at once, the way they come. Being held up didn’t empty his head.

And the people hurting the widows are still doing it. Right to the last line of the song, they’re still there. He says God will deal with them one day. One day. Not yet.

So nothing in this song gets fixed.

He’s still standing, and he’s still talking.

Selah

I sat with a man yesterday who was about to give up.

I met him in prison. That was fourteen years ago and we’re still talking. He’s kind, and he gets misunderstood a lot, which is a hard way to live.

He opens his house to people coming out of prison and people with nowhere to go. He feeds them.

Yesterday he went through the list. His car has been stolen. His phone was stolen last week. There’s money owing to him he won’t see. All of it from people he took in.

He said, I just don’t want to do this anymore.

Then he sat there for a bit. And he started talking about what God has done for him. And he said he’d keep going.

His car is still gone. Nobody is bringing his phone back. He didn’t get fixed.

He’s still standing, and his door is still open.
