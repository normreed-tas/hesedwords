2 Chronicles 20
18 Aug 2026
Norm Reed

What They Sang

I read 2 Chronicles 20 this morning and knew it before I started.

Jehoshaphat gets word that three armies are coming up from the south. He is afraid, and he says so. He calls a fast, the whole of Judah gathers in the court of the temple, and he prays out loud in front of them — a prayer that ends with a line I have quoted more times than I could count. We have no might against this great company that cometh against us; neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee. Then a Levite named Jahaziel speaks. They get up early the next morning and go out toward the wilderness of Tekoa. By the time they arrive the enemy is already dead in the field, and it takes them three days to carry off what is left. They call the place the Valley of Blessing.

I have preached that. I could tell it with the book shut.

What has always held me is Jehoshaphat himself. He is one of the few kings the Chronicler can say something good about, and what he is good at is trying. He sends teachers out through the towns with the book. He appoints judges and tells them plainly whose judgement they are handing down. He gets it wrong often enough — the business with Ahab, the ships at Ezion-geber — and he keeps turning back. There is something in that I have always wanted for myself: not a man who got it right, a man who kept trying to get it right.

And the prayer is the part everyone remembers, and rightly. We do not know what to do, but our eyes are upon thee. You can pray that in a hospital corridor.

So I have always read the middle of the chapter as the setting for those two things — the king and the prayer — and moved through it quickly. The middle of the chapter is where he appoints the singers.

He puts them in front of the army. Not behind it, where you would keep the choir. Ahead of the soldiers, on the road, first into the valley where three armies are waiting. And the Chronicler is careful about the order of events: when they began to sing and to praise, the LORD set ambushments.

Except this morning I stopped and saw what they actually sang.

It is one line. That is the entire song the Chronicler records.

Praise the LORD; for his mercy endureth for ever.

I have read that as a stage direction. Singers sang something suitably devotional; the ambush followed. But it is not a stage direction, it is a quotation, and the Chronicler has quoted it before. It is what they sang when the ark came up to Jerusalem. It is what they sang at the dedication of the temple, when the house filled with glory and the priests could not stand up in it. It is the refrain of Psalm 136, twenty-six times over. When Judah's singers walked out ahead of the army, they sang the line Judah always sang. They did not compose something for the occasion. They reached for the sentence that had been true the longest.

And the word in the middle of it is hesed.

Not mercy, quite, though every English Bible has to choose something. Coverdale had to build a word for it — loving kindness — because English did not have one. Hesed is the loyalty that will not let go, covenant faithfulness with skin on it, the sort of commitment that keeps showing up when the other party has given it no reason to. Ki le'olam hasdo. For his hesed is for ever.

Look at what the song does not say. It does not ask for anything. There is no petition in it, no plea for rescue, no bargaining, not even a request that God act. It says nothing at all about the outcome. It is a sentence about the character of God, sung by people walking toward an army that outnumbered them, who had no idea whether they would be alive that afternoon.

Since my heart stopped I have not been able to leave hesed alone. When everything is taken away from you, all you have to depend on is the loving kindness of God. I know that now. I did not know it when I was preaching this chapter.

And it makes the song harder than I had understood it to be. What I want to say is that God brought me through, and he did. But that is not what the singers said. They sang before the valley, not after it. If the three armies had come over the ridge that morning and cut them down where they stood, the line would still have been true. His hesed endureth for ever. That sentence does not depend on my heart starting again.

That is what I had never seen. It is not a battle strategy. It is a refusal to have one.

Selah

It is a little over six months since my heart stopped. The other day the specialist gave me the all clear, and I am thankful for it.

But the all clear is not what I have learned. What I have come to over these six months is the thing Jehoshaphat's singers knew on the road to Tekoa: that whether it had turned out to my benefit or not, God's loving kindness endures for ever. When God told Moses his own name, this was the word he used about himself. It is not a small word to lose. I do not think Scripture could stand without it.
