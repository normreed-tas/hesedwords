A Hesedwords Reflection

Called in Righteousness

A reflection on Isaiah 42 : 6

Norm Reed · 1 May 2026

What follows began not with a commentary but with an admission. I have sat with the word righteousness for over forty years of ministry — preaching it, teaching it, building theology on it — and never quite trusting that I understood it.

I have never really understood the word righteousness.

That is not a comfortable thing for a pastor to say. The word is everywhere. It runs through the Psalms and the prophets, through Paul's letters, through the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus names it alongside the kingdom of God — seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness. It is not a marginal term. It may be the most important term.

And yet something has never felt right about the way the tradition handed it to me.

The word that shaped me was a legal word.

Righteousness meant right standing before God. It meant moral conformity to a divine standard. It meant the status of the believer declared righteous through faith. These are not wrong ideas. But somewhere in the translation — Hebrew to Greek, Greek to Latin, Latin to English — something was quietly lost.

The Hebrew word is tzedakah. And tzedakah is not primarily a legal word. It is a relational word. It describes what a relationship looks like when it is working as it was designed to — when each party is being what the bond requires them to be. A king is righteous when he governs in ways that sustain the community he is responsible for. A judge is righteous when he rules in ways that restore broken relationship between parties. God is righteous precisely in his saving acts. Not despite them. Because of them.

The saving is the righteous thing to do.

This is why the Psalms can speak of God's righteousness and his salvation in the same breath without any sense of tension. They are not two different things. They are the same reality named from two angles.

I was reading Isaiah 42 when three words stopped me.

I am the Lord; I have called you in righteousness; I will take hold of your hand and keep you; I will give you as a covenant to the people, as a light to the nations.

Isaiah 42 : 6 · ESV

Three Hebrew words: qerā'tîkā betsèdeq. I called you. In righteousness. The small preposition be- is doing the weight-bearing work. It means in, or within, or from. The call does not arise from the servant's merit. It does not arise from any external pressure on God. It arises from within his own relational-righteous character.

God does not call the righteous. He calls from within his own righteousness.

This felt like a key turning in a lock I had been carrying for a long time without knowing what it opened.

God does not call the righteous.
He calls from within his own righteousness.

I found myself trying to say it simply.

God desires relationship. Righteousness describes the sort of relationship God has. God actively seeks to address the lack of righteousness in those he seeks relationship with. And he establishes the means.

Each move is load-bearing.

The first says something about God's nature — not that he wants relationship in addition to being who he is, but that relationship is what his nature looks like in motion. The second says righteousness is not a standard applied to the relationship from outside. It describes the quality of the relationship itself. The third names what God does about the gap — he moves toward it, takes it seriously, and does something about it from his side. The fourth says the means is not a mechanism or a transaction. It is a person.

Which is why Isaiah 42:6 ends where it ends: I will give you as a covenant to the people, as a light to the nations. Not one who brings the light. One who is the light.

I have spent many years working alongside people who carry the weight of being defined by their deficit.

People in prison. Their families waiting outside. Children who have grown up with a parent on the other side of a barrier. The question that drives the work is not primarily what did they do? The question is what is the relationship that has been severed, and what would it take to restore it?

That is a tzedakah question.

When I read Isaiah 42:6 now, I do not read it primarily as a text about the Messiah, or about Israel, or about a distant theological category called righteousness. I read it as the ground of the work. The call to the servant issues from within God's own relational-righteous character. The servant is given not as a message but as a means. And the means is personal — a held hand, a kept life, a covenant embodied.

The bruised reed and the smouldering wick in the verses just before this phrase are not illustrations. They are the address list.

For Reflection
  1. Where have you understood righteousness primarily as your standing before God, rather than as the character of God's relationship moving toward you?
  2. What would change in the way you read the prophets if righteousness were heard as a relational word rather than a legal one?
  3. Who in your community is defined primarily by their deficit? What does it mean that God calls from within his righteousness toward that deficit?
