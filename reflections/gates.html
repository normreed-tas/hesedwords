Psalm 9:13–14
21 Nov 2026
Norm Reed

Gates

Today is my wedding anniversary. Forty-five years.

This morning I was reading Psalm 9, and I stopped at this:

O you who lift me up from the gates of death,
that I may recount all your praises,
that in the gates of the daughter of Zion
I may rejoice in your salvation.

O you who lift me up from the gates of death.

For a lot of people that isn’t a real verse. It’s poetry, a way of saying things got very bad. But when you’ve been in that place, you read it differently. On the thirteenth of February my heart stopped. I died. Reading that line this morning, I thought: that’s me.

In the Old Testament, death is sometimes pictured as a place with gates. God asks Job whether the gates of death have ever been shown to him. Hezekiah, sick and expecting to die, says that in the middle of his days he is being sent to the gates of Sheol. You don’t come back out through those gates. That’s the whole point of them.

The psalm says God lifts a man up from there. I was taken to those gates, and I was lifted up from them.

Since then, every day has been an extra day.

While I was recovering I wasn’t allowed to drive for six months. People were kind about it. Poor you, they said. That must be frustrating, all those restrictions. But I kept thinking: if that day had gone differently, there would be nothing I could do. Nothing at all. One restriction, that I can’t drive, and I can do everything else. Isn’t that amazing?

Since February my granddaughter has been born in England. I flew to Cairns for my grandson’s first birthday. I’ve met a lot of people. When I look back over these nine months, so much has happened, and there has been so much joy I would never have known.

Today is another anniversary I wasn’t supposed to get.

But the verse doesn’t stop at the first gate. It goes on to another one.

that I may recount all your praises, that in the gates of the daughter of Zion I may rejoice in your salvation.

In an Old Testament town, the gate was where everybody was. It was the market and the meeting place. The elders sat there, and that was where disputes were settled. When Boaz wanted to marry Ruth, he went to the gate, because that was where things were done in front of everyone. Whatever you said at the gate, the whole town heard.

So God lifts a man up from the gates of death, and the next place the psalm puts him is the gates of Zion, telling people. He doesn’t stay in his room. He goes out in public.

The word “that” in the middle is doing a lot of work. He lifts me up that I may recount, that I may rejoice. The psalm doesn’t say I must. It says this is what the lifting was for.

The word for salvation there is yeshu’ah. Jesus’ name comes from it. So this morning, when I say, “Thank you, Jesus, for saving me,” I’m very nearly saying the verse.

I have so much to celebrate. I knew that before this morning, but the psalm reminded me. It also reminded me that salvation isn’t something you keep to yourself. It’s meant to be celebrated where people meet: in church on a Sunday, and out in the community the rest of the week.

Forty-five years today. Nine months of extra days. I want to say thank you where people can hear it.
