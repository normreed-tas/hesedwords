God Seeks What Has Been Driven Away

A morning with Ecclesiastes 3

There are mornings when a passage of Scripture that you have read many times suddenly opens differently.

Ecclesiastes 3. A text I know well — or thought I did. Fourteen pairs of opposites. The famous list. A time for this, a time for that. Familiar enough that I have probably read past it more than I have read it.

This morning I read it slowly.

And something that I have been trying to understand for most of my adult life — what time actually means, not as a concept but as something we live inside — began, quietly, to take a different shape.

A Morning with the Teacher

I found myself reading the passage again — this time as I might explain it to a child.

Have you ever noticed that life has different seasons? Sometimes things are happy. Sometimes things are sad. Sometimes things end, and sometimes things begin.

The Teacher looks at life and makes a list. There is a time to be born and a time to die. A time to laugh and a time to cry. A time to hold on and a time to let go. He lists fourteen pairs — all the opposite experiences that make up a human life.

And here’s the important thing: none of them are wrong. The sad times are not mistakes. The hard times are not accidents. Every season has its place. Even the difficult ones belong.

Deep inside every person there is a longing for something bigger than what we can see. A dog doesn’t lie awake wondering what life is for. But people do. That longing is something God placed inside us on purpose.

And the last thing — perhaps the most surprising. God doesn’t forget what gets lost. People who have been pushed aside, driven away, forgotten — God goes looking for them. Nothing that matters to God simply disappears.

The Longing Inside

As a young man, I never thought I would die.

I suspect most of us don’t. Death exists — we know this the way we know about distant places. Real, but not here. Not today. It belongs to the far end of a very long road, and the road ahead still looks long.

Then something happens. Someone close to us dies and the distance collapses. Or a doctor uses a particular tone. Or a morning arrives that we did not expect to see.

For me it was a cardiac arrest. One moment ordinary life. The next, everything I had assumed about the future became suddenly, bodily uncertain. Time, which had always stretched comfortably ahead, revealed itself as finite. Not a concept. A fact about me, personally.

I think about pets in this connection — particularly the ones we give to children. A dog. A cat. Creatures whose lives are short by design. I have come to believe this is not incidental. The lifespan of a beloved animal is one of the ways children first learn that things end. That the warmth of a particular creature, once gone, is gone. A gentle classroom for a lesson that has no gentle version.

But here is what I notice. The reason mortality feels like loss — genuinely, achingly like loss — is precisely because of the olam Qoheleth describes. We carry eternity in our hearts, and so when time takes something from us it doesn’t feel like a natural process. It feels like a wrong. An interruption. Something that should not have the last word.

Mortality is the cost of carrying eternity inside you.

The animal does not experience it this way. But we do. And that ache for something time cannot provide is not a design flaw. It is a clue.

Driven Away

Verse fifteen is the verse I cannot stop returning to.

That which is, already has been; that which is to be, already is; and God seeks what has been driven away. Ecclesiastes 3:15

The first two clauses establish something about time under God’s sovereignty. Nothing is truly new. Nothing is truly unprecedented. Time, for God, does not move the way it moves for us — in one direction, carrying things away as it goes.

And then the third clause lands.

And God seeks what has been driven away.

The Hebrew word is nirdaf — from the root radaf, to pursue, to hunt, to chase. It is not a gentle word. It carries force. The nirdaf is the one pushed from their place by something outside themselves. Not someone who wandered. Someone who was driven.

God seeks that person out.

This is a statement about the character of God — what he does, where his attention goes. And it runs as a thread through the whole of Scripture.

In Ezekiel 34, God indicts the shepherds of Israel who scattered the flock and drove away the weak and wounded, and declares:

I myself will search for my sheep. Ezekiel 34:11

In Luke 15, Jesus answers the Pharisees who grumble that he eats with sinners — with three stories about going after what is lost. The sheep. The coin. The son. In John 9, he gives sight to a man born blind, and the religious authorities throw the man out. Drive him away. Immediately — the very next chapter — Jesus responds:

I am the Good Shepherd. I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly. John 10:11, 10

The thread is unbroken. From Qoheleth to Ezekiel to Luke to John.

God seeks the driven-away. The person the system marked and moved on from. The one reduced to a case number. The season of life that felt like pure loss and nothing more.

This is God’s current occupation.

Not managing the comfortable. Not administering the settled. Going after the one pushed from their place.

God seeks what has been driven away.

· · ·

The question was never really about what time means philosophically. It was about what time does to us. What it takes. What it leaves behind.

Qoheleth’s answer, on an ordinary morning, was this: time does not have the last word. The seasons are real — all fourteen pairs of them. The losses are real. What has been driven away has genuinely gone.

But God seeks it out.

Nobody driven away is beyond his reach.
