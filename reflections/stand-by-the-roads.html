A Hesedwords Reflection

Stand by the Roads

A reflection on Jeremiah 6:16

✦   ✦   ✦

Norman Reed · June 2026

Somewhere in the middle of a judgment speech, God interrupts himself.

The charges against Jerusalem have been building for chapters. The indictment is specific. The consequences named. And then — without warning, inserted into the oracle — a single verse:

Stand by the roads. Look. Ask for the ancient paths, where the good way is. Walk in them. And you will find rest for your souls.

Jeremiah 6:16

One verse. Then the judgment speech continues.

Most readers pass over it. I find I cannot.

The invitation begins not with walking but with standing. The crossroads — the place where paths diverge, where the pressure to choose is greatest — and the word is: stop. Not yet. Look first.

There is something countercultural in that instruction. We bring urgency to our crossroads. The demand to decide, to act, to move in some direction, is relentless. But the ancient word offered here is different. Stillness before movement. Looking before walking. The asking precedes the going.

And what you are looking for, the verse says, are paths that have been worn by generations of faithful walking. Not novelty. Not the latest direction. The ancient paths — netivot olam — roads proven across time because they are grounded in what is timelessly true.

What is promised to those who find them is not certainty. Not safety. It is rest for your souls. The rest not of exhaustion but of arrival — of finding yourself, at last, where you were made to be.

The crossroads is not the problem. The crossroads is the invitation. Stand here. Look. Ask which way the faithful have walked before you.

Something in this stopped me. Not the judgment — I have read Jeremiah’s judgment speeches before. The standing. The instruction to stand before walking. I am not sure I do that as well as I once thought I did.

That question, taken seriously, opens into something larger — into the heart of what the prophets are most deeply doing, and into the God whose grief is visible even in the act of judgment.
