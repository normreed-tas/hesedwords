Understanding?

Norm Reed

I was reading Psalm 49 this morning when the funeral came back to me.

Not the whole service, just fragments. The state ceremony. The accolades. The news clips cycling through his life: the radio voice, the senator, the outspoken man, the figure who had risen to prominence and then, suddenly, was gone. The kind of man for whom a city stages a farewell.

And then verse 16 of the psalm opened, and I couldn't look away:

Do not be afraid when a man becomes rich, when the glory of his house increases. For when he dies he will carry nothing away; his glory will not go down after him.

The verse keeps moving. Though while he lives he counts himself blessed, and though men praise him when he does well for himself, his soul will go to the generation of his fathers, and he will never again see light.

And then the last line stops everything: man in his pomp, yet without understanding, is like the beasts that perish.

That word, understanding, caught me. Not intelligence. Not achievement or prominence or the ability to move a room. Understanding.

The knowing I keep coming back to is da'at — the intimate knowing of God. The word that runs through Hosea: I desire hesed and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings. The same knowing Jeremiah points to when he says of Josiah's father: he judged the cause of the poor and needy — is not this to know me? Knowing God and doing justice are the same reality in two registers.

So the man of Psalm 49, full of glory and achievement, departs without it. Without the knowing that turns everything outward: toward God, toward mercy, toward the neighbour. The wealth accumulated. The name was remembered. And he left having understood nothing of the only understanding that matters.

Not to know about God. But to know God in such a way that it changes how you live. The poor fed. The widow defended. The prisoner visited. The child held. The knowing that is not in the head but in the hands.

That is what was missing at the ceremony. Not intelligence.

And so the question remains: What does it mean to understand?
