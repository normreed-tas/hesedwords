You Should!

A companion piece on the weight of what we say: Idle Words.

I have a friend called James. We got to know each other when he joined the church nearly ten years ago, and not long after that he and his wife came onto our leadership team. Between them they brought a great deal of experience, and they model what it looks like to live a Christian life.

Their life is not free of trouble. It is what they do with the trouble. They find something true in God, they live it out, and other people start wanting to live it out too.

Ever since he came onto the team, James has challenged me. Not by opposing my decisions — he sends me back to Jesus to work out whether what I am hearing is God or a bad case of indigestion. More often than not it is the indigestion.

And like the James in the Bible, he keeps making me look at my words. There is one phrase he pounces on every single time.

You should.

Selah

I used it again this morning, on the phone to my son. He is grown, with a family of his own, living in the UK and holding down a very responsible job. Somewhere in the middle of the conversation I started telling him what he should do.

It did not go as well as it could have, and I heard James correcting me in my ears.

I have apologised. He will read it when he wakes up over there.

I was being his dad, giving advice he had not asked for. He heard a stone being thrown.

They brought her to him with everything already decided. They said her sin out loud, in front of the crowd, and they had the law ready and the stones in their hands. All they wanted from Jesus was his agreement.

He knelt down and wrote in the dust.

When he stood up he said one sentence, and it was not about her. Let the one without sin throw first. Then he knelt down again.

Notice what he did not say to the men.

He knew. He knew what was in every one of them, because they walked away — you do not put a stone down over a sentence unless the sentence has found something. He could have named it. He could have said which of them had no business being there, said it in front of the whole crowd, and been right.

He said nothing. Not one name. He did not tell them what they had done or what they should do about it. He let each man work it out standing there with a rock in his hand, and then let him leave without being watched out the door.

One by one they went, until it was just the two of them.

And here is the part I had not noticed. When he finally spoke to her, he did not tell her what her sin was. He did not use the word they had used. He did not say, what you did with that man was wrong. He said, go, and sin no more. Go — and as you go, don't.

He gave her a road. They had given her a label.

Once you have seen that, it is worth counting how few people Jesus ever told what they should do.

He said follow me — but he said it to people standing in front of him who wanted to come. He said go and do likewise — but the man had asked him a question and would not let it go.

When somebody came wanting a ruling on someone else's life, he never once gave one. Two brothers arguing over their father's money. A lawyer wanting to know exactly who counted as his neighbour. A crowd holding rocks. Every time he handed the question back — a story, another question, or the dust.

And the word that carries the weight, must, he used about himself more than about anyone else. The Son of Man must suffer. I must be about my Father's business. I must go on my way today and tomorrow and the day after. He picked the must up and carried it.

He did lay it on people sometimes — but look where it landed. On the men who had appointed themselves judges: these you ought to have done, he told the Pharisees, and he meant justice and mercy. On a woman bent double for eighteen years — ought she not be loosed? And after that it is prayer, and worship, and what to say when they put you on trial. Not once does he pick the word up and set it on the back of somebody whose life he had been asked to rule on.

James does not tell me I am wrong. That is not what he does. He asks the question that sends me off to find out for myself, and then he leaves me to it.

Which is how I ended up this morning standing in a courtyard in my own head, with a stone still in my hand, working out where it came from.

I will probably say it again next week.
