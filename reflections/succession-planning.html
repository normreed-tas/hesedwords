Reflection
1 Chronicles 22–29
15 Aug 2026
Norm Reed

Succession Planning — Everything Set in Place

I read the last chapters of Chronicles this morning. Twenty-five to twenty-nine, the end of David's life, and it is mostly lists.

Twenty-four courses of singers, cast by lot, the pupil alongside the teacher. Gatekeepers assigned east, north, south and west, and to the storehouses. Treasurers over the dedicated things. Officers and judges. Twelve divisions of the army, twenty-four thousand men each, one for every month of the year. Overseers for the vineyards, the olive trees, the herds in Sharon, the camels and the donkeys.

Then the plans. The porch, the treasuries, the upper rooms, the inner chambers. The weight of gold for each lampstand and its lamps. The weight of silver for each table. The weight of the gold for the altar of incense.

Everything set in place. Every detail. The man could not build it himself and he made certain that nothing his son needed was missing.

And he did not stop at the plans. Before the specifications are produced, he turns to Solomon and speaks to him directly.

Know the God of your father and serve him with a whole heart and a willing mind.

That comes first. Then the buildings, the gold, the silver, the courses.

And at the very end, in front of the whole assembly, he prays it back to God.

Grant to Solomon my son a whole heart, that he may keep your commandments, your testimonies and your statutes.

A whole heart. Lev shalem. A heart that is entire, undivided, all of it pointed the same way.

So the handover was complete twice over. Complete on paper, and complete in the charge. Whatever else you say about David, he did not hand his son a system and leave the rest to look after itself.

Turn to Kings and you find the same word again.

For when Solomon was old his wives turned away his heart after other gods, and his heart was not whole with the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father.

Not whole. Lo shalem. The word from the prayer, negated.

Chronicles records what David asked for. Kings records what happened.

Any successful pastor could build a leadership seminar out of those chapters. Transitions done properly. A manual on how to hand over a church. He planned, he provisioned, he wrote it down, he handed it over.

That is how we read Scripture. We find a man doing something well, and because it is in the Bible we take it as prescribed.

But sometimes we stop reading too soon. And sometimes what we are looking at is not a model at all. It is an account — of what a man did, and of what came of it — and the seeds of what came of it are lying in the passage we have just admired.

The seed is in David's own reason, and he says it twice.

Solomon my son is young and inexperienced, and the house that is to be built for the LORD must be exceedingly magnificent, of fame and glory throughout all lands. I will therefore make preparation for it.

The boy is young, so I will do it for him.

Note what the house has to be. Not faithful, not sufficient, not according to the pattern — exceedingly magnificent, of fame and glory throughout all lands. That is the design brief, and it is the reason for the hundred thousand talents of gold and the million talents of silver and the bronze and iron beyond weighing. And when he has listed it all, he tells his son: To these you must add.

Then, years later, at the end of his life, standing in front of the whole assembly, he says it again. Solomon my son, whom alone God has chosen, is young and inexperienced, and the work is great.

He is not a boy any more. He is about to be king. And he is still being introduced as young and inexperienced, in public, by his father.

There is a way of preparing everything for a man that tells him what you think he is capable of.

I don't say David was wrong. Chapter twenty-eight says he had the plan in writing from the hand of the LORD, and I am not going to argue with that. The temple was built. It was built exactly as it was drawn.

But the temple is not the only thing he handed over.

Twenty years ago I was driving, with the phone on speaker, and I yelled at my senior minister.

I didn't swear. I didn't say anything bad. I just lost it.

When I got home I sat on the bed in my study and burst into tears.

Something was wrong. I could feel it and I could not name it.

A friend came round that week and prayed with me, and we sat down together and made some changes. We took things off the list. We moved some responsibilities. We made the load easier to carry.

They were band-aids. The problem was underneath and none of it went near the problem.

I had been an administrator all my life. I learned it in the navy, as an officer, where it is the whole job.

As a missionary I ran operations on a mission station of three hundred people, with five hundred churches and the training centres attached to them.

When I returned to Australia I became executive pastor of a large church, and later national coordinator for our movement.

I took the jobs other people couldn't make work and I made them work, and I was proud of it.

So when something broke in me, I reached for the only tool I trusted. I reorganised.

My heart had gone calloused. I had reduced everything I cared about to a to-do list, and there were never enough hours in the day for the list, and never enough resources for what was being asked. I could not have told you that at the time. It has taken twenty years to be able to say it in a sentence.

Years later the opportunity came to care for a church in Tasmania, and I came to it knowing what I did not want.

I did not want the rosters. I did not want a building project. I did not want a full calendar, or a set of key performance indicators, or a mortgage that dictated what we could and could not do for the next fifteen years.

I wanted to care for people. I wanted to be a pastor.

That was not neglect. I knew by then what a full framework does to the man inside it.

Solomon did not lose his wisdom.

He asked for it at Gibeon and God was pleased with the asking and gave it to him, and Scripture says so plainly. He built the temple to specification. The lists all worked. The courses sang, the gatekeepers kept the gates, the treasurers counted the dedicated things. The system his father left him ran.

What he lost sight of was the other thing his father asked for. Not in a moment, and not by any decision he would have recognised as one. It went in the ordinary busyness of running what he had inherited.

Wisdom on its own is not enough. Knowing what to do without the heart to do it will leave you sitting on a bed in tears.

A successor always has to arise. Every leader dies, and someone inherits what he built. Some of them will inherit enormous organisations without any preparation at all, and that is not their fault, and none of this is a rebuke of them.

It is a question for the one handing over.

The temple David planned stood about four hundred years. The kingdom lasted one generation. Rehoboam was asked to lighten the load his father had put on the people, and he refused, and the ten tribes walked away. The system needed a hardness to run, and the hardness cost them the kingdom.

Jesus left no building. No blueprint, no roster, no gold weighed out for the lampstands, no endowment. Eleven men, most of them unqualified for anything much, and a promise. When they accused him at his trial they said he had threatened to build a temple not made with hands — which is what he did.

The plans were not the inheritance. They were never the inheritance.

Selah

Not long after I handed the church to a younger man, my heart gave out.

What I gave him was a church I had deliberately kept clear. No building project, no debt, no calendar already full when he arrived. It does not look like much on paper. David's looked like a great deal.

What I hoped he would have was time to hear from God — and a whole heart, and a willing mind, and the years to learn what those cost.
