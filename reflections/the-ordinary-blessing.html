Reflection

The Ordinary Blessing

Psalm 128
You shall eat the fruit of the labour of your hands; you shall be blessed, and it shall be well with you. Psalm 128 · 2

Psalm 127 left us with a question: whose hands are doing the building? The answer it gave was not abstract. It was bodily — the sleep of the beloved, the child received as nachalah, the gate where the family’s name is upheld. The theology landed in a kitchen.

Psalm 128 stays in that kitchen. And it does something that is easy to undervalue precisely because it is so unhurried and domestic: it shows us what the life built on the fear of the LORD actually looks like from the inside, on an ordinary day, when nothing dramatic is happening.

There is no crisis in this psalm. No enemy, no flood, no contempt to endure, no depth to cry from. There is a table. There is a vine. There are children. There is bread earned by honest labour and eaten without anxiety. The psalm does not apologise for the smallness of this scene. It calls it blessed.

This is, in its quiet way, one of the most countercultural moves in the entire collection. The pilgrim who has walked from Meshech to Jerusalem, who has been kept and tested and nearly swallowed by the flood, who has planted seeds in tears — arrives not at a throne room or a vision of cosmic glory, but at a family meal. And the psalm says: this. This is what you were walking toward.

· · ·

The psalm opens with ashrei — blessed, happy, to be envied. It is the same word that opens the entire Psalter in Psalm 1, and the same word that opened the Beatitudes from Jesus’s lips on the mountain. Each time it appears in Scripture it is worth pausing to ask: what does the canon mean by blessed? What kind of life is being pointed to?

Here the answer is immediately covenantal. The blessed person is the one who fears the LORD and walks in his ways. Fear of the LORD — yirat Adonai — is not terror. It is the orientation of a life rightly ordered toward God, the posture that recognises who is the builder and who is the one being built. It is the interior disposition of the person who received the nachalah of Psalm 127 with open hands rather than clenched fists. And walking in his ways is the outward expression of that interior reality — not a programme of religious performance but the natural gait of someone who knows where they are going and whose road it is.

The blessing that follows is notably concrete. You shall eat the fruit of the labour of your hands. This is not spiritual language. It is agricultural, economic, bodily. The blessed person works and the work produces something. They sit down to eat what their hands have made. There is a directness here — a connection between effort and fruit — that is itself a sign of things being as they should be. When the created order is functioning as God designed it, labour produces food and food is received as gift. The anxiety that drives the early rising and late resting of Psalm 127 has been replaced by something quieter: honest work, honest rest, honest eating.

Then the image that is perhaps the most quietly beautiful in all fifteen psalms: your wife will be like a fruitful vine within your house; your children will be like olive shoots around your table.

The vine and the olive are not decorative. They are the two most economically significant plants in the ancient Near Eastern world. The vine produces wine — the symbol of joy, of covenant celebration, of the abundance that God desires for his people. The olive produces oil — for food, for lamp-light, for anointing, for healing. A fruitful vine within the house is not merely pleasant. It is the sign of genuine flourishing, of life producing life, of abundance close at hand rather than distant and theoretical.

And the olive shoots around the table — shethilei zeitim — are young trees, not yet mature, but already present, already growing, already oriented toward light. The word shethilim carries the sense of something newly planted but taking root. These are not children as achievement or children as legacy project. They are children as the living evidence that the household is fruitful, that life is being passed on, that the table is a place where the next generation is already gathering.

The psalm then widens its frame in the closing verses. What began as an interior domestic scene — one household, one table, one vine — opens outward to Zion, to Jerusalem, to Israel. The blessing that rests on the God-fearing household is not sealed inside four walls. It participates in the blessing of the whole city, the whole people. May you see the prosperity of Jerusalem all the days of your life. The family at the table and the city on the hill are not separate realities. The shalom of one is bound up with the shalom of the other.

And then the most tender verse in the collection: may you see your children’s children. To live long enough to know a grandchild is presented here not as a private blessing but as a covenant sign — the promise moving forward, the nachalah passing through another generation, the olive shoots of today becoming the trees under which tomorrow’s children sit. This is what the fear of the LORD produces, given time and grace: a table that keeps being set, a blessing that keeps being passed.

The psalm closes, as Psalm 125 closed, with shalom upon Israel. But notice what has happened to shalom across the journey. In Psalm 120 it was longed for and refused — I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war. In Psalm 122 it was prayed — pray for the peace of Jerusalem. In Psalm 125 it was declared. And now in Psalm 128 it is simply the last word — quiet, unhurried, almost matter-of-fact. Peace be upon Israel. The word has been travelling with the pilgrim all the way from Meshech, and here, at a family table with olive shoots and a fruitful vine, it lands without fanfare. This is where shalom lives. Not in the dramatic moments only, but in the ordinary ones. At the table. With the children. In the house the LORD built.

Selah

I have sat at tables like the one this psalm describes. Not always — the road has had its Meshech seasons, its floods, its long plantings in tears. But I have sat at them. And what strikes me now, reading this psalm from the far side of a cardiac event, is how little I noticed them when I was there.

This is perhaps the psalm’s most precise pastoral challenge: the ordinary blessing is the one most easily overlooked. The fruitful vine within the house, the olive shoots around the table — these do not announce themselves. They do not arrive with the drama of an answered prayer or the relief of a narrow escape. They are simply there, day after day, and it is entirely possible to eat the fruit of your hands and never once register it as ashrei — as the blessed life, as the thing the whole ascent was for.

The cardiac event, among its other clarifications, clarified this. In the days of recovery, when the ordinary rhythms of a life were suspended — the meals, the conversations, the small routines that constitute a household — their absence made their weight visible. What I had been given, and had not quite known I was given, became suddenly legible. The table. The people around it. The food that someone’s hands had prepared. The fact of another ordinary day.

The grandchildren verse lands differently after a cardiac event. May you see your children’s children. I had not been certain I would. The nachalah that passes through generations — I had stood close to the place where my thread in it might have ended. And it did not. In the weeks of recovery, our eighth grandchild was born — in the UK, while I was still recovering in Australia. I did not hold her. But I was alive to know she had arrived, alive to receive the news, alive to be her grandfather. Her name was Rebekah — from the Hebrew Rivkah, meaning held fast, secured. I did not know then how precisely that name would sit inside this psalm. The psalm does not say hold your children’s children. It says see them. And seeing, in the Hebrew sense, is not a lesser thing — it is the full weight of witness, the confirmation that the line continues, that the nachalah is moving forward through another generation. The cardiac event had not only been survived. It had been followed, almost immediately, by the most ordinary and extraordinary of blessings. May you see your children’s children. I saw. And the seeing was, in the precise sense the psalm intends, ashrei — the blessed life, the thing the whole ascent was for.

I think of the families I have watched across the years of this work — families separated by incarceration, trying to maintain across a barrier what this psalm takes for granted. The table with the absent parent. The olive shoots growing up without the one who should be there to watch them root. The grandchildren who may never know the grandparent. What the psalm describes as ordinary blessing is, for these families, the precise shape of what has been taken. And that makes it not sentimental but urgent. The ordinary blessing is not a small thing. It is the thing. Its absence is a specific kind of grief.

What the Onesimus Foundation has always understood — sometimes more instinctively than theologically — is that the work of maintaining family across incarceration is the work of recovering the ordinary blessing for those from whom it has been taken. Not the dramatic intervention. Not the transformative programme. The table. The visit. The child who gets to sit near the parent, even in a prison visiting room, and know they have not been forgotten. The olive shoot, still growing, still oriented toward light.

Sit with this:

What ordinary blessing have you been moving through without registering? And what would it mean to sit at the table today — whatever table is yours — and receive it as ashrei?

The vine does not need to be extraordinary to be fruitful. The olive shoots do not need to be tall to be growing. The blessing is already present. The psalm is simply asking whether you have noticed.

One of the fifteen Songs of Ascent — the whole journey, from Meshech to the temple courts, is Songs for the Road.
