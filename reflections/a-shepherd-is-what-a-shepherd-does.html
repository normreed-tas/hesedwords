Reflection

A Shepherd Is What a Shepherd Does

Ezekiel 34

• • •
Norm Reed

One

Read the fat sheep's offence slowly, because it is easy to miss.

They eat the good pasture. Then they tread down what is left. They drink the clear water. Then they foul the rest with their feet. And the lean sheep — the ones who came late, or were pushed, or never had the size to get to the front — eat what has been trampled and drink what has been muddied.

Then the sentence that gives the picture its shape: you push with flank and shoulder, and thrust at all the weak with your horns, until you have scattered them abroad.

Notice what the strong sheep are not doing. They are not attacking. There is no malice in the passage. They are eating. They are drinking. They are getting to the front, which is what a strong animal does in a field. The water is fouled by feet, not by mouths — by ordinary movement, by simply being large in a small space and not looking behind. Almost nothing in these verses would be recognised as sin by the sheep committing it.

And the lean sheep are thin.

That is the thing to sit with. They are not thin because they lack appetite. They are not thin because they will not eat. They are thin because someone else got there first and left them the ruined edge of the field. Their leanness is not a condition. It is a record. It is the visible print of what the strong ones did on their way through.

Now count the corrections in this chapter. The shepherds are indicted — at length, by name, for feeding themselves. The fat sheep are indicted — for the trampling and the shoving. And the lean sheep are corrected not once. Not one word. They are the object of every rescuing verb in the oracle and the subject of none of the accusing ones. Nobody in Ezekiel 34 tells the weak sheep to try harder.

Two

Which is strange, because in most churches they are the ones being corrected.

Not cruelly. That is the part worth being careful about. The lean sheep present as the problem. Their attendance is patchy. Their money is chaotic. Their marriage is loud. Their kids are feral. They turn up late, or hungover, or not at all for six months. All of that is legible. It reads as failure, and it reads that way immediately, and it does not take a hard-hearted man to see it.

The shoving is not legible at all. The strong sheep look like commitment. They look like the ones who are actually here. They look, quite often, like leadership. And what they do to the pasture happens behind them, in a part of the field the shepherd is not standing in.

So the correction goes where the evidence appears to be. The shepherd mistakes the trampled grass for a poor appetite, and speaks to the sheep who is thin.

But there is a second reason, and it is worse.

Correcting the lean sheep is free. It costs nothing. They have the least power to leave, the least standing to object, the least capacity to withdraw anything the church depends on. They cannot make a phone call that matters. Correcting them can even be done in a way that looks like courage.

Correcting the strong sheep costs the shepherd his pasture. They are where he eats.

And that is not a figure of speech. It is the charge Ezekiel lays at the front of the chapter, before a single sheep is mentioned. Woe to the shepherds of Israel who have been feeding themselves. You eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fatlings — but you do not feed the sheep. The wool and the fat and the meat do not come from the lean ones. There is nothing on them to take. A shepherd who lives off the flock lives off the strong end of it.

Which is why the two indictments belong in one oracle. The chapter opens with shepherds who eat, and turns, without changing subject, to sheep who trample and shove. The second is the price of the first. A shepherd who is feeding himself from the flock cannot afford to confront the ones he is feeding from — so he corrects downward, where correction is cheap, and the water stays muddy, and the thin sheep are told to try harder.

Selah

Years ago I sat in a room with the staff of a large city church, working out who we were. Vision, mission, values, and then a line to hold all three.

I put one on the table. A church where everyone is important.

And I watched it land, and in the same breath I understood what I had done.

Nobody argued. Nobody said it was untrue. They didn't need to. It simply sat there, and it was obvious to every person present that if we adopted it, it could be tested — and that the testing would not be done by anyone in that room.

We were a good church. Genuinely. We cared, we gave, we had a mission to the city and to the world. Most churches are like this. We embraced all people. We had simply lost sight of everyone.

We chose the other sentence. A church for all people. It was good, and it was true, and nobody had to be wrong.

I did not argue for mine either. I let it go.

I carried it to Hobart.

Three

Verse sixteen is where the chapter puts its hand on you.

I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak — and then, in the Hebrew, the fat and the strong I will destroy. The Greek translators softened it to guard, and a number of our English versions have quietly followed them. But the harder reading is almost certainly right, and not only because it is harder. If verse sixteen ends with God tenderly watching over the fat and the strong, then everything that follows — the trampling, the fouled water, the flank and the shoulder — arrives out of nowhere. If it ends where the Hebrew ends it, the rest of the chapter is simply that last clause unfolding.

Which means the binding and the breaking are one hand. Not two moods of God, one soft and one severe, held in some careful balance. One act. The shepherd who binds up the injured sheep is the same shepherd who deals with the sheep that injured it, and he is not doing two things.

There is no version of care that leaves the cause standing. A shepherd who dresses the same wound every week and never goes near the horns that made it is not caring for the flock. He is maintaining the injury. He may be doing it tenderly. He may be doing it for years.

And the chapter has already told us why he does it. Not because he is hard. Because he is eating.

So the diagnostic is not am I a good shepherd — no one has ever answered that question against himself. The diagnostic is smaller and it is answerable:

Whose correction costs me something?

If the people I correct are the ones with least power to leave, and the people I do not correct are the ones I cannot afford to lose, then I already know what I am. Whatever else is happening, it is not Ezekiel 34. It is the shepherds of verse two, feeding themselves, and calling it a ministry of the word.

Four

Peter had the title. He never lost it. What he lost were the verbs.

And when Jesus restores him on the beach, he does not give him the title back. He does not tell him he is forgiven, or reinstated, or still the rock. He gives him three verbs and nothing else.

Feed my lambs. Tend my sheep. Feed my sheep.

Three times, because there were three denials. But also, perhaps, because a shepherd is what a shepherd does — and a man who has just discovered that his title survived his failure needs to be handed something that will not.

They are Ezekiel's verbs. They are the ones the shepherds of verse two never did and the ones God says he will do himself: seek, bring back, bind up, strengthen, feed. Not qualities. Not a character. Things that must be done today, to someone, or they have not been done.

And they are the reason a good church could not say a true sentence.

A church where everyone is important is not a value. It is a verb list wearing the clothes of a value. It claims nothing about what we believe. It only tells you what we can be caught not doing — and it hands the catching to the people at the far end of the field, who have no power to enforce it, and every right to ask.

That is the sentence I could not argue for in that room. It is the one I have not been able to put down since.

The good grass was always enough. It was never a scarcity problem.
