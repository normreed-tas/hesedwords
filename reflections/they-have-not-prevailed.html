Psalm 129

Norm Reed

They Have Not Prevailed

A Song of Ascents

Greatly have they afflicted me from my youth — let Israel now say — greatly have they afflicted me from my youth, yet they have not prevailed against me. Psalm 129 · 1–2

The domestic warmth of Psalm 128 has not prepared us for this. We move from the fruitful vine and the olive shoots around the table to plows driven across a human back, furrows cut long and deep into flesh. The collection does not allow us to stay in the kitchen. The road reasserts itself.

And yet the psalm does not open in despair. It opens in testimony — and testimony of a particular kind. Greatly have they afflicted me from my youth — let Israel say. The communal summons is the first thing. Before the affliction is described, the community is called to say it together. What has been endured is not a private wound to be nursed in solitude. It is a shared history to be spoken aloud, owned corporately, narrated in company.

This is the same instinct we encountered in Psalm 124 — let Israel say — the liturgical summons to communal testimony. There the near-catastrophe was the flood barely survived. Here it is something older and longer: the affliction that has been present since youth, the suffering that is not an incident but a pattern, not a crisis but a history. Israel has been afflicted from its beginning. And Israel is still here to say so.

That still-here-ness is the psalm’s entire argument.

· · ·

The psalm’s structure is deceptively simple. Two movements: testimony of survival (verses 1–4), then a prayer for judgment against the enemy (verses 5–8). But the weight falls almost entirely on the first movement, and within that movement on a single phrase that carries the whole theological load: yet they have not prevailed.

Lo yakhelu li. They have not been able against me. The affliction is not denied — it is named in its full violence. The plowers plowed upon my back. The image is visceral and precise. The back of a human being treated as a field, the furrows cut long and deep. This is not metaphor softening reality. It is metaphor intensifying it — giving the suffering an image strong enough to hold the full weight of what corporate affliction across generations actually feels like in the body.

But then — yet. The hinge word. The affliction is real and the affliction has not won. Both things simultaneously true. The psalm does not resolve the tension between them. It simply holds them in the same breath and insists that the second is as real as the first.

The theological ground of that insistence is verse 4: the LORD is righteous; he has cut the cords of the wicked. The word for righteous here is tsaddiq — not righteousness as moral conformity but righteousness as the fidelity of the one who keeps covenant, who stands by those in right relationship with him. The LORD is tsaddiq — and because he is, the cords that bound the afflicted have been cut. Not removed before the suffering happened. Cut after. The furrows were plowed. And then the cords were cut. The sequence matters. The psalm is not promising exemption from suffering. It is declaring that suffering is not the final word.

The prayer for judgment in verses 5–8 has troubled careful readers across the centuries, as these prayers for judgment in the Psalms always do. But notice its register. This is not a cry for personal revenge. It is a prayer for the failure of those who hate Zion — that their efforts would come to nothing, that they would be like grass on a rooftop, springing up in thin soil with no depth of root, withering before it can be harvested, so insubstantial that no one passing by would think to offer the harvest blessing. The blessing of the LORD be upon you — the greeting of solidarity between harvesters — is conspicuously absent from this grass. It produces nothing worth blessing.

The image of rootless rooftop grass beside the deep-rooted vine and olive of Psalm 128 is not accidental. The collection is making a sustained argument about what flourishes and what does not. The household built on fear of the LORD — with its vine and its olive shoots and its table — has roots. The affliction of the wicked is rootless, shallow, unable to sustain itself. It plows and binds and oppresses, but it does not endure. The cords get cut. The furrows heal. The back that was plowed is still here, still speaking, still summoning Israel to say what was survived.

There is one more thread worth following. The communal summons — let Israel say — connects Psalm 129 backward to Psalm 124 and forward to something larger. In Psalm 124 the community was called to narrate the near-miss, the flood barely survived. Here the community is called to narrate the long history — the affliction from youth, the pattern across generations. Together these two communal summons build a liturgical practice: the regular, corporate, spoken acknowledgment of what was endured and what did not prevail. This is not wallowing. It is the opposite of wallowing. It is the discipline of honest memory in the service of tenacious hope.

Selah

There is a particular kind of suffering that does not arrive as a single crisis but as a long pattern — the accumulated weight of years in an environment where the values you live by are consistently met with opposition, where the furrows are plowed slowly and the damage is not dramatic enough to name until you look back and see how deep they went.

I have known that kind of suffering in the work. Not the acute crisis — those have their own clarity — but the chronic wearing down of institutions that do not change, systems that grind on, the same families cycling through the same courts and prisons across generations, the same children bearing the same consequences their parents bore. The furrows plowed long.

The plowers are not always malicious — but institutional indifference is not passive. It is relentless. It cuts the same furrows into the same families across generations, and what Vanessa Goodwin’s research maps with careful precision is what Psalm 129 names in a single image: furrows made long.1 The system does not set out to destroy families. It simply operates by a logic that was never designed with these families in mind, and the result, repeated across decades, is the same as deliberate harm. It requires the intervention of the tsaddiq — the covenant-keeping God who cuts cords — to name what is happening for what it is, and to insist that it does not get the last word.

What Psalm 129 offered me — and offers still — is not a strategy for changing the systems. It is something prior to strategy: the insistence on narrating survival. Greatly have they afflicted me from my youth — let Israel say. The communal summons before the theology. The speaking-aloud before the analysis. There is something the community needs to do before it can think clearly about what to do next, and that something is to say together what has been endured and then say the second thing: yet they have not prevailed.

The families I work alongside know this instinctively. The ones who have survived the system’s logic longest — who have kept their children connected to the incarcerated parent across years of bureaucratic obstruction, who have driven hours to prisons in remote locations, who have filled in the same forms and navigated the same processes year after year — these families carry in their bodies the knowledge that Psalm 129 is trying to articulate. They have been plowed. And they are still here. And the still-here-ness is not nothing. It is, in the psalm’s terms, testimony. It is the cord the LORD has cut — not the suffering prevented, but the final defeat refused.

After the cardiac event, this psalm sat differently in my chest than it had before. The furrows plowed upon a back — I had a body that now knew something about that image it had not known before. Not the same thing as corporate historical suffering, not the same thing as the slow grinding of systemic injustice. But the body that has been through something knows how to read this psalm in a way the untested body cannot quite manage. The back that carries the mark of what happened to it and is still upright — still walking, still summoning others to say what was survived — that is the posture Psalm 129 is describing.

Yet they have not prevailed. That is not triumphalism. It is the most honest thing available to the person who has come through something they might not have survived. Not: it was not that bad. Not: I am stronger than I knew. But simply: I am still here. The cords were cut. The furrows are real and the furrows did not win.

Sit with this:

What is the long affliction — the pattern rather than the crisis — that you have been carrying? And have you said it aloud, in company, as testimony rather than complaint?

The psalm does not ask you to minimise what was plowed into you. It asks you to add the second sentence. Greatly have they afflicted me. And then: yet they have not prevailed. Both sentences. In that order. Said together, in whatever company the road has given you.

The cords have been cut. You are still here to say so.

1 Dr Vanessa Goodwin’s post-doctoral research traced patterns of justice involvement across multiple generations of Tasmanian families, providing empirical evidence for what many working in this field observe intuitively: that contact with the justice system is not primarily an individual phenomenon but a family systems phenomenon, with consequences that compound across generations. Dr Goodwin later served as Tasmania’s Attorney-General and Minister for Corrections. Her research has informed the theological and practical framework of the Onesimus Foundation from its earliest days.

Psalm 129 belongs to the fifteen Songs of Ascent, walked as one road in Songs for the Road.
