The Last Word of the Road

A Song of Ascents

Come, bless the LORD, all you servants of the LORD, who stand by night in the house of the LORD! Lift up your hands to the holy place and bless the LORD! Psalm 134:1–2

The road is over.

Fifteen psalms. One journey. From Meshech to the temple courts, from exile to arrival, from the deceitful tongues of people who chose war to the lifted hands of people who have chosen blessing. The ascent is complete. And the final psalm — the last word of the collection — is three verses long and contains almost nothing except a command, a posture, and a blessing returned.

It is worth pausing before we enter the text to feel what has been travelled. The pilgrim who sang woe to me, that I sojourn in Meshech is now standing in the house of the LORD. The feet that set out from the far country are standing where they were always meant to stand. The shalom that was longed for and refused in Psalm 120, prayed in Psalm 122, declared in Psalm 125, received quietly at the table in Psalm 128 — it is here now, in the place where the LORD has commanded his blessing, where the menuha of God himself has settled, where the yachad of the community has gathered and the oil has run.

And the psalm that marks this arrival does not linger. It does not describe the temple or narrate the moment of entering or offer a theology of what has been accomplished. It simply turns — immediately, almost urgently — to what the arrival calls for. Come, bless the LORD.

The ascent ends not in contemplation but in blessing. Not in the pilgrim receiving, but in the pilgrim giving. The last gesture of the journey is the lifted hand.

· · ·

The psalm addresses the servants of the LORD who stand in the house by night. These are the Levitical priests and temple singers — the ones whose vocation was to maintain the worship of the LORD through the dark hours, when the pilgrims had gone to their rest and the temple courts were quiet. The night watch of the sanctuary. The ones who kept the flame burning when no one else was watching.

This is the context into which the psalm speaks its opening word: come. Not a gentle invitation. The Hebrew hinneh — behold, look, here now — carries urgency. The servants who stand through the night are being summoned to attention, called to the central act of their vocation. Whatever else the night watch involves, this is its heart: bless the LORD.

The word bless here is barak — the same word that has been present throughout the collection, the word that carries the weight of covenant favour flowing in both directions between God and his people. In the Psalms of Ascent barak has appeared as the blessing the LORD commands at Zion, the blessing prayed for Jerusalem, the blessing declared over Israel. Now, at the collection’s end, barak flows upward — from the servants to the LORD. The people bless God.

This is theologically surprising and worth sitting with. Can the creature bless the Creator? What does it mean for a human being to bless the one who needs nothing, lacks nothing, is diminished by nothing? The tradition has wrestled with this. The most honest answer is that human blessing of God is the acknowledgment of who he is — the overflow of a heart that has received so much that it must give something back, even knowing the giving adds nothing to the one who receives it. Barak directed toward God is the creature’s response to the Creator’s prior generosity. It is praise as gift, offered freely, because the one who has been kept and carried and held through the whole journey can do nothing else at journey’s end but lift their hands and say: you are worthy of this. All of it was yours. I give it back.

The posture is specific: lift up your hands to the holy place. The raised hands of ancient worship — open, empty, upward — are the physical expression of barak. Not clenched fists grasping what has been accumulated. Not folded hands in private devotion. Open hands, raised, toward the place where the LORD dwells. The gesture that says: I have nothing to offer except this — the open hands that received everything, returned now in the direction from which everything came.

And then — in the psalm’s final verse, in the collection’s final verse — the blessing turns. May the LORD bless you from Zion, he who made heaven and earth. The servants blessed the LORD. The LORD blesses the servants. Barak flows upward and then flows back down. The blessing that the pilgrim offered returns to the pilgrim — but now it comes from Zion, from the place the LORD has chosen, from the menuha he declared. And it comes from the one who made heaven and earth — the title that has run through the collection since Psalm 121, since my help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth. The keeper of the pilgrim on the road is the same one who blesses the pilgrim at the journey’s end. The road and the arrival have the same author.

The collection closes in this exchange of blessing — creature to Creator, Creator to creature — and the exchange itself is the theological summary of everything the fifteen psalms have been saying. The pilgrim did not make the journey alone. The journey was kept, guarded, held, sustained by the one who made heaven and earth. And the right response to that — the only adequate response — is the lifted hand, the open palm, the barak offered back. Not because it adds anything. Because it is true.

The Christological thread that has run beneath the whole collection surfaces here with quiet power. Luke 24 records that on the day of the resurrection, Jesus led his disciples out to Bethany — and lifted his hands and blessed them. And while he blessed them, he parted from them and was carried up into heaven. The last thing the disciples saw was the lifted hands. The last gesture of the incarnate Son toward his people was barak — the blessing flowing downward from the raised hands of the one who had walked every step of the road with them, who had sung these psalms on the ascent, who had been kept and carried and held through the depths and the darkness and the cross and the resurrection morning.

Jesus enacted Psalm 134 at his ascension. The final psalm of the Psalms of Ascent became, at the moment of his departure, the last gesture of his earthly presence. The servants standing in the house of the LORD by night — the disciples, bewildered and bereft and about to receive what they could not yet imagine — received the blessing of the lifted hands. And then he was gone. And they returned to Jerusalem with great joy.

The blessing does not require the visible presence of the one who gives it. It has been commanded. It is real. The hands are lifted even now.

Selah

I did not come to these psalms fresh. They were already part of the regular rhythm of my reading — the daily movement through the Psalter that has shaped my mornings. But in the weeks of recovery, moving through them in the ordinary course of that reading, I stumbled into Psalm 120 and stopped. Not because it was unfamiliar. Because it was, suddenly, precisely familiar in a way it had not been before. Meshech. The deceitful tongues. The reaching for shalom and the meeting of war. The gur — the resident alien, the one who does not quite belong in the environment they are living in. I had been there. I was still, in some ways, there. And the psalm did not so much teach me something new as give voice to something I had been living without adequate words for. That is what the best texts do. They do not inform us of what we did not know. They name what we already knew but could not say.

What followed was not a study. It was a journey. The psalms opened one into another the way a road opens — each bend revealing the next stretch, each arrival becoming the departure point for what comes next. The keeping God of Psalm 121 who does not slumber. The threshold of Psalm 122 where the feet discover they are standing somewhere they had been walking toward without knowing it. The eyes fixed on the hand of the master in Psalm 123. The flood survived and narrated in Psalm 124. The encircling hills of Psalm 125. The seeds planted in tears and the harvest not yet visible in Psalm 126. And then the long second half of the journey — the house built on the right foundation, the table with the olive shoots, the furrows plowed and the cords cut, the depths and the watchman, the weaned child, the menuha of God in the prison visiting room, the yachad of the community that chose, at cost, to stay in the same room.

I have arrived. Not at the end of the work — the work continues, the road continues, the families who need the nachalah of their relationships restored are still there, the system is still grinding, the children are still carrying what was never theirs to carry. The ascent does not dissolve the world that was left behind in Meshech. It changes the one who walks it.

That is what I have discovered in these fifteen psalms. The journey does not fix the circumstances. It forms the pilgrim. The person who arrives at Psalm 134 with lifted hands is not the same person who cried woe to me in Psalm 120. Not because the suffering has been resolved or the exile has ended or the system has changed. But because something has been learned on the road that could not have been learned any other way. The keeping is real. The menuha is real. The yachad is real. The barak that flows between creature and Creator — upward in lifted hands, downward from Zion — is real. And the one who made heaven and earth, who kept the foot from slipping on every mile of this road, is the same one who stands at journey’s end with hands lifted in blessing.

The cardiac event was my Meshech. Not only my Meshech — the work had been Meshech in its own way for years, the accumulated weariness of a system that does not change as fast as the families need it to. But the cardiac event was where the psalm found me. Where I discovered that I was a gur — a resident alien — in my own assumptions about my own strength and the durability of my own effort. And where the road began.

I did not know, when I started walking, that I would arrive here. At the lifted hands. At the blessing that flows both ways. At the quiet, settled knowledge that the one who made heaven and earth has been keeping this journey from the beginning — not just my journey, but the journey of every family that has sat in a prison visiting room and refused to let the separation be the last word. Every child who has crossed the threshold of a correctional facility to be with a parent. Every correctional officer who has sat in a church pew next to the person they once supervised. Every father who has sent his son down the shop for a pack of playing cards.

May the LORD bless you from Zion, he who made heaven and earth.

That blessing is not mine to give. I am not the source of it. I am, at best, one of the servants standing in the house by night — keeping the flame burning in the hours when the pilgrims are resting, watching for the morning, lifting whatever I have to offer in the direction from which everything came. The night is still dark. The cells are still full. The children are still carrying what was never theirs to carry. The flame is kept not because the darkness has lifted but because the morning is coming and someone has to be at the wall when it does.

But the blessing is real. It has been commanded. It flows from the one who chose Zion as his resting place, who desired it, who dwells there — in the visiting room and the church pew and the kitchen table and the game of Concentration and the birth of an eighth grandchild in the UK while her grandfather recovered in Australia. He made heaven and earth. He has been keeping this road. He is keeping it still.

The road has been good. The keeper has been faithful. The city was closer than it seemed from Meshech, and the journey was worth every step.

Shalom upon Israel.

Psalm 134 closes the fifteen Songs of Ascent. The whole road that leads here — beginning in the far country of Psalm 120 — is walked in Songs for the Road.
