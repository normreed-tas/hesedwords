Spurgeon on the Song of Songs

Charles Spurgeon returned to the Song of Songs more often, and more tenderly, than to almost any other book. He read it as most of the Church once did — as a love-song between Christ and his people — and across some sixty sermons he traced that love through every season the soul knows: nearness and absence, longing and rest, the dark night and the breaking day. What follows is the complete collection, gathered by chapter. Each may be read on its own; together they form one of the most sustained meditations on the love of Christ in the English pulpit. All are freely available to download.

Better Than Wine

Download PDF →

Sermon 2459

Spurgeon takes a single clause and finds the whole gospel folded inside it — the love of Christ as something costlier, more exhilarating, and more lasting than any joy the world can pour. He lingers over why it is better: better in its source, its effect, and its permanence, a draught that gladdens without ever cloying. A warm, expansive opening to the collection, and a fair taste of how much he can draw from a few words.

1:4

The Memory of Christ’s Love

Download PDF →

Sermon 2294

Preached when Spurgeon was worn down and unwell, and he does not hide it — the sermon opens almost as a confession of weakness. What follows is the more moving for it: a man with little strength left choosing to do the one thing still open to him, which is to remember a love he can no longer match in vigour. For anyone reading in a season of diminishment — illness, age, tiredness of soul — this one knows the territory from the inside.

1:4

A Refreshing Canticle

Download PDF →

Sermon 2794

Draw me, we will run after thee. Spurgeon notices how one soul’s longing for Christ becomes contagious — the bride is drawn, and others find themselves running too. It is a sermon about the social pull of grace: that desire for Christ, when it is real, rarely stays private but kindles the same desire in those nearby. Refreshing is the right word for it; the whole thing moves at the pace of glad pursuit.

1:4

Rejoicing and Remembering

Download PDF →

Sermon 2461

On the same verse, Spurgeon holds two things together that easily come apart — present joy and the memory that feeds it. The gladness he commends is not a mood that floats free but one rooted in what Christ has already done, recalled and savoured until it warms the heart again. A steadying sermon on the discipline of remembering well.

1:6

Self-Humbling and Self-Searching

Download PDF →

Sermon 990

Look not upon me, because I am black. The bride’s frank confession of her own unworthiness becomes, in Spurgeon’s hands, an invitation to honest self-examination that does not tip into despair. He holds the two together with great care: a clear sight of one’s own failure, and an equally clear sight of the love that does not depend on one’s worthiness. Searching, but never crushing.

1:6

The Unkept Vineyard; or, Personal Work Neglected

Download PDF →

Sermon 1936

A practitioner’s sermon, and an uncomfortable one. They made me keeper of the vineyards; but mine own vineyard have I not kept. Spurgeon turns the verse on those who tend everyone’s ground but their own — the minister, the carer, the worker so spent on others that the inner life goes to seed. He is not gentle about it, but he is not crushing either; the aim is recall, not reproach. Read it if the work has been eating the soul that does it.

1:7

The Church’s Love to Her Loving Lord

Download PDF →

Sermon 636

Tell me, O thou whom my soul loveth, where thou feedest. One of the better-known of these — the soul, restless without Christ, asking plainly where he may be found. Spurgeon makes the question itself a mark of love: the heart that cannot be content with anything less than his actual presence. A clear, ardent treatment of seeking.

1:7

Love to Jesus

Sermon 338

An early sermon, plain and direct, on the same longing to find the One the soul loves. There is little ornament here — the young Spurgeon simply pressing the question of whether we love Christ, and what it looks like when we do. Its plainness is part of its strength.

1:7–8

The Good Shepherdess

Sermon 1115

Go thy way forth by the footsteps of the flock. For the soul that has lost sight of Christ, Spurgeon offers a homely instruction: follow the tracks the faithful have already worn — the means of grace, the company of God’s people, the paths others have walked to find him. Guidance for the disoriented, gently given.

1:12

Fragrant Graces

Download PDF →

Sermon 3480

While the king sitteth at his table, my spikenard sendeth forth the smell thereof. Spurgeon reflects on the graces that rise like perfume in Christ’s presence — how nearness to the King draws out of his people a sweetness they did not know they had. A short, fragrant meditation on what communion produces in us almost without our noticing.

1:13

A Bundle of Myrrh

Download PDF →

Sermon 558

One of the most loved of all these sermons, and a good place to begin. Spurgeon takes the bride’s image — a sachet of myrrh worn against the heart through the night — and makes it the whole posture of faith: Christ kept close, carried in the most private place, his bitterness turning sweet by sheer nearness. There is nothing abstract here. It is a sermon about where you keep Christ when no-one is watching, and what happens to grief when he is kept there.

Sermon 2472

I am the rose of Sharon. Christ names himself by the loveliest things the land could offer, and Spurgeon will not let the superlative pass quietly — he piles excellence on excellence, delighting that there is no comparison Christ does not exceed. An exuberant sermon, almost extravagant, on the sheer surpassing beauty of the One the Song is about.

Sermon 784

Perhaps the most loved of all these, and rightly. Spurgeon holds the two emblems of Christ’s beauty up to the light in turn — the rose for its glory, the lily for its purity — and finds in each a different facet of the same surpassing loveliness. Tender and unhurried, it is a sermon to read slowly, the way one would look long at something beautiful.

The Lily Among Thorns

Sermon 1525

As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters. Spurgeon draws out the church’s distinctness — the lily known at once from what surrounds it, even when it grows among cruel and piercing thorns. A word of recognition for believers in hard or graceless settings: Christ knows his own wherever they are found, and is not deceived by the soil they grew in.

As the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my beloved among the sons. Christ singled out from the common ranks of the forest — shade for the weary, fruit for the hungry. Spurgeon dwells on both the shelter and the sweetness, the rest and the nourishment, that the soul finds in sitting down beneath him. An earlier treatment of a verse he clearly loved.

Sermon 3249

The same verse, returned to years later, and now the emphasis falls on the sitting down — the deliberate choosing of Christ’s shade, the rest and delight of staying there. I sat down under his shadow with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste. A sermon to be read slowly, in no hurry to get up.

2:7

The Roes and the Hinds

Sermon 1463

I charge you… that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please. Spurgeon takes the bride’s strange oath as a lesson in the holy carefulness of love — the wisdom of not disturbing the soul’s fellowship with Christ, of guarding the quiet hours of communion rather than crowding them out. A sermon about reverence, and about the things love refuses to rush.

Sermon 436

Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away… for, lo, the winter is past. Spurgeon reads the Song’s spring passage as the call of grace breaking over a soul — the cold gone, the flowers appearing, the season of life arriving. One of his more lyrical efforts, alert to the turn of the year and the turn it pictures in the heart.

Sermon 2480

The vines with the tender grape give a good smell. On the small, early beginnings of grace — fragile, easily bruised, not yet fruit but full of promise — and the care with which Christ watches over them. A gentle sermon for those whose faith feels slight and unfinished; Spurgeon would have them know that the tender grape is precious to him precisely as it is.

Sermon 374

My beloved is mine, and I am his. Spurgeon turns the verse over to show the mutual belonging at the heart of the whole Song — not only that we have Christ, but that Christ has us, each party glad in the other. An early, warm treatment of the exchange he will return to again and again.

2:16

Loved and Loving

Download PDF →

Sermon 1634

Sent to his church from France while Spurgeon recovered from illness, to remind them of Christ’s love during his absence — and the more poignant for the circumstance. On the same verse of mutual belonging, written by a man parted from the people he loved, pointing them past himself to the Love that never leaves. There is a tenderness here that the situation sharpens.

2:16

My Beloved Is Mine

Download PDF →

Sermon 2442

Spurgeon calls this versicle the very soul and heart of the Song, and preaches it as such — possession answered by possession, the believer’s mine met by Christ’s own. He handles it as the central jewel of the book, the plainest statement of a belonging that runs both ways. If one sermon distils the whole, he thought it was this verse.

A Song Among the Lilies

Sermon 1190

He feedeth among the lilies. On communion enjoyed in the place of beauty — the soul feeding where Christ feeds, finding him among the pure and lovely things rather than the coarse. A sermon about the company Christ keeps and the table he spreads, and about wanting to be found where he is.

Sermon 3307

Behold, he cometh leaping upon the mountains. Spurgeon delights that nothing is too high for Christ’s love to cross — no barrier of sin, distance, or difficulty that he will not leap to reach his own. A buoyant sermon on the eagerness of Christ toward us, all the obstacles notwithstanding.

A Song of My Beloved

Sermon 3185

The same mountains and barriers, set to a slightly different key — Christ’s swift, barrier-crossing love toward his people, and the answering song it draws from them. A companion piece to Over the Mountains, worth having for the variation in emphasis.

2:17

The Believer’s Glad Prospects

Download PDF →

Sermon 3323

Until the day break, and the shadows flee away. Spurgeon turns the verse toward hope — the certainty of the coming morning, and the prospects that steady a believer who must wait through the dark for it. A forward-looking sermon, set on the dawn.

2:17

Darkness Before the Dawn

Download PDF →

Sermon 2477

Until the day break, and the shadows flee away. This is a sermon for the waiting stretch — the long night when Christ feels absent and the morning is only promised, not yet seen. Spurgeon refuses both easy comfort and despair: the shadows are real, but they are fleeing, and they flee precisely because the day is certain. A steadying word for anyone holding on in the dark on the strength of a dawn they cannot yet make out.

I held him, and would not let him go. On the seeking soul that finally lays hold of Christ and refuses to release him. Spurgeon honours the tenacity of love — the watchfulness that keeps looking through the night and is, in the end, answered. Persistence rewarded, and the reward held tightly.

3:4–5

The Real Presence, the Great Want of the Church

Download PDF →

Sermon 1035

A sermon with an edge to it. Spurgeon argues that the church’s deepest need is not better method, organisation, or eloquence, but the actual presence of Christ himself — and that everything else is hollow without it. Pointed and bracing, it reads as a corrective to any age, his or ours, tempted to substitute machinery for the living Lord.

Spurgeon reads the bridal procession of the Song as the King and his people borne together in glory — and the cross-as-enthronement note, the suffering that becomes coronation, sounds clearly through it. A richer, more ceremonial sermon than most here, alive to the splendour of the picture.

Sermon 1134

The midst thereof being paved with love, for the daughters of Jerusalem. Spurgeon fixes on a single phrase — the very interior of the King’s chariot laid with love — and makes it an image of grace woven into the fabric of everything Christ does for his people. A small detail, opened into a large comfort.

The bride prays for whatever winds will draw out her fragrance for Christ — even the cold north wind of affliction. Spurgeon traces a small, lovely shift across the verse — the garden the bride calls my becomes, in the next breath, his. On the glad exchange of belonging: what is ours given over to Christ, and in the giving made more truly a delight. A short sermon turning on a single change of pronoun.

I am come into my garden… I have eaten my honeycomb; I have drunk my wine. Christ comes down to take pleasure in his own — and Spurgeon makes much of the mutuality of the feast, that our communion is his joy as well as ours. A warm sermon on the surprising thought that Christ delights to be with us as much as we long to be with him.

Sermon 1943

On the same verse, Spurgeon presses the circle closed — love delighting in being loved in return, Christ glad in his people’s gladness. A sermon about reciprocity at its fullest, where the joy runs both directions and neither party is the mere recipient. Among the most purely happy in the collection.

5:2

Asleep and Yet Awake — a Riddle

Download PDF →

Sermon 1561

I sleep, but my heart waketh. Spurgeon takes the bride’s riddle as a portrait of the half-hearted believer — drowsy, slow to rise, and yet not wholly dead to Christ, the heart still stirring at his voice. He handles the divided soul honestly, neither excusing the slumber nor despairing of the heart that still hears the knock. A searching sermon about lukewarm love and the patience that knocks anyway.

Sermon 3013

The same knocking at the door, now weighted toward warning — the cost of keeping Christ waiting, the loss that follows when we are too comfortable to rise and let him in. Spurgeon balances the caution with encouragement, but the caution is real: love left unanswered has consequences the heart later feels.

Sermon 793

Spurgeon follows the whole sorry sequence — the bride’s delay, Christ’s withdrawal, her frantic seeking through the streets — and finds in it a strange grace: that the loss drives her to seek him more earnestly, until she holds him nearer and dearer than before. A sermon on how even our failures of love can be turned toward a deeper communion.

Sermon 3552

On the felt absence of Christ — the dark stretch every serious believer knows, when he seems to have gone and the soul cannot reach him. Spurgeon treats the desertion with great pastoral care, neither minimising the pain nor leaving the soul without hope of return.

I am sick of love. Spurgeon takes the bride’s words plainly — the soul made genuinely ill with longing for an absent Christ — and preaches a kind of holy ardour that may startle cooler temperaments. On Christ first and best in every comparison — Spurgeon ranges across his excellencies to show why he takes precedence over all, and why the soul that has seen him can be satisfied with no other. The bride describes her Beloved feature by feature, and Spurgeon follows her — the beauties of Christ catalogued like a garden in full bloom, each detail an occasion for praise. A sermon that simply lingers over loveliness, content to admire at length what it cannot exhaust.

Sermon 1001

Yea, he is altogether lovely. One of the great sermons of the collection. Spurgeon takes the bride’s summary verdict and makes it his theme — every excellence meeting in Christ, with nothing lacking and nothing out of proportion. He moves through facet after facet and arrives, each time, at the same conclusion: there is no part of him that is not lovely. A sermon to send the reader back to Christ himself.

Sermon 1446

On the same verse, Spurgeon presses past admiration toward decision — Christ not merely lovely in the abstract but chosen, settled upon, the heart’s deliberate best. A sermon about turning the verdict altogether lovely into the personal and therefore mine.

Sermon 1716

Thou that dwellest in the gardens… cause me to hear thy voice. Spurgeon takes Christ’s request before parting and makes it a sermon on prayer — that the voice of his people is the thing the Bridegroom longs to hear, and that our asking is a delight to him, not a burden. Come, My Beloved!

Download PDF →

Sermon 2360

The closing cry of the whole Song, and a fitting end to the collection. Make haste, my beloved. Spurgeon reads the Song’s last verse as the church’s longing for Christ’s return — the bride, having known his love through every chapter, now asking only that he come quickly. After sixty-odd sermons on presence, nearness, and communion, the book ends not in possession but in yearning, and Spurgeon lets that yearning stand as the truest note of love.
